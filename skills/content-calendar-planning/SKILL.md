# content-calendar-planning

## 01 — Purpose

This skill turns "I should post more" into a working publishing calendar: a schedule
of slots — platform, format, date, status — sized to the hours the creator actually
has, filled with a deliberate mix of themes and formats, and connected to a review
loop that improves the next cycle instead of restarting it. The artifact is one
document holding capacity math, pillar ratios, the weekly grid, repurposing
decisions, seasonal overlays, and a cumulative review log.

Content planning fails in a predictable way: an ambitious cadence is chosen first
and capacity discovered second, the schedule collapses within two to three weeks,
and the audience learns that announced schedules mean nothing. This skill reverses
that order. Capacity is measured before cadence is committed. Ideas earn slots;
slots are not invented to host ideas. Repurposing is structural — anchor assets are
chosen partly for how many platform-native cuts they yield. The review loop changes
one variable at a time so the calendar improves by evidence instead of by mood.

The craft is fit between three things: what the audience should come to expect (the
mix), what the creator can sustain (the cadence), and what the business needs (the
arcs — launches, seasonal windows, series). A professional calendar holds all three
for months. A generic one holds for nine days. The difference is rarely talent; it
is whether the plan was built against measured hours and reviewed on a fixed
cadence, or assembled from enthusiasm and renegotiated weekly.

This skill assumes a small operation — solo creator or two-to-three person team —
and optimizes for plans that survive contact with real weeks: bad weeks, client
weeks, illness, platform changes. It avoids tool lock-in: every artifact works
in a spreadsheet, a notes file, or a board tool. A calendar built this way is
also a decision record: six weeks later the grid shows what was published, what
was skipped, demoted, killed, and why.

What this skill does not do: it does not write the content, choose platforms or
positioning, or design the business behind the content. It owns the scheduling
layer: it takes strategy as input and produces a fillable, reviewable plan.

A calendar that follows this method functions as a personal operations manual for
content. The grid shows not just what to publish on Thursday, but what production
step each asset is in, which anchor it derives from, whether it cleared its quality
floor, which pillar it serves, and what lead time it requires. When something goes
wrong — a missed slot, a format that stops working, a week over capacity — the
calendar has the data to diagnose the failure without guesswork.

The method separates three concerns that amateurs merge: (1) **Capacity** — how
much time the creator actually has, measured in hours per asset per format and
summed against total batching hours. (2) **Strategy** — what the creator wants to
be known for, expressed as pillar ratios and series decisions, not content volume.
(3) **Scheduling** — when each asset reaches which audience, expressed as a grid of
slots with statuses, dependencies, and lead times. Merging any two of these
produces a plan that collapses in its own contradictions. A creator who sets
strategy without measuring capacity will abandon strategy for easier content within
two weeks. A creator who schedules without strategy will produce a full grid that
builds no recognition. The professional practitioner works in the layer between
these concerns: the calendar as a negotiation tool between what is wanted, what is
possible, and what will be reviewed.

Outcomes this skill owns: (1) A multi-week content calendar with per-slot surface,
format, pillar, working title, repurposing source, lead time, and status. (2) A
cadence commitment per surface with pillar ratios and at most two named series,
plus the repurposing map. (3) A recurring review practice — five questions per
week, one change per week, monthly keep/kill/iterate pass. (4) A platform strategy
summary per surface covering posting posture and audience expectations.

## 02 — When to Use / When Not to Use

Use this skill when building, resizing, repairing, or scheduling a recurring
publishing plan. Concrete triggers: (1) "Help me build a content calendar" for any
combination of surfaces over any horizon from one week to one quarter. (2) The
creator posts irregularly and wants a named cadence without burning out by week
three. (3) A launch or event needs a content arc: warm-up posts, launch-day
sequencing, and follow-through. (4) The existing calendar keeps slipping — missed
slots, apology posts, silent gaps — and the ask is to diagnose capacity, ratio, or
workflow. (5) One strong long-form asset exists and the ask is to derive a week of
platform-native content through systematic repurposing. (6) A routine needs a
review practice: what to measure, when to keep/kill/iterate, how to improve without
overcorrecting. (7) A named series needs a fixed slot, episode count, and exit
rule. (8) The creator is changing surfaces and needs to know what the arrangement
costs in hours before making commitments. (9) The creator wants to move from blank-
page publishing to a pipeline with a buffer.

Not to use — and what to do instead: (1) The ask is what to post, not when — use a
content-authoring skill. (2) Platform strategy or positioning — use
`social-media-strategy`; this skill takes the surface list as input. (3) Upstream
business model questions — use `marketing-strategy`; a calendar is downstream.
(4) Raw idea generation with no scheduling intent — use a creativity skill.
(5) A one-off single post with no recurring schedule — write and publish it.
(6) The calendar tool itself is broken — that is tooling support, not planning.
(7) Zero content or audience — start with audience research or positioning.

Boundary rule: if the conversation drifts from scheduling into writing or strategy,
finish the artifact first, then hand off explicitly.

## 03 — Inputs and Outputs

Inputs the user must supply: (1) **Surfaces** — publishing surfaces and owning
accounts. If unsure, fix current ones and flag expansion as a separate cycle.
(2) **Measured capacity** — batching hours per week and hours per asset per format
from at least one real draft session. If unmeasured, run the capacity audit
protocol or proceed with estimates at doubled margin, clearly labeled.
(3) **Audience and goal** — who the content is for and what action it should
produce. "Grow the audience" is not enough; push for a measurable action.
(4) **Existing assets** — current series, banked evergreen content, anchor material.
Ask which past assets overperformed and why. (5) **Date anchors** — launches,
holidays, conferences, blackout weeks inside the planning horizon.
(6) **Constraints** — tools, collaboration deadlines, post dates, time zones.

If the user cannot answer any of these, resolve it before proceeding. A calendar
built on assumed inputs fails in week two.

Outputs this skill returns: (1) **The calendar grid** — one row per slot for the
full horizon. Each slot has id, date, surface, format, pillar, working title,
source anchor (or "independent"), and status (empty → outlined → drafted →
scheduled → published → reviewed). (2) **The cadence commitment** — per-surface
frequency derived from capacity math with safety margin shown. (3) **The content
mix** — 3-5 pillars with monthly target ratios, format rotation notes, and at most
two named series with fixed slots, episode range, and end condition. (4) **The
repurposing map** — for each anchor: which cuts it yields, which slots they occupy,
spacing, angle rotation, skipped cuts with one-word reason. (5) **The batch plan**
— which hours produce which asset types in pipeline order, with the one-batch
buffer. (6) **The review loop** — weekly check and monthly keep/kill/iterate, with
the first review date on the calendar. (7) **A platform strategy summary** — per-
surface posture, audience expectation, and cadence strategy. Every output lands in
one template file — the single document of record.

## 04 — Workflow (14 steps)

**Step 1 — Scope the engagement.** Confirm surfaces, horizon (default 4 weeks),
and what "done" means — filled grid, launch arc, or rebuilt cadence. Source:
`calendar-fundamentals.md` planning horizons. Produce: a one-paragraph scope note.
Check: "Which surfaces are in scope, what horizon, and what makes the plan
finished?"

**Step 2 — Audit capacity with real numbers.** Collect batching hours per week and
hours per asset per format from a real draft session, not memory. If unmeasured,
run the two-week measurement protocol or proceed with estimates at doubled margin.
List all competing obligations including invisible ones. Source:
`calendar-fundamentals.md` capacity audit. Check: "Are these hours measured or
estimated? If estimated, are you comfortable with the wider margin?"

**Step 3 — Compute the sustainable cadence.** Apply cadence math from
`calendar-fundamentals.md` and frequency guide from `cadence-and-frequency.md`.
Divide batch hours across formats by fill priority: anchors first, cuts second,
community third, experiments last. Subtract a safety margin of at least one asset
or 20 percent, whichever is smaller. Show the arithmetic. Name what was cut.
Check: "This cadence costs X of Y weekly hours. Should we reduce a slot type?"

**Step 4 — Set quality floors.** Define the minimum bar each format must clear to
publish. An asset below its floor is demoted, swapped for evergreen, or cut
without apology. Source: quality floor table in `cadence-and-frequency.md`.
Check: "Assets below their floor do not ship. Agreed?"

**Step 5 — Design the pillar mix.** Define 3-5 pillars with monthly target ratios.
Set ratios from strategy — what the creator wants to be known for in six months.
Assign a default format per surface. Name at most two series; give each a fixed
weekday slot, episode count, and end condition. Source: `content-mix-design.md`
mix grid and series rules. Check: "Do these pillars say what you want to be known
for, or what is easiest to write today?"

**Step 6 — Overlay dates and seasonal events.** Place launches, holidays, events,
and blackout weeks. For each event, decide: use, reduce cadence, or ignore. Block
launch arc slots before ordinary fills. Source: checklists in
`seasonal-and-event-planning.md`. Check: "Which events matter to this audience,
and which do we deliberately ignore?"

**Step 7 — Audit existing assets for repurposing.** Review banked anchors, past
series, and overperformers as cut candidates. Decide per candidate: produce, skip
with reason, or defer. Source: cut list in `repurposing-frameworks.md`.
Check: "Which anchor in your backlog has the strongest claim or detail?"

**Step 8 — Assign anchors and cuts.** Choose each week's anchor. For each, record
which cuts it yields and assign them to slots with `source: anchor-id`. Apply
spacing: cuts follow anchor by 2-3 days, never consecutive on the same surface,
angles rotate. Record skipped cuts. Check: "Which anchors have material for 3-5
cuts, and which stay single-format?"

**Step 9 — Build the weekly grid.** One row per slot per surface per week. Every
slot needs date, surface, format, pillar, working title, source, and status
`empty`. Check realized mix against pillar ratios; rebalance from overrepresented
pillars without changing strategy targets. Run sequence rules: no heavy anchors on
consecutive days; proof follows framework within two weeks. Check: "Walk the grid
— does anything feel impossible on a bad week?"

**Step 10 — Define the batching workflow.** Map slots to production blocks.
Sequence: outline, draft and edit, asset prep, schedule. Establish the one-batch
buffer: next week's anchor is outlined before this week's publishes. Identify
approval needs. Source: `scheduling-workflows.md`. Check: "Which block is your
most reliable two hours, and which falls first under stress?"

**Step 11 — Load the first batch.** Advance week 1 slots to `outlined` with
working titles and core arguments. Schedule nothing below its floor. Confirm the
template is the one calendar of record. Check: "Week 1 outlined and confirmed?
Which slot are you most nervous about?"

**Step 12 — Install the weekly review.** Fix the ritual: shipped/slipped, what
moved (metric per asset), what surprised, cost check (hours vs budget), one change
for next week. Source: five questions in `performance-review-loop.md`. Place the
first review date on the calendar. Check: "Which day and how long?"

**Step 13 — Plan the weekly adjustment.** Apply exactly one change from each
review: cut a slot, swap a format, add a community slot, or move timing. If more
than one change surfaces, the strongest signal wins; the rest go to the monthly
pass. Write the change into the grid before the session ends. Check: "What is the
one change for next week?"

**Step 14 — Hand off and set the next cycle plan.** Deliver the filled template,
cadence commitment, repurposing map, and review calendar. Set the monthly
keep/kill/iterate date. Point to `calendar-case-studies.md` and
`examples/worked-content-calendar.md`. Check: "The grid is yours. When do we sit
for the first monthly review?"

Recovery note: if any checkpoint fails, return to the owning step. Steps 2-3 own
capacity, step 5 owns mix, step 9 owns grid layout. Do not adjust downstream
until upstream is resolved.

## 05 — Rules and Quality Bar (16 rules)

1. **Measure capacity before committing cadence.** An unmeasured schedule is a
wish that collapses in week two. No step proceeds without it. Without measured
numbers, the cadence arithmetic in step 3 produces a false sense of precision.

2. **Show the cadence arithmetic to the user.** "Post twice a week" is not a plan;
hours per asset times assets against measured hours is. The math makes the trade
visible and lets the user renegotiate the right variable — fewer formats, smaller
anchor size, or a lower publishing frequency.

3. **Keep a safety margin always.** A calendar at 100 percent capacity has no way
to survive a bad week except by sacrificing quality. One asset or 20 percent,
whichever is smaller. The margin is not a flex pool; it is an insurance policy
that is used only in crisis.

4. **Slots are promises; ideas are inventory.** An idea that cannot earn a slot
stays parked without guilt. The calendar is full when slots have statuses, not
when the list is long. (Check: `calendar-fundamentals.md` slot vs idea rules.)

5. **Design repurposing at slot assignment time, not publish day.** Cuts discovered
on publication day have no spacing plan and become feed spam. Include the
`source:` tag and spacing interval at the same moment the anchor is chosen.
(Check: `repurposing-frameworks.md` spacing rules.)

6. **Rewrite every cut for its surface.** Paste from the anchor reads as
advertising for the anchor. Each surface gets a first line written for its reading
mode: hook for short posts, takeaway for feeds, standalone post for threads.
Compression is the craft — a good cut is shorter than the original line.

7. **Cap named series at two; end them deliberately.** Three stalled series train
the audience to ignore announcements. A series without an end date decays into
gaps that teach the audience that numbered episodes carry no weight. (Check:
`content-mix-design.md` series rules.)

8. **Miss a slot silently; never publish filler or apology.** One filler costs more
trust than one missed slot because the audience can tell. An apology converts
a private slip into a public event that draws attention to the gap. (Check:
`cadence-and-frequency.md` miss handling.)

9. **Match the metric to the asset's job.** Judging reach posts by saves or essays
by impressions kills the assets that achieve the intended strategy. Each asset
type has a primary metric that aligns with its purpose in the mix. Every slot
must answer: does this move the primary metric? If unclear, restate the goal
before filling the slot; a slot that cannot answer even after restatement is a
vacancy, not an opportunity — leave it empty. (Check:
`performance-review-loop.md` primary metric table.)

10. **One change per week from reviews.** Two variables changed simultaneously
makes next week's results unreadable, and unreadable results restart the cycle
of opinion that the review exists to break. Park proposals for the monthly pass.

11. **Kill recurring patterns, never single assets; record the reason.** Do not
kill a format before testing it across three attempts in two conditions —
different pillar, surface, or angle. Three consecutive misses or a full month of
negative contribution: kill. Anything less is noise from a single
underperforming execution. A kill without a recorded reason gets re-litigated
forever at every review.

12. **One calendar of record.** Mirror trackers drift within a month. A calendar
not trusted at a glance stops being consulted. The template is the single source
of truth for scheduling decisions. (Check: `scheduling-workflows.md` tool-agnostic
rules.)

13. **Respect lead times as hard constraints.** Collaborations run on someone
else's calendar. Paid content needs a cooling-off pass. Names and quotes require
review before scheduling. A lead time missed by one day often means a missed slot.
(Check: `seasonal-and-event-planning.md` lead-time table.)

14. **The grid must survive a bad week on paper.** Simulate: batch block halves
— which slots drop first? A plan that cannot answer is fiction with dates on it.
Run the simulation before committing to the grid, not after the first crisis.

15. **Plan experiments as explicit slots with a review date.** "I will try threads
sometime" neither happens nor dies. A slot with a review date converts curiosity
into evidence that feeds the monthly keep/kill/iterate pass. (Check:
`performance-review-loop.md` keep/kill/iterate pass.)

16. **Deliver one artifact.** Capacity, mix, grid, logs, and reviews in the same
file. The user finds it in week six and notes attach to the slot that produced
them. Separate files for each function drift apart within a cycle. (Check:
`templates/content-calendar-template.md`.)

## 06 — Platform Strategy Guidelines

This section adds surface-level depth beyond the cadence numbers in
`cadence-and-frequency.md`. Every surface has a consumption mode, production cost,
and posting posture that determines how cuts from the same anchor behave on it.

**Consumption modes.** Short-post platforms are goal-oriented: the reader decides
in under two seconds. The hook determines everything. Professional feeds are
work-mode: the same reader may scan or read deeply, so the takeaway-first structure
is the only reliable approach. Video surfaces require audio attention and cost more
but pay back in trust and retention. Newsletters are the anchor surface — opted-in
audience with deliberate reading mode.

**Posting posture in calendar terms.**
- **Newsletter.** Sets the week's core argument. One per week is the sustainable
solo cadence. Newsletter owns the primary slot priority in the batching block.
- **Short-post platform.** Highest benefit per hour. Thesis cut Tuesday, story cut
Thursday, independent observation Friday. One hook per post; if it cannot land in
two sentences, cut it.
- **Professional feed.** Best for shortened proof: a case number, architecture
note, worked example. Two to three per week maximum; more reads as noise.
- **Video (short or long).** Only if the creator has presence AND the topic
benefits from demonstration. Budget for tools, lighting, audio. Always plan a
text conversion for archive value.

**Platform audit before each cycle.** Each quarter, check which surfaces produced
engagement within the target cost-per-engagement band. A surface without measurable
audience action is a candidate for reduced cadence or removal.

**Anti-pattern.** Deciding posture from mood: "I will post daily because I feel
productive." Use measured data. `cadence-and-frequency.md` floors and ceilings
prevent this.

## 07 — Content Auditing Before Planning

Assess what already exists before building the calendar. This saves capacity for
material the creator already has.

**Back-catalog triage per asset:** (1) **Evergreen** — still helpful six months
later. Flag for recovery-week repurposing at zero writing cost. These are the
first assets to pull when a crisis hits or a recovery week arrives. (2) **Outdated
but has a strong number or framework** — rewrite the data point and resurface at
about half a new asset's cost. The framing ages; the evidence does not. (3) **Dead
links or expired posts** — delete from active library so the calendar does not
schedule dead ends. A link that resolves to a 404 weakens an otherwise strong post.
(4) **Underpublished but strong** — a piece that received less attention than its
quality deserved. Ideal for second publication on a different surface in a different
format. Build a standby list of 3-5 candidates tagged with surface and format.

**Identify the overperformer.** Which asset outperformed its recent average by
a meaningful margin? In which metric — replies, saves, shares, opens? Trace the
pattern: same format, pillar, surface combination, or angle? The overperformer
often seeds the repurposing map for the next cycle because the audience already
signaled interest in that specific format-pillar pairing.

**Old content repurposing cost.** Not every anchor needs to be written from
scratch. A piece from three months ago that still holds truth — does it yield
three fresh cuts at current platform norms? If yes, it counts as a full anchor
slot at zero writing cost. The only cost is rewriting the cuts for current surface
formats, which is roughly a third of a new anchor's production time.

**New-to-old ratio signal.** A healthy grid is about 70 percent new, 30 percent
repurposed from past cycles. Above 80 percent new: too much write-from-scratch
overhead; the creator will exhaust their idea catalog within two months. Below
50 percent new: the audience sees too much recycled material and engagement on
repurposed cuts decays faster than on fresh content.

**Pre-audit checklist.** (1) List top-5 most-referenced past pieces. (2) Mark each
as evergreen / needs-rewrite / dead. (3) Identify one past anchor yielding 3-5
fresh cuts. (4) Assign it to the repurposing map before building fresh content.

**Check question.** "Which past asset is strong enough to use as proof for a
different format today? That is your repurpose target."

## 08 — Crisis and Disruption Handling

Creators have weeks that explode: client emergencies, illness, travel, platform
outages. A calendar that cannot survive a bad week is fiction. The test is whether
the creator knows what to drop and in what order.

**Two crisis types.** Zero capacity: no production possible due to illness,
emergency, or travel without connectivity. Half capacity: heavy project deadline,
reduced energy, family obligations that cut available hours roughly in half. Both
share one rule: no filler, no apology, no makeup. The audience noticed less than
the creator fears and an apology converts a private slip into a public narrative.

**Zero-capacity protocol.** (1) Cancel all publication silently — no explanation,
no apology post. (2) If a named series episode would be missed, post exactly one
sentence: "The series continues next week." (3) On return, do not double up
episodes in the same week; doubling reads as rush and the second episode inherits
the first week's audience fatigue. (4) Re-enter at the next scheduled slot as if
nothing happened. The one-batch buffer is what makes this possible: returning to
an already-outlined next week rather than a blank page.

**Half-capacity protocol.** (1) Drop independent slot first — it belongs to the
lowest priority tier. (2) If still over capacity, drop the weakest-performing cut
identified from the previous week's review data. (3) The anchor always publishes;
if only one asset ships in a week, it is the anchor. (4) If half-capacity extends
into a second consecutive week, reduce the slot count before that week's batch
starts rather than trying to compress production into fewer hours.

**Pre-planned priority ordering.** Independent slots first, lowest-performing cut
per anchor second, experiments third, community slots fourth. Anchor never falls
below its cut count.

**Trigger condition.** If both protocols fire in a four-week window, the cadence is
wrong. Next cycle: cut one recurring slot, re-measure, rebuild from step 3.

**Crisis recovery checklist.** (1) Log the crisis type and dropped slots. (2)
Check if used twice in four weeks — flag for reduction. (3) On return, do not fill
missed slots. (4) Resume at the next anchor slot.

**Check question.** "What is the first slot you drop when a work item lands at 9
AM Monday?" If the user cannot answer, the grid needs explicit priority ordering.

## 09 — Performance Metrics and Tracking

This section governs how the creator knows whether the plan is working. Metrics
feed the weekly review and monthly keep/kill/iterate cycle.

**Primary metric per surface.** Each surface has a metric that measures its job:
- **Newsletter** — open rate and reply rate. Subscriber count lags by weeks.
- **Short-post platform** — engaged replies, saves, reshares. Impressions are
vanity.
- **Professional feed** — engagement-to-follower ratio.
- **Blog/long-form** — reading depth, scroll depth, click-through rate.

**Cost-per-engagement metric.** Divide actual hours by primary metric value. This
tells the creator which format earns the best engagement per invested hour. High
hours/engagement: expensive but possibly valuable for trust. Low hours/engagement:
earns its keep — push more volume.

**Tracking format.** A table in the review log: week's published slots, primary
metric per asset, actual hours per asset. Eight weeks of this produces a strategy
document written by evidence — which pillar earns the most engagement per hour,
which format has the widest variance, and which surface signals format fatigue.

**Trend tracking, not snapshot comparison.** Chart cost-per-engagement over three
months of data. A flat or improving trend confirms the decision to keep or increase
that format's slot count. A declining trend — even if absolute engagement numbers
look fine — signals audience fatigue with the format, pillar, or repetition. This
is the strongest signal to iterate before killing. A snapshot comparison of last
week to this week is noise; a three-month slope is evidence.

**The not-everything-table.** Community reply and Q&A slots serve presence, trust,
and community depth — not cost-per-engagement. They belong in the monthly
keep/kill/iterate pass on qualitative criteria: do they produce notable
conversations, open new relationships, or surface audience needs that inform
the anchor mix? Judge them by conversation quality, not by metrics.

**Review frequency.** Weekly: check metrics, produce one change. Monthly: check
pillar performance (ratio drift vs targets), format performance (cost-per-
engagement trends), series health (episode slope). A pillar with no measurable
primary response for three months needs to be merged, made dormant, or replaced —
but never killed without a recorded reason.

**Check question.** "What is your personal cut-off for calling a piece of content
successful for its format?" The answer defines the kill bar.

## 10 — Worked Example: Code & Canvas

Scenario: a solo creator with a weekly newsletter (Thursday) plus short-post and
professional feed platforms. Deliverable: a 4-week calendar.

**Capacity (measured).** 6 hours per week — Monday 2h (outlines), Wednesday 2h
(draft + edit), Friday 1h (cuts + scheduling), 1h reserve. Per asset: newsletter
essay 3.0h, short post 0.4h, feed post 0.6h, thread 1.2h (estimated until the
first thread is timed, then replaced with the measured value).

**Cadence math.** 6h = 1 essay (3.0) + 3 cuts (0.8 + 0.6 = 1.4) + 1 independent
(0.4) = 4.8h. Margin: 1.2h. Commitment: 1 anchor + 3 cuts + 1 independent per
week. Two proposals rejected: daily short posts (14h) and a second newsletter
(consumed margin).

**Pillar selection.** Proposed four pillars: Frontend Craft, Design Engineering,
Studio Notes, Culture Commentary. Culture Commentary had no recent material —
removed with a re-entry trigger (three case threads in a month).

**Seasonal overlay.** Conference on week 3. Decision: anchor publishes, one cut
drops to reserve, independent becomes a conference thread.

**Grid.** Each week: newsletter essay (Thursday) + 2 short cuts + 1 feed cut + 1
independent. Angles rotate: thesis, story, contrarian, number.

| Week | Anchor | Cuts | Independent |
|------|--------|------|-------------|
| 1 | "#1 Persistent Toolbar" | thesis (short), number (feed) | decision log |
| 2 | "#2 Modal That Survived" | story (short), teardown (feed) | design constraint |
| 3 | "#3 Navigation That Doesn't Migrate" | contrarian (short), thesis (feed) | conference thread |
| 4 | "#4 What 5 Years Taught Me" | number (short), recap (feed) | community reply |

Realized mix: craft 8, proof 5, studio 3 — on target.

**Repurposing with skip reasons.** Anchor 1: cuts thesis, number; skipped story,
graphic (strong claim, no anecdote). Anchor 2: cuts story, teardown; skipped
thesis (story IS the thesis). Anchor 3: cuts contrarian, thesis; skipped thread
(reserved as month-2 experiment). Anchor 4: cuts number, recap; skipped contrarian
(finale already contrarian). All cuts spaced 2-3 days apart with `source:` tag.

**Status at handoff.** Week 1: essay and cuts `scheduled`, independent `outlined`.
Weeks 2-4: essays `outlined`, cuts `empty` with sources recorded, independents
`empty`. One weak cut demoted to `empty` at planning and replaced.

**Batching.** Monday 2h: outline week N plus week N+1 skeleton. Wednesday 2h:
draft and edit essay; publishes Thursday. Friday 1h: write cuts, load schedulers.
Buffer: next week's essay outlined before this week's publishes.

**Buffer in action.** Week 2 Wednesday ran 40 minutes over. Reserve held; cuts
moved to Friday. All slots published. No apology.

**Week 2 review.** Shipped 4/4. Surprise: story post outperformed recent average
~3x — audience replied to voice, not facts. Cost: 5.5h, reserve untouched. One
change: cut writing moved from Friday to Thursday evening.

**Month-end.** Keep: series format and batch structure. Iterate: independent studio
posts — 2 of 4 lacked a concrete number; next month they must come from client
work with a specific decision. Kill: none. Feed cut on watch.

**Why it held.** Measured capacity (4.8 of 6h = 80%). Series carried recognition;
cuts carried reach. Buffer absorbed a slip. One change per week. Seasonal overlay
prevented surprise. Capacity honesty protected every other decision.

## 11 — Anti-Pattern Catalog (11 entries)

Each with signal, prevention, and fix.

1. **Cadence from ambition.** Signal: capacity from memory, not measurement. Fix:
run measurement protocol; cut in half if user refuses. Prevention: step 2 gates 3.

2. **Grid fills with easiest pillar.** Signal: one pillar exceeds two-thirds of
slots. Fix: move slots to underweight pillars. Prevention: balance check at grid
construction.

3. **Repurposing as feed spam.** Signal: 3+ cuts from one anchor on same surface
in two days. Fix: space remaining cuts, rotate angles, drop weakest. Prevention:
step 8 assigns cuts with spacing.

4. **Buffer erosion.** Signal: cost-check above budget two consecutive weeks. Fix:
cut one recurring slot for a month; re-measure. Prevention: rule 3 margin.

5. **Review overcorrection.** Signal: 3+ changes from one week. Fix: keep
strongest signal; park rest for monthly pass. Prevention: rule 10 one change.

6. **Series decay.** Signal: row shows `empty` consecutively. Fix: restore with
stated count or publish finale. Prevention: step 5 end condition.

7. **Tool sprawl.** Signal: tools disagree about next post. Fix: reconcile into
one; delete mirror. Prevention: rule 12 one calendar of record.

8. **Launch arc without follow-through.** Signal: post-launch week blank. Fix:
block retrospective before drafting launch. Prevention: step 6 full checklist.

9. **Expired anchor.** Signal: "not excited about week 4." Fix: replace with fresh
topic or evergreen. Prevention: anchors assigned max 2 weeks ahead.

10. **False recovery.** Signal: creator publicly announced break. Fix: remove
announcement. Prevention: recovery is built silently with banked assets.

11. **Series count inflation.** Signal: 3+ named series, most with no episodes.
Fix: merge weakest into pillar. Prevention: rule 7 caps at two.

## 12 — Failure Modes and Recovery (8 modes)

Each with signal, diagnosis, fix, and prevention.

1. **Cadence from ambition.** Signal: cadence before hours measured. Diagnosis:
step 2 skipped. Fix: measurement protocol; label as estimates if user refuses.
Prevention: step 2 gates step 3.

2. **Grid stacks with easiest pillar.** Signal: one pillar >2/3 of slots. Fix:
move slots between pillars; record the imbalance. Prevention: mandatory mix check
at step 9.

3. **Repurposing becomes spam.** Signal: 3+ cuts same anchor same surface
consecutive days. Fix: space, rotate, drop weakest. Prevention: cuts assigned with
spacing at step 8.

4. **Buffer erosion.** Signal: cost-check above budget 2 weeks. Fix: cut one slot
for a month; re-measure. Prevention: rule 3 margin is non-negotiable.

5. **Review overcorrection.** Signal: 3+ changes from one week. Fix: keep single
strongest; park rest. Prevention: one change per session.

6. **Series decay.** Signal: consecutive empty rows. Fix: restore with remaining
count or finale. Prevention: end condition fixed at series creation.

7. **Tool sprawl.** Signal: tools disagree. Fix: reconcile, delete mirror, re-
verify. Prevention: one calendar of record.

8. **Expired anchor.** Signal: week 3, week 4 anchor at empty. Fix: replace with
new topic or evergreen. Prevention: anchors ≤ 2 weeks ahead.

**Also covered in §11:** launch-arc-without-follow-through and false recovery
are anti-pattern catalog entries there; their workflow-side preventions are the
step 6 full checklist and rule 8 (silent recovery with banked assets).

**Post-pattern cleanup.** (1) Apply fix. (2) Write prevention into template notes.
(3) If same failure from a previous log, reinforce the owning workflow step.
(4) If from two cycles ago, change the workflow sequence itself.

**Reading order for a new practitioner:** This file top to bottom, then
`calendar-fundamentals.md` and `performance-review-loop.md`, then the template,
then the worked example.

**Quick-reference decision map:**
- "How many hours?" → `calendar-fundamentals.md`
- "How often per platform?" → `cadence-and-frequency.md`
- "What should I write about?" → `content-mix-design.md`
- "How to handle an event?" → `seasonal-and-event-planning.md`
- "How to get more from one piece?" → `repurposing-frameworks.md`
- "How to get it done weekly?" → `scheduling-workflows.md`
- "Is it working?" → `performance-review-loop.md`

**If none of these match:** the situation is probably a calendar problem in disguise — a cadence that no longer fits available hours, a mix that has drifted toward one content type, or a backlog that has quietly become the plan. Read `calendar-fundamentals.md` end to end before touching the calendar; the fundamentals file resolves most "none of these match" cases in one pass.

**The one question that beats every decision map:** "What did I publish in the last four weeks, and would I publish it again?" If the honest answer is "I don't remember," the calendar has been running the operator instead of the operator running the calendar. Stop planning forward, run the pre-planning content audit in §07, and rebuild the next four weeks from what the evidence — not the backlog — says works. A calendar is a hypothesis about attention, and hypotheses that are never checked become habits that are never questioned.

**The four-week build at a glance, for when you return.**

1. Audit: what was published in the last eight weeks, and what did it earn.
2. Capacity: real weekly hours, minus admin, minus one buffer week.
3. Pillars: three to five recurring themes the audience actually engages.
4. Slots: fixed cadence per surface, sized to the capacity number, not ambition.
5. Mix: pillars distributed across slots with deliberate format variety.
6. Repurpose: every pillar piece gets one derivative planned, not three.
7. Events: dates that matter go in first; the grid bends around them.
8. Batch: outline, draft, edit, and schedule in themed blocks, one surface per block.
9. Review: weekly metrics check, monthly keep/kill/iterate on slots.

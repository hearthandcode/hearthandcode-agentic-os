# Dialogue Craft

A checklist and methodology reference for writing dialogue that works. Good dialogue is not good conversation — good conversation is messy, repetitive, and polite. Good dialogue is compressed, pointed, and says things the speaker would never say directly.

## 1. The compression ratio

Dialogue operates at approximately 3:1 compression: every line that the reader sees counts for about three lines of real speech. Contractions, interruptions, pauses, and the words before the real word are not dialogue — they are drag. A rule of thumb: if you can delete a line and the scene still makes sense, delete it. Then cut again.

Compression preserves:
- **Conflict:** any line the character could not say in real conversation because it is too direct.
- **Emotion:** any line that shows what a character would not admit.
- **Information:** the least possible information needed to advance the scene.

What compression removes: greetings, polite exchanges, transitions, recaps.

---

## 2. Voice differentiation

Characters in the room with similar voices produce anonymous dialogue. Apply three differentiation techniques:

**Lexical range:** Each character picks a short list of words they use more heavily than others and a list of words they never use. Track these in the character sheet. A soldier who says "affirmative" and "negative" has a different voice than one who says "sure" and "no chance."

**Sentence architecture:** Some characters speak in full well-formed clauses; others trail off mid-thought. Some question; others command. Some clarify; others deflect. The shape of the sentence says as much as its content.

**Disclosure control:** Characters have different default reactions to being uncomfortable. One fills silence with facts. One fills it with accusation. One goes quiet completely. The response to discomfort is one of the most revealing voice markers.

A character should be identifiable from a single line of dialogue in unfamiliar context — without name tags, without action beats.

---

## 3. The three functions of dialogue

Every line of dialogue must do at least one of these:

**Advance information.** The audience learns something new about the plot, the world, or the character's agenda. If the line restates the obvious, cut it.

**Character-building raise.** Each line reveals the character's state. Even a neutral remark reveals mood, attitude, or value. If the character could be anyone, the line is not doing its job.

**Tension change.** A line must either escalate or deescalate the scene's tension. Lines that keep the same temperature are filler. A rule I've used: each character in a scene should want something that only the other character can give, and each line should move that wants.

---

## 4. Exposition control

People in real life share information we already know on both sides of a conversation. In fiction, that's what exposition sounds like. Exposition control rules:

**Rule 1 — One truth per scene.** The reader learns only one piece of necessary.

Necessary: the reader must know it to understand the scene that follows. This tests the entire scene's content.

Not necessary: the reader can infer it from later context. A good test: the reader could figure out the character's occupation from observing them for a scene without being told.

**Rule 2 — Stakeweighted dialogue.** A character will reveal information only when doing so is necessary to get something they want. The more they want it, the more their guarded information can be revealed. If a character reveals a secret easily, the reader doesn't know the secret cost.

**Rule 3 — Interruption.** Information is revealed in fits, not blocks. Interruptions break exposition into bite-sized pieces. If a scene has uninterrupted exposition, it will feel educational.

**Rule 4 — Specific characterize.** Instead of "I was in the war," use "I was at the crossing of the Bulge." The specific locks the exposition into the world of the reader.

## 5. Common dialogue faults and fixes

| Fault | Signal | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Q&A dialogue** | Character A asks a series of questions; B provides. The dialogue reads as a police interview. | Reverse the script: either both characters have something to hide or the questions have acknowledged costs. |
| **Vig dialogue** | Characters say they are angry. | Trust punctuation and word choice. "I am truly very angry right now" is always weaker than the silent alternative. |
| **On-the-nose dialogue** | Characters say exactly what they think. | Every line of dialogue should be a reveal of less than the character thinks they are revealing. |

## 6. Dialogue structure for the scene

Every scene has a single point where dialogue moves from the pretense of normalcy to the true business of the scene. That turn is the scene's "moment of acknowledging":

- The moment where both characters understand what the scene is actually about.
- The moment often begins "most of you" or "what are we doing here."

This moment happens about 20 percent of the way into a typical effective scene. Before it is setup; after it is the scene's real work.

---

## 7. Self-testing the craft

**Read aloud test.** Dialogue read aloud and sounds unnatural is wrong. If a line makes you stumble on the word, rewrite it.

**Remove tags.** If the reader can't assign a line of dialogue to the correct character without the "he said" tag, the dialogue is not differentiated enough.

**Overhead test.** Reread a dialogue that is long without direct attribution. If the reader has lost track of the speaker, the scene needs more grounding or the voices need sharper.

**The table test.** Write a scene's dialogue out as a table with two columns, one per speaker. Then cut it: map the conflict progress across each exchange. A Scene whose conflict flags conflict has risen to the point of dialogue.

## 8. The dialogue craft checklist

- Every line serves at least one of the three functions (info, character, tension)
- No two character voices are interchangeable
- Subtext is present in at least 80% of exchanges, the exception being intentional directness
- Exposition is measured in sentences, not paragraphs
- The scene's pivot point is present and identifiable
- Each character begins the scene wanting different something that only another character in the scene can grant
- The scene contains at least one line the reader could not have predicted
- The excerpt has at least two lines of no dialogue (action beats or description)
- Read it out: if it's missing a line, the character's voice is a match to the writer's instead of their own

# Narrative Outlining

A methodology reference for creating outlines that guide rather than trap. The outline's job is not to predict every word of the story but to surface problems — pacing gaps, missing character motivation, structural dead ends — before you write 10,000 words into a dead end.

## 1. Scene cards

The atomic unit of outlining is the single scene. A scene is a unit of continuous action in a single location, one point of view, one purpose. If the location changes, it is a new scene. If the point of view changes, it is a new scene. If time passes, separate scene.

**Scene card format:**

```
Scene: [number and short name]
In: [location]
Time: [relative position, elapsed duration]
POV: [character whose experience we follow]
Purpose: [what this scene exists to accomplish]
Emotional move: [feeling start → feeling end]
Beats: [3-5 action verbs of what happens]
Conflict: [what the POV character wants vs. what the scene gives them]
Next scene requires: [what the scene must establish to open the next]
```

**Limitation to one sentence per field keeps each scene a single unit of controlled thinking.**

**Cards more than one per purpose:** very few scenes have more than one central purpose. A scene that is both "establish the protagonist's failing marriage" and "reveal the antagonist's plan" is two scenes; pick one or split them.

**Working with scene cards:**

- Use them to test story sequence: lay out all scenes physically or sequentially. If any scene seems to not follow from its preceding scene, the story has a logic gap.
- Use them to test proportion: if 15 scene cards for the story so far spend too many scenes on a small action and too few on a pivot, the outline shows the imbalance.
- Use them to test range: calculate the number of scenes is among characters. A cluster of scenes with only the protagonist suggests the world is empty.

## 2. Beat sheets

A beat sheet is a high-level outline of the story's structure, tracking the major narrative beats — the infrastructural moments that define the shape of the story. Beat sheets do not track individual scenes; they track movements.

Key beats:

1. **Inciting incident:** the event that makes the story necessary. If the protagonist did not go from this moment, the story would be normal life.
2. **First plot point:** the first major decision by the protagonist that changes the direction. Usually corresponds with the end of Act One.
3. **Midpoint:** the point of no return. The central conflict is now about something the character cannot walk away from.
4. **All is lost:** the lowest point before the end. The protagonist has tried everything and failed.
5. **Final push:** the climax, the confrontation, the decision.
6. **Denouement:** what follows the climax. The reader sees the new steady state.

These beats are not prescriptive; some stories shift or compress. But they are diagnostic: if a draft's outline lacks any of these beats, the outline may be missing the structural pressure that keeps the reader turning pages.

**Beat summary:** in a beat sheet, include not only the plot events but also the emotional movement. The beat "the protagonist receives the summons" is incomplete: "the protagonist receives the summons, refusing at first (leaving because of fear) is a beat that contains information about the character."

## 3. Discovery vs planning

Every writer inhabits some position on a spectrum between pure discovery (writing forward to see what happens) and pure planning (complete outlining before writing). The skill of narrative outlining is not to force planning but to test that discovery produces structural completeness early.

**Pure discovery mode:** Write without an outline. Let the story teach you what is wanted. When the story does into revision, produce the outline from the first draft to see if it is coherent. If the story is good and the outline curve appears fixed, good. If the story is good and the outline curve is compromised, fix the outline for the next draft.

**Pure planning mode:** Outline the entire narrative first. Mark only the insistent chart and be precise. Exhaust the outline of its contradictions before writing line one. The risk is knowing the story as a structure rather than a system.

**Mixed method:** Outline the first strong dramatization of the inciting incident up to about the midpoint — enough to see that you have an engine — then discovery the second half. The discovery of the second half can fill in what the outline could not foresee. Write a placeholder for the last act based on how the second half resolves.

**Test of a good outline (any mode):** If you can explain the story from the outline to a reader, and the reader can intuit the story's emotional duration, the outline is working. If the reader asks "but what does the protagonist want?" the outline is weak on character.

## 4. Scene-by-scene progression

After beats, build scene-by-scene with movement at the scene level:

- Each scene must have a conflict in the movement from the beginning of the scene to the end.
- The scene's task is to either advance the narrative or contract the narrative; no scene must be at the same state as the map for the preceding scene.
- Between scenes, place a break point: usually, something that makes the reader want to turn the page. The break function can be a revelation, an escalation, a question (the narrative), or an absent detail.

A useful formula: "And then" is progression; "but therefore" is structure. An outline where each beat connects with "and then" is a flat sequence; an outline where each beat connects with "but therefore" is a story.

---

## 5. Outline formats

No single outline format fits all stories. Match format to story:

- **Storyboard:** Visual layout for large-scene shifts with a visual-to-ratio structure.
- **Table outline:** the beat sheet mapped as a table with characters, setting, purpose, and emotional arc per scene.
- **Beat-extended prose:** For stories where the beat's thematic and internal development is important.
- **Role expand:** Write the outline scene summaries of whole scenes (prose pages) rather than card values.

---

## 6. Common outlining pitfalls around discovery mode

- **Professional framing.** Setting, characters, and structure in the outline before scenes. The outline then is list of nouns, not active story.
- **Perfect outline syndrome:** Working the outline to the point of exceeding plot but drawing intent out of narration. Set a draft of the outline: the outcome is the story report, not the outline of the story.
- **Late skip.** The outline is complete but reveals a big gap in story logic at plot. The gap should be solved in the outline before prose proceeds.

---

## 7. Outline verification

After writing a scene outline, give the following test:

1. Does every scene have a character with a want?
2. Does every scene's want contended by someone or something?
3. Do the scene outcomes produce the emotional curve of the structure? (Is there a shape of the story: rise, peak and fall?)
4. In the scene progression, is the story's theme present at the inciting, midpoint and resolution?
5. Does the scene that is test change from the protagonist's interior position?
6. Are there any redundant scenes (two scenes that do the same job)?
7. What is the first scene that can be removed without breaking the story?

If at least three of these are answered with confidence, the outline is ready to write.

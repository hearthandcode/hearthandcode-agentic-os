# Character Design

A methodology reference for building characters who drive story. Character design is the art of creating someone whose desires, fears, and contradictions make the plot inevitable. A character who could be removed from the story and replaced with a different person without changing events is not yet a character — they are a function.

## 1. Four foundational principles

**Desire:** Every character wants something specific, concrete, and immediate. A reason for being in the story's first scene. This is not their life goal; it is what they want in Chapter 1 that the story will not let them have. Extrinsic desire is what the character states out loud; intrinsic desire is what the story reveals they actually need.

**Wound:** A durable flaw or injury — psychological, social, or physical — that the character developed out of a past experience. The wound does three things: it explains why they behave in ways that hurt themselves; it makes empathetic failure or redemption arcs possible; it determines what kind of last-act choice they will be untested for.

**Contradiction:** A trait that resists the reader's desire to simply categorize the character. The hero who is afraid of crowds. The liar who is scrupulously honest about one thing. Contradiction creates a sense that the character is deeper than the classification.

**Arc type:** The change the character cannot avoid passing through. Not all characters arc: some stories demand a static protagonist, and the change in that story is in the world. But every story demands that at least one relationship (character to value, character to self, character to another) transform by the end.

## 2. The three-axis character model

Use three axes to track the character's positions relative to the story:

**Interior:** The gap between self-image and reality. The wider this gap, the more the character's self-narrative is a source of the story's tension. Measured by: earliest appearance of the self-narrative; contrasting evidence; cost of any story event that widens the gap.

**Relational:** The character's stance toward others. Not just who they love or hate, but the question they ask of every new person: what does this person want controls them? If a character consistently misassigns others in this way, the relational axis controls the plot.

**Active:** The character's stance toward their own agency. Do they act on the world or are they acted upon? The arc of a protagonist who begins passive and ends decisive is the most common character arc. The arc of a protagonist who begins decisive and ends passive ends tragically.

## 3. The seven archetype slots (not roles)

Treated as slots rather than fixed archetypes: these are positions any character can occupy for a given story scene or segment of story. A single character may shift between slots over the arc's development.

1. **Initiator:** Acts first. The character who makes something happen.
2. **Witness:** Observes and transmits. The reader's lens into the world.
3. **Obstructer:** Blocks the protagonist's path. Not necessarily the villain: may be the best friend, the law, inertia.
4. **Helper:** Contributes capacity. Provides facts, tools, shelter, or the other function of the dominant character.
5. **Lesson:** Embodies something the protagonist must learn, by symbol, by example, or by caution.
6. **Reflector:** Offers the protagonist a mirror — shows who they are, what they are avoiding, or what they could become.
7. **Catalyst:** Appears, changes something, and leaves. A catalyst character need not recur to have changed the story.

## 4. Voice differentiation: the three-layer test

Characters' voices should be classifiable by anyone reading the dialogue:

- **Lexical:** Not just "uses jargon" but which 3-5 words they reach for under stress. The lawyer reaches for "clearly." The soldier reaches for "solid."
- **Syntactic:** Sentence length, question-as-response, unfinished sentences, interruption. A character who always speaks in complete clauses suggests a writer or a politician.
- **Relationship to disclosure:** How much of their internal state they show. A character who reveals everything will sound different from one who reveals only the outward.

Each character should be recognizable in the middle of a heated group dialogue with all name tags removed.

---

## 5. Team and mismatch arcs

Characters in the same story cohere not by agreement or by conflict. The strongest team is a set of characters whose desires cross in ways that generate the narrative. Use a mismatch matrix:

For each pair of characters in a core cast: is the fit, in the current scene, (a) matching, (b) complementary, or (c) conflictual? A story that stays in matching for every pair becomes static; conflictual across all pairs is exhausting. Alternate (b) complementary and (c) conflictual across scenes.

Every state of the mismatch matrix must be realizable at least once in the story.

## 6. Wound depth

A character's wound sits at one of three levels:

1. **Surface:** A correctable mistake they made as a child. The rest of the story is the chance to make it right.
2. **Identity:** A chosen identity or belief that is wrong but not reversible. The character can only learn to renegotiate how they live within this.
3. **Truth:** A wound so central that the character cannot survive after confronting it.

The level of the wound determines the story's arc temperature. A surface wound yields a comedy or a light romance. A truth-level wound yields a tragedy or a redemption that costs everything.

For assigned characters, you know the wound level. For invented characters, select the wound, then sketch the backstory that could cause it up to that level.

## 7. Checklist for a complete character

1. What does this character want, in scene one, that they will not get?
2. What do they believe — the unexamined chip in their worldview that the story will break?
3. What is the moment at which they could believe the story is over, and why would they act on that false information?
4. Whose opinion do they weigh too heavily, and why?
5. Name two ways the character contradicts themselves.
6. What skill do they think is their strongest that is actually a crutch?
7. What do other characters say about them that is both true and unfair?
8. What is the line the character will not cross, and how does the story get them to cross it or watch it be crossed?
9. What is the sentence that would break the character? (This is the line the story is moving toward.)
10. If the character's arc is not shown — if the story only observes them — what is the fixed quality they prove true of them?

---

## 8. Common faults

- **Pancake character:** All surface traits, no pressure. Test: the reader cannot say what the character wants for any given scene.
- **Tag character:** Reduced to a single feature — witty dialogue, strong accent, drinking problem. Test: drop the single feature; does the character disappear?
- **Determined character:** Has so much control that no outcome surprises the reader. Test: is there a moment when the character acts against their own best nature?
- **Passive character:** Reacts to everything, initiates nothing. Test: if at least one scene does not begin with the character motivating content to, the character is passive and the story is about forces.

All four can be fixed with the same tool: give the character a want that is in conflict with themselves, and put that want at the center of the scene.

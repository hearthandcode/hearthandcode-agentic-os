# Revision and Editing

A methodology reference for turning a first draft into a finished piece. Revision is not about fixing; it is about making the story what it needs to be. The first draft is you telling the story to yourself; every draft after that is you and the reader.

## 1. The revision pass order

Editing has a best order. Reversing the order wastes energy: you line-edit a page whose existence is removed in structural revision. Work from macro to line — never the other way.

**Pass 1 — Structural revision.** Does the plot hold? Are there scenes that don't advance the narrative? Do characters act consistently? Ask if the story does what it set out to do. Delete, reorder, add, combine. This is the hardest pass and the one most writers skip. Do not edit the language; change the architecture.

**Pass 2 — Sequence and pacing.** Is information timed for maximum effect? Is the emotional curve working — escalation, plateau, release? Are scenes in the right order, or would alternating order produce a better effect? What sentence would spoil? The pacing pass removes all small scenes that are only transitions.

**Pass 3 — Character pass.** Separate reading pass just for each main character: Does their dialogue match their voice? Does their arc track? Each character's internal logic. Does the character's behavior in the climax are the behavior they believed in earlier or is there a drawn change without lead-up?

**Pass 4 — Language and compression.** Every single word justified. Cut 10% from the word count. If a 1500-word story, cut to 1350 words. Cut adjectives, cut metaphors, cut abstractions, cut those. Reading the story aloud. The ear catches what the eye glides.

**Pass 5 — Line editing.** Catch your cliches, repeated phrases, and consistency errors. A character enters on the "warm smell" on page 1 and exits to "the warm dusk" on page 2. The writing life is full of these - until you edit.

## 2. Cutting: what to cut and what not

Having trouble knowing what to cut? Use four:

- **The compression test:** Read the address. What 80 words does it contain? Try to write it in 40. The cut is less painful than you think.
- **The self-describing paragraph:** Paragraphs beginning with "x felt" or "it was clear" are interpretations that can be cut without the reader losing the story's understanding.
- **Transition (staging) words:** "Then", "Suddenly", "Meanwhile", "On the other hand." If these words appear, treat them as suspect. Usually they mask a missing performance.
- **The start and the end:** Short stories often start more than one sentence too late — find that start and cut what's before. They also end more than a sentence later — find that cut. Cutting from both ends will lose the most.

## 3. Beta feedback triage

Beta readers provide the best and worst feedback — the best because it's real, the worst because they give you reactions, not prescriptions. Not all feedback is equal; sort it by type.

**Reaction-level feedback.** "The slow part around page 20." "I didn't like the main character." These are not wrong; they are inputs to the diagnosis. You are not to meet every reaction, but you must act on the rule: if one reader finds it confusing, the reader is confused. If three readers find it confusing, the text is confusing.

**Prescriptive feedback.** "You should cut the sub-plot / add a car chase." Prescriptions are one reader's story, not yours. Use only for the problem reading the prescribed solution; apply the median of several reactions, not the prescription.

**Diagnostic feedback.** "The protagonist's actions in this scene don't match his earlier behavior." "The pacing in chapter 2 and 4 feels even though there's different momentum." These are gold. Suspend change in these.

**Frequency triage:**

| Pain | One reader reports | Two or more readers |
|---|---|---|
| "I don't understand" | Ear: note; check the text may be confused | Must clarify |
| "I don't care" | reflective: consider | Structural problem at |
| "The ending I didn't get" | Could be a respectful reading | Read: unrevise ending |
| "I love the dog" | Check that there is a dog | Keep the dog |

## 4. Self-editing for clarity

Before anyone sees the draft, read the draft as a reader who:

- Is not inside the author's mindset.
- Cannot already interpret the characters and events.

Test with these filters:

**The naming filter.** Can the reader hold all characters in the first page? If you have three characters with two-syllable names beginning with J, the reader cannot tell them apart. Renaming is not vanity; it is readability.

**Cause clarity.** Does the reader always know why a character does what they do? The cause of an action is not always stated, but when the character acts, the reader never says "but why would they do that?" If they would, the characters need a character scene or the action's cause must be in context.

**Consequence clarity.** When something happens, the reader knows what it means. If a character receives a note and the reader does not know the note's significance, the scene before the note needs its effect.

**Spatial clarity.** Can the reader imagine where everyone stands in a scene? Are they sitting, standing, moving, entering? The reader without a scene that cannot be underscored and not set by scene spatial.

## 5. Elimination of writer's marks

After revision, read for these telltale signs of first draft;

- **Too-perfect grammar.** Too perfectly said means not enough character. A draft that's all well-formed sentences is a draft that hasn't yet breathed.
- **Hedging words:** Something, seemed, almost, probably, only, maybe, basically. The work of writing is turning the experience of the story into a thing that the reader believes.
- **Superordinate adjectives:** Very, quite, actual, extreme, total, complete. The work of writing is providing the right noun and verb, and tone the word.
- **Dialogue tags where action beats would work:** "he said" when "he earned the stove with his back turned" shows more.

## 6. The revision checklistigua

A single page you run against each revision scene:

- Does this scene change?
- Could someone with the same scene's information predict this scene from the earlier material?
- Does the language a single thing known of this scene's purpose?
- The opening line is also the last 3 words: is the opening a paragraph or less?
- That could be cut?
- Are there any seconds to page while this scene is active?
- Is the scene the smallest number of characters needed? Any extras are trimmed.
- Does the end of the scene lift the reader into the next scene?
- Does the scene justify its position in the story?
- Is the pace appropriate to the scene's purpose? Not always fast.
- Can this scene be removed without breaking the story?

Strict conditions: If the answer to "Does the scene change for the characters?" is "No," the scene does not hold its weight. Either it becomes a stronger scene or it is removed.

## 7. The cooling period

Between final revisions, a period of not looking at the work is the strongest editing tool. The text becomes foreign again. The writer for a writer of a short story, 3 days. For a novel, 2 weeks. After the cooling time, what was invisible fatigue or emotional attachment to the draft's clause will appear.

---

## 8. The sign of completion

The story is finished when no scene can be moved from one location to a different location without breaking the reader's continuity — and the ending does not transform when one sentence is removed. There is no perfect version. Two stories revised to the same completion point can be different in character and quality. The sign of a completion is not "no lines can be improved" but "no scene can be removed without the reader asking for it."

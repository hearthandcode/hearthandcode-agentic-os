# story-and-narrative-design

## 01 — Purpose

A story fails in one of a few predictable ways: the structure sags in the
middle, the character has no credible want, the world leaks inconsistency, the
dialogue explains instead of dramatizes, the theme is asserted rather than
carried, or the draft is revised in the wrong order. This skill exists to
catch those failures while they are still cheap — at the outline stage, at the
character-sheet stage, at the beat-sheet stage — and to turn a finished draft
into a finished story through disciplined revision.

The skill covers the full arc: developing the premise into a structural
choice, designing characters whose desires make the plot inevitable, building
only as much world as the story needs, drafting dialogue with subtext,
registering a motif system that carries theme, and then running revision
passes in an order that finds structural problems before line problems. It
produces a story packet — a set of linked artifacts — rather than prose alone;
the prose is yours, and this skill makes sure the machinery underneath it
holds.

**Three outcomes this skill owns:**

1. A story design that a writer can draft from: structure chosen for a stated
   reason, beat sheet with per-beat word budgets, and scene cards that name
   each scene's turn.
2. Character and world documents that survive contact with drafting — sheets
   where desire, wound, and contradiction drive the plot rather than decorate
   it, and a world whose rules were tested before page 40.
3. A prioritized revision plan that tells the writer what to fix first, what
   to leave alone, and when the piece is done.

This skill treats structure as a toolkit, not a formula: every model in it
carries a domain fit, a failure mode, and a test, so the choice is made with
intent and the trade is known.

**Scope boundary.** This skill owns the story's design: structure, character,
world, dialogue, theme, and revision. Adjacent work is adjacent: raw idea
generation is brainstorming-and-ideation, finished audience-facing copy is
copywriting-and-messaging, game rules are game-mechanics-design, and the
spaces themselves are level-and-encounter-design territory. Handoffs stay
explicit — a level designer receives the beats a space must serve; a
copywriter receives the voice sheet and message hierarchy of a brand story.

**Operating stance.** Three habits run through everything here:

- **Structure serves the story; the story does not serve the structure.** A
  beat sheet is a diagnostic instrument — it surfaces the sagging middle
  before 10,000 words are written into it, not a template to enforce.
- **Design on paper before prose.** A character who cannot answer ten
  questions on a sheet will not survive a chapter; an outline that cannot
  state what changes in each scene will not survive a draft.
- **Revise in passes, not stains.** Structure first, scene next, line last —
  a gorgeous sentence in a cut scene is a wasted sentence.

**Vocabulary used throughout.** These terms are used with these meanings in
every file in this skill:

- *Premise* — the story in one sentence: a character with a problem that
  forces action. If it has no character and no problem, it is a mood, not a
  premise.
- *Beat* — one movement of story value: the unit that changes something the
  reader cares about from one state to another.
- *Scene card* — one scene on one block: goal, conflict, disaster or turn,
  POV, setting, characters, and the value the scene shifts.
- *Want / need* — what the character consciously pursues versus what would
  actually resolve their wound; the gap between them is the arc.
- *Wound* — the past injury that shaped the character's flawed strategy for
  living; it explains why the ordinary behavior is wrong for this plot.
- *Motif* — a recurring concrete pattern (image, phrase, action) that carries
  theme by accumulation rather than statement.
- *Pass* — one revision sweep with one job; mixing jobs in a pass is how
  structural problems hide under line polish.

## 02 — When to Use / When Not to Use

**Use this skill when any of these are true:**

1. **Turning a premise into a plan.** You have a one-line premise ("a lighthouse
   keeper receives a signal from a ship that sank decades ago") and need a
   structure, beat sheet, and scene plan before drafting.
2. **Outlining a story or chapter.** You are building a beat sheet or scene
   cards, deciding scene order, or checking whether the outline has a
   structural dead end before the drafting investment.
3. **Developing characters.** You need sheets with want, wound, contradiction,
   arc, and voice — or you suspect a character is a function, not a person,
   because the plot would not change if they were replaced.
4. **Designing the world.** You are building a setting's rules, history, and
   texture for fiction or a game, and need top-down versus bottom-up decisions,
   iceberg discipline, and a consistency checklist.
5. **Writing or repairing dialogue.** Scenes feel flat, voices blur together,
   or exposition is being delivered by characters who would never say it —
   dialogue needs craft rules, not more words.
6. **Designing theme and motif.** You know what happens but not what it is
   *about*, or you want a motif system that accumulates meaning instead of
   repeating decoration.
7. **Diagnosing a draft that is not working.** Something is off — pacing,
   stakes, a character who acts like a puppet — and you need a structured
   diagnosis before rewriting anything.
8. **Planning revision.** You have a complete first draft and need the pass
   order, cut list, and feedback-triage method that turns it into a finished
   piece.
9. **Designing narrative for a game or experience.** You need story beats,
   environmental storytelling intent, or dialogue direction that hands off
   cleanly to level and mechanics work.

**Do not use this skill when:**

1. **The ideas do not exist yet.** Blank-page ideation — "give me twenty
   premises," "find the angle" — belongs to brainstorming-and-ideation. This
   skill shapes a chosen premise; it does not generate the candidates.
2. **The output is marketing copy.** Taglines, product stories, landing-page
   narrative, or brand voice writing belong to copywriting-and-messaging.
   Story craft feeds a brand narrative; the deliverable here is not that
   deliverable.
3. **The mechanics are the job.** What the player does every thirty seconds —
   loops, rules, rewards — belongs to game-mechanics-design. This skill
   supplies the story those mechanics carry, not the rules themselves.
4. **The layout is the job.** Where the corridor bends and where the ambush
   waits belongs to level-and-encounter-design. Environmental storytelling
   *intent* is designed here; the space itself is theirs.
5. **Aesthetic rendering is the job.** Visual polish, palette, screens, and
   final presentation belong to ui-design-critique and
   design-system-foundations. This skill describes the burn marks on the
   door; it does not draw or judge them.
7. **The business plan is the job.** Pricing, market sizing, and
   go-to-market for the finished work belong to business-planning and
   marketing-strategy; writing the book is this skill, selling it is theirs.
8. **Publication logistics are the job.** Formatting, distribution, and
   submission housekeeping need no skill here — they are mechanical steps
   any competent writer or assistant can run from a checklist.

When in doubt: if the deliverable is a *beat*, a *sheet*, a *scene*, or a
*revision plan*, this skill applies. If it is a *rule*, a *screen*, a *sale*,
or a *space*, hand off at the boundary with the artifacts the neighbor needs.

## 03 — Inputs and Outputs

**Inputs this skill consumes:**

- A premise, however rough: one line is enough to start; a paragraph with
  protagonist and stakes is enough to align on.
- Constraints: target length (flash to novella), POV and tense intentions,
  audience, genre expectations, deadlines, and any series or continuity
  obligations.
- Existing material when the work is iterative: a partial draft, a treatment,
  an existing outline, world bible, or character notes from earlier work.
- Feedback material when revising: beta reader notes, editorial letters, or
  your own dissatisfaction — the triage method needs raw reactions, not
  pre-digested task lists.
- For game narrative: the game's pillars, player fantasy, session structure,
  and any mechanics that story must serve (from game-mechanics-design).

**Input readiness checklist.** Before starting the workflow, confirm:

- The premise names a character and a problem, not just a situation ("a city
  flooded for a hundred years" is a setting; "the last lighthouse keeper
  receives a signal from a ship that sank decades ago" is a premise).
- Length and audience are named, or steps 1-2 will invent them silently.
- For revision work: the draft is complete or the gap is explicit — revising
  an unfinished draft mixes discovery with repair and poisons both.
- Feedback being reacted to is attributed and quoted; paraphrased feedback
  is the reader's theory, not their reaction. Where the story extends
  existing work, the authority document for continuity is identified.

**Outputs this skill produces:**

- `templates/story-outline-template.md` — the story packet's spine: metadata,
  thematic argument, beat sheet with word budgets, scene cards, motif
  registrations, and revision targets. The primary artifact.
- `templates/character-sheet-template.md` — one per principal character:
  identity, want, wound, contradiction, arc type, three-axis profile, voice
  differentiation, and scene-by-scene slot tracking.
- Structure decision record: chosen model, alternatives considered, and why
  the choice fits this premise — one paragraph kept beside the outline.
- World notes: rules, history, costs, and the iceberg inventory — what is
  decided, what is deliberately unknown, and what must never contradict.
- Dialogue revision notes: per-voice lexical and syntax rules, subtext map
  for key scenes, exposition load audit.
- Revision plan: prioritized and pass-ordered, with cut lists and a
  completion bar — the artifact that turns notes into work.

**Acceptance criteria per output.** An output is done when:

- A *beat sheet* shows a value shift in every beat, a word budget per beat
  that sums to the target length, and no two adjacent beats moving the same
  value in the same direction twice without escalation.
- A *scene card* could be handed to a different writer who would write a
  recognizable version of the same scene — goal, conflict, turn, and exit
  state are all stated.
- A *character sheet* passes the ten-question test and names, for every
  scene the character appears in, what they want and what they do about it.
- A *world note* states its cost: every power, technology, or magic system
  entry says what it costs and who is excluded from it.
- A *revision plan* is ordered structural-first, cites the evidence (which
  beat, which line, which reader), and names what will *not* be changed.

**Artifact conventions.** How outputs are named, stored, and linked:

- One outline file per story; character sheets named for the character and
  kept beside it; world notes one file per story, not per chapter.
- Every artifact carries its template's frontmatter — title, version, date,
  status — so a later reader knows which draft the notes belong to.
- Beat numbers are stable: scenes get IDs at outline time and keep them
  through revision, so "beat 4" means the same scene a month later.
- Revision notes append to the artifact they concern; a cut scene is not
  deleted but moved to a dated appendix with one line saying why. Structure
  decisions are recorded beside the outline, or the next editor relitigates
  them.

## 04 — Workflow

The steps below are the full arc from premise to revision plan. A rescue job
(a broken draft, a flat act two) enters at the step that matches the symptom
and exits at step 12; greenfield stories run the whole list. Each step names
its reference file; load the reference before doing the step's work.

1. **State the premise and the question it asks.** Write the one-line premise
   and the dramatic question the story will keep open ("will Kaia answer a
   signal she was never meant to hear?"). If the premise has no character
   with a problem, grow it before anything else. Read
   `references/story-structure-models.md` for what a premise must supply to
   each candidate structure.
2. **Choose the structure deliberately.** Shortlist two or three models from
   the structure reference, test the premise against each model's domain fit
   and failure mode, and write the decision record: choice, alternatives,
   reason. A hybrid is legitimate; an unconsidered default is not. Read
   `references/story-structure-models.md` — the decision matrix section
   exists for this step.
3. **Design the protagonist from the wound up.** Fill
   `templates/character-sheet-template.md` for the protagonist: extrinsic
   want, intrinsic need, wound, contradiction, arc type, and the three-axis
   start/end profile. Read `references/character-design.md` first; the test
   is whether the plot is *inevitable* given who this person is — if any
   competent stranger would act differently in their place, the motivation
   is not yet load-bearing.
4. **Cast the supporting cast into slots.** Assign remaining characters to
   archetype slots, give each a voice-differentiation line, and verify no
   two characters could be swapped without changing events. Repeat step 3's
   sheet for any character who occupies more than two scenes.
5. **Build only the world the story will touch.** Apply the iceberg rule:
   decide the rules that will bite, the history that will press on the
   present, and the costs that make choices matter — and leave the rest
   unknown on purpose. Read `references/worldbuilding-methods.md`; write the
   consistency checklist now, while changes are still cheap.
6. **Register the thematic argument and motif system.** Write the theme as an
   arguable statement ("a kindness can be a cruelty"), then choose two or
   three motifs and place their first appearances on the beat sheet. Read
   `references/theme-and-motif.md`; restraint rules apply — a motif that
   appears once is noise, and one that appears everywhere is wallpaper.
7. **Build the beat sheet.** List the story's beats in order, each with the
   value that shifts, the emotional move, and a word budget that sums to the
   target length. Read `references/narrative-outlining.md` for beat-sheet
   mechanics; fill `templates/story-outline-template.md` as you go. Check
   the middle now: the second act's sag is an outline problem before it is a
   draft problem.
8. **Expand key beats into scene cards.** For each beat that carries a
   turning point, write a scene card: goal, conflict, disaster or turn, POV,
   setting, characters present, and exit state. Read
   `references/narrative-outlining.md` on scene cards and progression; the
   card set should reveal any structural dead end while it still costs one
   page to fix.
9. **Draft the prose.** Write scenes from the cards, in whatever order the
   energy allows, deferring polish. Where dialogue carries the scene, apply
   the compression test and the three functions before accepting a line.
   Read `references/dialogue-craft.md`; watch its common-faults section
   during drafting, not after.
10. **Run the revision passes in order.** Structure pass (beats, sag, dead
    ends), scene pass (goals, turns, entrances), then line pass, then motif
    audit, then a cooling period before the final proof. Read
    `references/revision-and-editing.md` for the pass order and the cut
    criteria; the order is not negotiable — line polish before structure is
    how writers fall in love with scenes the story does not need.
11. **Triage reader feedback.** Collect reactions verbatim, sort them into
    must-fix (confusion, broken empathy), should-fix (pacing, clarity), and
    taste (leave it), and translate every complaint into a need before
    translating it into a change. Read `references/revision-and-editing.md`
    on beta feedback triage; a note that begins "you should" is a hypothesis,
    not an instruction.
12. **Produce the revision plan and declare completion.** Write the
    prioritized plan (what, where, why, cost), verify the motif audit and
    consistency checklist one final time, and state the completion bar: the
    conditions under which no further revision runs. Read
    `references/story-case-studies.md` to compare your arc against a full
    premise-to-revision run before declaring done.

Steps 1-6 are design work with sheet outputs. Steps 7-8 are structural work
with outline outputs. Steps 9-12 are execution work with draft and revision
outputs. Most failed stories skipped 3 or 7 — an unmotivated protagonist or
an unchecked middle — and paid for it across all twelve.

**Emitted artifacts by step:**

| Step | Emits |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Premise line and dramatic question |
| 2 | Structure decision record: choice, alternatives, reason |
| 3 | Protagonist character sheet (template-filled) |
| 4 | Supporting cast sheets and slot assignments |
| 5 | World notes with consistency checklist |
| 6 | Thematic argument and motif registrations |
| 7 | Beat sheet with value shifts and word budgets |
| 8 | Scene cards for turning-point beats |
| 9 | Draft prose (scenes from cards) |
| 10 | Pass-by-pass revision notes and cut list |
| 11 | Feedback triage table: reactions, buckets, needs |
| 12 | Prioritized revision plan and completion bar |

**Common entry points.** Not every job starts at step 1:

- *Greenfield story:* start at 1, run all twelve.
- *Broken middle:* enter at 7 with the existing beat sheet; if none exists,
  reconstruct it from the draft before touching prose.
- *Flat characters:* enter at 3 with the draft in hand; audit sheets against
  the character reference's common faults, then re-plot the scenes where the
  flaw bites.
- *Dialogue repair:* enter at 9 with the scenes listed; run the fault list
  and voice test scene by scene.
- *Post-feedback revision:* enter at 11 with the reader notes; exit at 12.

**Working rhythm.** The full arc runs in four working sessions:

- Session one: steps 1-4, ending in a cast that can carry the premise.
- Session two: steps 5-8, ending in a beat sheet and scene cards you would
  trust enough to draft from.
- Session three: step 9, the drafting sprint; cards may flex, and the
  flexes are written back into the outline the same day.
- Session four: steps 10-12, the passes, the triage, and the plan.

The rhythm assumes short fiction; a novel repeats sessions two through four
per act, with the beat sheet re-verified at each act boundary. What does not
compress is the ordering: structure before scene, scene before line, cooling
before judgment.

## 05 — Rules and Quality Bar

1. **Every beat shifts a value the reader cares about.** A beat where
   nothing changes is a pause, and pauses must be earned deliberately, not
   defaulted into. If you cannot name the before/after state, the beat is
   not designed yet.
2. **The character's want drives the plot; the wound explains the want.** A
   sheet with a tragic backstory that never constrains choices is
   decoration. The test: does the wound make the protagonist's flawed
   strategy the *reasonable* choice to them?
3. **Structure is chosen, recorded, and serves the story.** Name the model,
   record why, and record what was rejected. An unexamined default shows in
   the middle act; a recorded choice can be revisited knowingly.
4. **The iceberg holds.** The reader receives the tenth of the world that
   presses on the scene. Worldbuilding that must be explained to be enjoyed
   is a wiki, not a story — convey through pressure and cost, not lecture.
5. **Dialogue is compressed, pointed, and voice-differentiated.** Apply the
   three functions (reveal character, advance conflict, deliver information)
   and cut lines that do only the third. Two characters must never pass the
   voice-attribution test by accident.
6. **Exposition rides on conflict or is cut.** Information the reader needs
   arrives at the last responsible moment, inside a scene with tension; if
   a paragraph exists to inform, it is a candidate for deletion.
7. **Motifs accumulate; they do not repeat.** Each recurrence of a motif
   must carry more meaning or a different shade than the last. Three
   appearances, escalating, is the working minimum; a motif without a
   climax placement is unregistered.
8. **Theme is an argument, not a topic.** "Grief" is a topic; "grief
   unshared curdles into duty" is an argument the story can dramatize on
   both sides. If the theme cannot be disputed, it cannot be dramatized.
9. **Revise in passes, structure first.** No line edit before the beat
   structure is sound; no beat change after the line pass without a new
   line pass. The pass order in the revision reference is the law.
10. **Cut with a reason and a date.** Cut scenes move to a dated appendix
    with one line of why; unexplained deletions get re-litigated by their
    author at 2 a.m. and reinstated badly.
11. **Feedback is triaged, not obeyed.** Sort reactions into must/should/
    taste; solve the need under the complaint, not the complaint itself.
    One reader's "too slow" and another's "rushed" often describe the same
    unearned transition.
12. **POV and tense are decided once and violated never.** Head-hopping and
    tense drift are defects, not style — unless designed as a documented
    system from the start.
13. **Length budgets are real.** Beat budgets sum to the target length; a
    draft that runs 20% over its budget has a structural cause, and the cut
    list addresses causes, not symptoms.
14. **Worldbuilding failures are logged, not remembered.** Every consistency
    fix goes into the world notes with a date, or chapter three will
    contradict the rule fixed in chapter eleven.
15. **The completion bar is written.** "Done" is defined before the final
    pass: motif audit clean, consistency checklist clean, one full cooling
    period elapsed, feedback triaged. Without the bar, revision never ends.
16. **Hand off at the boundary, with the artifact.** Level designers receive
    the beats a space must carry; game designers receive the story pillars
    and dialogue direction; copywriters receive the voice sheet. Handoffs
    name open questions; they do not silently absorb the neighbor's job.

## 06 — Worked Example

Scenario: **"The Last Signal"**, a 3,000-word science-fiction short story.
Premise: a deep-space relay operator on the last crewed station receives a
transmission from a colony ship that launched decades ago and lost — and must
decide what to tell it. The full run below is expanded artifact-by-artifact in
`examples/worked-story-design.md`; this section shows the intermediate
artifacts and the reasoning between them.

**Steps 1-2 artifacts (premise and structure choice).** The premise line is
written and the dramatic question named: *will Kaia answer, and what will she
say?* Three structures were considered from the structure reference's
decision matrix: plain three-act (rejected — the story's engine is a single
decision, not a rising campaign), kishōtenketsu (considered seriously — the
twist that the ship's destination never existed is a genuine ki-shō-ten
pivote), and a modified hero's journey hybrid (chosen: the call, the refusal
by hesitation, and the abyss map well onto a story about duty and farewell,
while the second half runs three-act escalation). The decision record states
the trade-off accepted: journey framing gives the ending its weight but
risks ceremonial padding in beat one, to be watched at revision.

**Steps 3-4 artifacts (character sheets).** Kaia Duvall, 34, deep-space
relay operator. Extrinsic want: finish her final rotation with a clean log.
Intrinsic need: to have been present for something that mattered — to not
have missed a last signal. The wound: at twelve she received her dying
mother's ordinary good-night transmission two hours before the accident —
final contact that carried no farewell — and built an identity around
monitoring without engaging. The contradiction: a meticulous professional
who will break protocol precisely when protocol would abandon someone. Arc
type: transformation from passive to active. The supporting cast is
deliberately thin (a single voice on the ship's recording system), accepted
as a short-story constraint and flagged in self-critique: a novel version
would require a second on-stage character to run the voice-differentiation
test against.

**Steps 5-6 artifacts (world and motif).** Worldbuilding stays inside the
iceberg: the relay station's decayed automation, the dead colony plan (the
Hub that was never built), and the ship's century-long automated cruise —
each surfaced only as it presses on the scene. Costs are explicit: sending
an unauthorized transmission is a career-ending breach, which is what makes
the choice a choice. The thematic argument: *a lie can be the only honest
farewell.* Motifs registered: "signal/silence" (primary — appears at the
duty log, the reply, the denouement), "cold" (secondary, capped at two
placements), and "hands" (the body acting before permission — once, at the
climax only).

**Steps 7-8 artifacts (beat sheet and scene cards).** Nine beats, 3,900
words budgeted, 3,000 targeted after compression: ordinary duty (400) →
anomalous signal (600) → first decode (500) → the unauthorized reply (500)
→ the ship's answer reveals the dead destination (500) → the decision among
truth, false route, or silence (400) → the cargo manifest broadcast — trees,
soil, a future with no port (300) → the lie: Kaia sends safe-passage
coordinates (400) → denouement, empty report, permanent echo (300). Every
beat names its value shift (calm→unease, hope→crushing knowledge, and so
on). Scene cards for the three turning points (beats 4, 6, 8) fix goals,
turns, and exit states; the beat-6 card is where self-critique later found
the decision resolving too fast, and the revision plan slows it by one
paragraph.

**Steps 9-11 artifacts (draft, passes, triage).** The draft lands at 3,200
words. The structure pass finds beats 1 and 4 overlapping and marks the sag;
the scene pass cuts redundant technical exposition of the ship's routing
protocol (the reader needs "it is navigating a map of a future that never
arrived," not the protocol standard); the line pass fixes the triple
"not-" construction to two placements. Beta triage on two readers: the
"all is lost" beat confused one reader (must-fix: render it as what Kaia
physically does, not as metaphor); "more about the colony" from the other
reader is triaged as taste — the iceberg is the point, and that note is
declined in writing in the triage table.

**Step 12 artifact (revision plan, abridged).** Six items, priority-ordered:
merge the overlapping beats to kill the sag; replace ship-protocol
exposition with the map-of-a-dead-future shorthand; change the ship's log
to loop the same cheerful message twelve times (tragedy without new
exposition); cut the denouement's lockdown sentence — start with boarding
the shuttle; verify motif trajectories one final time; render "all is lost"
as physical action. Completion bar: motif audit clean, both readers re-read
without confusion, one cooling week elapsed.

**What the example demonstrates.** The structure was chosen against a stated
alternative (kishōtenketsu), not defaulted. The wound is load-bearing: it
explains exactly why a protocol-perfect operator breaks protocol. Motif
counts were budgeted at outline time and enforced at revision time. Feedback
was triaged into need versus taste, and one reader note was *not* obeyed.
Each artifact exists because a later step needed it.

**Adapting the example to another story.** The transferable skeleton, with
"The Last Signal" specifics stripped:

- A single-decision premise wants a structure whose *abyss* is the decision
  itself; multi-campaign premises want three-act or journey ladders. Choose
  by where the irreversible act sits.
- Give the protagonist a wound that makes the wrong choice *reasonable to
  them*; the story's moral weight lives in that reasonableness.
- Budget motifs at outline time — primary with three escalating placements,
  secondaries capped — so line editing enforces a plan instead of guessing.
- The revision plan's first item is almost always a structural merge or cut,
  not a sentence. If your first fix is a sentence, you have not finished the
  structure pass.

## 07 — Failure Modes and Recovery

1. **The sagging middle.** Signal: beats 4-7 all "develop" without
   escalating; readers say it drags; the writer feels it too. Correction:
   return to the beat sheet (workflow step 7), find the beats that shift no
   value, merge or cut them, and re-check that each remaining beat raises
   stakes. Prevention: verify value shifts at outline time, before drafting.
2. **The function, not the character.** Signal: the protagonist does what
   the plot needs with no reason a stranger would accept; the sheet says
   "brave" and the draft says "convenient." Correction: rebuild the sheet
   from the wound up (step 3) until the flawed choice is the reasonable
   choice, then re-plot the scenes the flaw touches.
3. **The world that ate the story.** Signal: chapters of history before the
   plot moves; the writer defends every detail as "important later."
   Correction: apply the iceberg rule — move the inventory to world notes,
   keep only what presses on this scene, and convey through cost and
   pressure. Prevention: the step-5 rule that world entries must state
   their cost and their scene.
4. **Explaining dialogue.** Signal: characters tell each other things both
   already know; voices blur; beta readers say "stilted." Correction: run
   the dialogue reference's fault list — cut the information the listener
   has, move exposition into conflict, differentiate voices by the
   three-layer test, and rewrite as subtext.
5. **Motif wallpaper.** Signal: the image appears everywhere, meaning
   nothing; or it appears once and is invisible. Correction: audit
   placements against the theme reference's repetition mechanics — three
   escalating appearances minimum for the primary motif, cap the secondary,
   cut the orphaned ones. Prevention: register motifs on the beat sheet at
   step 6, not at line-edit time.
6. **Revision as stain.** Signal: the writer has "revised" eleven times,
   polishing sentences inside a broken structure; nothing improves.
   Correction: stop line work; re-run the structure pass from the beat
   sheet; only when beats hold do lines get touched, and the pass order in
   the revision reference governs from there.
7. **Obeying the loudest reader.** Signal: a single beta note triggers a
   rewrite; contradictory notes from two readers both get "fixed."
   Correction: re-triage all feedback together (step 11); find the shared
   need under conflicting complaints; reject taste with a one-line reason
   recorded in the triage table.
8. **The revision that never ends.** Signal: the draft is "nearly done" for
   months; every read finds one more tweak. Correction: the completion bar
   from step 12 — motif audit, consistency checklist, cooling period,
   triage closed — and when it is met, the piece is done even if it is not
   perfect. Perfection is not a completion condition; the bar is.

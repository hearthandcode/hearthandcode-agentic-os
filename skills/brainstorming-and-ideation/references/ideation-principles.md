# Ideation Principles — Methodology

## The Three Pillars

Ideation rests on three principles that are individually easy to understand and collectively hard to practice. Every technique in this skill package derives from one or more of them.

### 1. Quantity Breeds Quality

The first hundred ideas are the ones everyone has thought of. The second hundred are where novelty lives. A pool of 100 ideas contains more genuinely novel solutions than a pool of 10 — not because most of the 100 are good, but because the volume forces the mind past the obvious.

This is not a metaphor. Controlled studies show that the single strongest predictor of a session producing a breakthrough is total idea count, not the quality of any individual early idea. Osborn's original 1953 formulation put it directly: "Quantity is the most likely path to quality." The mechanism is combinatorial — large pools produce rare intersections that smaller pools miss.

**Practical implication:** Never stop at 10 ideas. Set a hard quota (30, 50, even 100) before you start. Enforce it. The last third of any quota is where the original work lives.

### 2. Deferral of Judgment

Judgment is the enemy of generation. The brain cannot both create and evaluate at full power — the two modes interfere. When you critique an idea while generating it, you stop the generative path and switch to an analytical one. The next idea will be safer, narrower, and less likely to break new ground.

**The practical rule is binary:** during divergence, no critique — not from yourself and not from others. During convergence, no new ideation. The two phases must be separated by a clear boundary, ideally one they cannot cross (different sessions, different days, or at minimum a mandatory 15-minute break).

What constitutes critique: any sentence that starts with "but," "that won't work because," "we tried that," "the client will hate," "that's too expensive," "that doesn't fit our brand," "that's weird." All of these must be deferred to the convergence phase. Write them down in a parking lot for later review — do not debate them in the moment.

**Deferral techniques in practice:**
- Use a "parking lot" sheet where objections go without discussion.
- The facilitator's sole role during divergence is to enforce deferral.
- Self-censorship is harder than external censorship. A small-group dry-run before the main session helps surface internal inhibitors.

### 3. Ideation Needs Limits

Unbounded space produces fewer ideas, not more. The number 12 is easier to divide by 3 and 4 than by 7 and 11, yet people do not find it freer; they find it easier to work with. Similarly, an ideation with no frame produces anxious wandering. A tight frame produces focused generation.

**How to set limits that work:**
- Time: 15 minutes per technique, never more than 90 minutes total.
- Quantity: exactly 20 ideas per technique block, no fewer.
- Constraint: "What would we do if we had only half the budget?" or "What would we do if we had to launch in two weeks?"
- Restriction: "No ideas that use video," "No ideas that require a new hire," "Every idea must use what we already have."

Limits are not the problem a solution overcomes — they are the thing that creates the solution. Ideation without limits produces recombinations of familiar ideas.

## The Two-Phase Process

Every ideation session alternates between two modes that must stay strictly separated:

### Divergence

Generate broadly. No evaluation. Quotas before technique. Write everything. The goal is volume, not quality. Each divergence technique is a different lens that forces the brain to see something it would not see naturally. The pattern catalog is the method.

### Convergence

Evaluate responsibly. Narrow to a shortlist. The convergence techniques bring judgment back with structure so the best ideas survive rather than the loudest. The rubric is the method.

## The 80% Rule

An ideation session is successful if 80% of the ideas generated are not used. If you use most of them, you did not push far enough. If you discard all of them, you learned something about your problem frame. The goal is not to produce a perfect idea — it is to produce a small set of realistic candidates, different enough from each other that you can choose rather than settle.

## When Principles Conflict

The volume principle can conflict with the evaluation principle: a 200-idea pool is harder to evaluate than a 30-idea pool. The fix is hierarchical: generate 30 ideas against each of the evaluation criteria early sorting, not depth evaluation — use dot-voting or quick sorting, and only the final 5-8 ideas get full rubrics. The volume is upstream of evaluation; it should not affect evaluation cost to receive benefits.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Divergence | The phase of generating many ideas without evaluation |
| Convergence | The phase of evaluating and narrowing ideas |
| Parking lot | A list of concerns, objections, and deferred questions from divergence that will be addressed during convergence |
| Idea quota | A minimum number of ideas a round must produce (enforces quantity breeds quality) |
| Deferral | The practice of setting the lens to postpone judgment until the convergence phase |
| Frame | The boundaries (time, budget, constraints, target audience) that provide focus |
| Threshold quality | The minimum idea viability level to enter the shortlist; defined before convergence begins |

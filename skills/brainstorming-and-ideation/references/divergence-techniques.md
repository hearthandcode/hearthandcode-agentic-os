# Divergence Techniques — Pattern Catalog

Each technique forces a different cognitive pathway. No single technique captures all the novelty a problem needs. Use 2-4 per session, 10-15 minutes each.

## 1. SCAMPER

SCAMPER is a mnemonic for seven modification moves to apply against a baseline concept. It is best used when you have one concrete idea or product to modify.

### The moves:

**Substitute:** Replace one component with something else. "What if we replaced the instructor with a simulation?" "What if we replace text with audio?"

**Combine:** Merge with another concept, product, or discipline. "What if this podcast combines mayor tours with field recording?"

**Adapt:** Borrow from another domain. "What if job interviews used first principles interviews, like design sprints?" "What if this feature uses restaurant menu adaptation?"

**Modify:** Change a dimension — size, scale, duration, audience. "What if episodes are 5 minutes of product highlights instead of 45?"

**Put to another use:** The thing as-is can solve a different problem. "What if the podcast listeners is actually a potential audience for our consulting service?"

**Eliminate:** Remove something considered essential. "What if there is no voiceover? What if it is just ambient sound and subtitles?"

**Reverse / Rearrange:** Flip the order of the process. "What if listeners choose the topics? What if each session solves a customer problem first, then uses the podcast as an extension?"

**How to run:** Pick the baseline concept. For each of the seven moves, generate exactly 5 ideas before the move. Write in short-form bullet. The constraint of 5 per move ensures you push past the obvious.

## 2. Forced Analogy

Take something completely unrelated from the problem domain and try to apply its principles to your problem.

**Mechanics:**
1. Pick a random domain (kitchen, aviation, forest ecosystem, car repair, anatomy, sports game, music orchestra).
2. For each key feature of that domain, map it to your problem.
3. The "forced" part is key — do not cherry-pick. Use the whole domain.

**Example:** "How would a forest ecosystem approach a podcast format?"
- Canopy catch light — "most important content is repeated, layers of the content"
- Mycorrhizal network — "cross-pollination between episodes, references work across episodes"
- Disturbance (fire, logging) — "a timeline disruption — how does the format react when a topic becomes obsolete and must be replaced?"
- Succession — "episodic arcs that evolve from simpler to more complex, with each season building on the previous"

** When to use:** When the group is stuck on same-domain analogies. In a single session, the first thousand rounds produce the same ten patterns.

## 3. Worst Idea First

The brain's number one barrier to creativity is a biased framing: good. By getting the worst possible ideas down first, you accomplish two things: detoxify scarcity, and mark the worsts of the spectrum line so there is room for other ideas.

**How to run:**
1. Frame: "For the next 5 minutes, generate only terrible ideas. Place them with quantity and enthusiasm."
2. Capture them all.
3. After the terrible slot, flip every terrible idea into its opposite — the positive advice of a truly bad concept often reveals a truly strong one.
4. A "worst idea" is not an idea that simply won't work — it is an idea that offends, is absurd, violates a governance rule, is dangerous, or would make the product worse.

**Example flips:**
- Terrible: "Do every episode as a live interactive session where the audience decides the direction of the conversation in real time." → Flip: "What if some episodes had a real-time component — live sessions with audience Q&A?"
- Terrible: "Make a podcast where each episode is only available to a single listener. Exclusive." → Flip: "What about building a listener-made-of-one episode — a personalized summary — in the regular feed?"

## 4. Constraint Flips

Take a common assumption or constraint of your problem and invert it or remove it. The new set of constraints forces the brain to create different solutions.

**How to run:**
1. List 5-7 constraints of your current problem/approach.
2. For each constraint, invert it (opposite), remove it, or exaggerate it (10x or 0.1x).
3. Generate ideas under the new constraint.

**Example (podcast constraints turned in):**

| Current constraint | Flip |
|--------------------|------|
| Episodes have one topic per episode | Invert: Each episode has three unrelated segments that the host must connect before the end |
| Listener finds the episode | Reverse: The podcast finds the listener — listener intros and suggests topics based on listener questions |
| Audio only | Invert: Audio + field guide PDF, contextual video loops |
| Weekly release | Invert: Daily micro-episodes (2-5 minutes) |
| Single host format | Invert: 3 hosts representing competing perspectives (naturalist, developer, split) |

## 5. Brainwriting (635 Method)

Rather than one person generating ideas at a time, everyone writes ideas simultaneously. Prevents active domination by the loudest voice in the room. Works for teams of 3-8.

**Procedure:**
1. Every person provides a paper (or digital doc) with three columns.
2. At the end of 5 minutes, each person produces 3 ideas for the first time.
3. Pass the page to the right.
4. In the next 5 minutes, each person reads the ideas on the page they just received and adds 3 new ideas, with the received ideas as scaffolding.
5. Repeat 6 times (30 minutes total, 18 ideas per person).
6. Collect all sheets.

**Solo adaptation:** Same procedure, but the solo practitioner runs 3 sub-sessions: write 5 ideas in 5 minutes, then step away for 2 minutes, come back and look at them as if someone else wrote them, and add 5 more that are "in response."

## 6. Random Entry

Introduce a random word, image, or concept. Connect it to the problem in as many possible ways as possible.

**How to run:**
1. Choose or generate a random element (a dictionary word, a stock photo, a Twitter meme, a philosophical term).
2. Write down up to 5 connections that could be solutions.
3. Do this for the 3-4 random elements.

**Example:** Random word = "kompas"
- "The podcast is like a compass — it always points to identifying what you can see."
- "A compass is a companion for navigation. The podcast is a companion for encountering the city."
- "A compass has only one direction.
- "A compass breaks notes (maps, lists, resources) that follow the compass subject."

## 7. Analogous Worlds

Similar to forced analogy, but the target is especially distant — not "nature" or "music" but "winter."

**How to apply:**
1. Pick a very specific domain (the ISS is a space, airport runway operations, an infrastructure team, bread and pastry).
2. Extract its principles.
3. Map to your problem.

**Example using "Airport" for "Generating weekly community newsletter topics":**
- **Check-in:** Always a landing zone or brief check — each newsletter starts with the same ask: "What do you want to see more of?" (like the check-in asks orientation for destination).
- **Terminals (categories):** Each terminal is a category: field guide, community report, erosion report, events.
- **Gates (final step):** Each terminal has one or more final steps — final editing, sending.
- **Boarding pass:** Each subscriber has a testable concept.

## Technique Selection

| Situation | Recommended techniques |
|------------|-----------------------|
| You have one concept to improve | SCAMPER |
| You want to break the known pattern | Forced Analogy, constraint flips |
| Productivity is low (blocked/fearful) | Worst-idea first |
| You know the problem but not the domain | Brainwriting, analogous transfer |
| You don't know where to start at all | Brainwriting, random entry |
| The team has a fixed dynamic (hierarchy/personality) | Silent brainwriting, not verbal |

## Technique Execution Guidelines

- Each technique should produce 12-20 ideas in its time slot.
- Capture the literal — no summarizing or "cleaning up" during divergence.
- Use a single format (index cards, digital board, shared document) that is visible to all participants throughout.
- Do not combine techniques mid-slot. Each technique is a distinct cognitive lens.
- After the slot, summarize: "We have X new at the board." "X are direct derivatives." "X are actual novel directions." This is the only note gathering; no evaluative content.

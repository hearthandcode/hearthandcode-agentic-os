# social-media-strategy

## 01 — Purpose

This skill turns "we should be on social media" into an operating plan a solo operator or small team can actually run — the decision layer that gets built before anyone writes a single post.
It exists because most presences fail structurally, not creatively: five platforms at half effort, promotion crowding out conversation, and no pre-decided answer to the first real incident.
The skill builds the system first — platform roles, audience segments, pillars, ratios, engagement rules, sensors, crisis posture — so every post has a job and every week has a shape.
What it covers, end to end:
- Platform selection by role, audience fit, format cost, and content durability — not by habit or fear of missing out.
- Audience segmentation into a few named groups you can genuinely serve, including the one you choose to disappoint.
- Content pillar design with explicit ratio math that survives contact with a real calendar.
- Engagement rules and personal boundaries that protect operator time, energy, and goodwill.
- Organic growth mechanics, collaboration patterns, and guardrails against dishonest shortcuts.
- A minimal listening setup that converts community signal into product and content decisions.
- A crisis-response one-pager written before the first incident, not during it.
The craft position: a presence is a system with inputs (what you publish), loops (how you converse), and sensors (what you hear).
Treat content as inputs with intended effects, replies as loop maintenance, and listening findings as sensor data — not as three unrelated activities staffed by the same exhausted person.
The skill prices every commitment in hours, because a strategy that ignores the operator's real weekly budget is fiction that collapses in week two.
It assumes durability over hacks: the mechanics here are platform-agnostic and stable — conversations compound, proof-of-work earns trust, silence has a cost — rather than tricks tied to any current feed algorithm, which change without notice and reward nobody who chases them for long.
The intended reader is a competent practitioner — an indie developer, solo founder, artist, or small-team operator — new to running a public presence, or running one badly and honest enough to admit it.
Nothing here requires paid tooling, an agency, or prior marketing experience: the economics are tuned for one person with a product, a launch date, and a few hours a week.
Everything executes with free platform features, a text editor, and the discipline to write decisions down.
The skill also runs in audit mode: when a presence already exists, the workflow first names the decisions that are operating implicitly, tests each against the quality bar, and rewrites only what fails — audits are cheaper than rebuilds and preserve the equity an existing account has earned.
Three doctrines run through every step. Decisions over intentions: every output section states what will happen, not what would be nice. Cost before creativity: no commitment enters the plan without an hour price attached. Conversation before broadcast: the reply budget is protected before the posting cadence is.
A fourth doctrine, quieter but equally binding: honesty scales badly — the plan assumes every post could be screenshotted, every promise will be remembered, and every shortcut will be found out, and designs a presence that survives all three.
A finished run leaves artifacts, not vibes — the platform table, segment cards, pillar table with arithmetic, rulebook, arc, listening config, and crisis one-pager all exist as checkable objects in the document. Section 04 names where each artifact is produced; section 08 indexes every file involved.
The skill assumes no pre-existing audience and no paid promotion: both help, neither is required, and the workflow treats paid spend as out of scope (that is a `marketing-strategy` decision; this skill governs the organic layer only).
If a presence already exists, run the skill as an audit: name the implied pillars, price the real hours, and rewrite the decisions that fail the checks in section 05.
Outcomes this skill owns:
1. A complete written social strategy document, assembled from `templates/social-strategy-template.md`, naming platform roles, the effort split, audience segments, pillars with ratio math, engagement rules, the content arc, the listening setup, and the crisis posture.
2. A pre-launch or launch-window content arc (8 weeks by default) whose pillar ratios are arithmetically checked against a stated posting cadence, ready to hand to `content-calendar-planning` for scheduling.
3. A crisis-response one-pager plus a minimal listening setup, so the operator's first incident and first hundred pieces of feedback land on prepared ground instead of adrenaline.
Each outcome is verifiable: open the document, find the artifact, check its numbers — a run that cannot show all three has not finished, however good the conversation felt.
Treat this as the entry briefing: before the workflow runs, the user should understand that a strategy document is a set of testable claims — platform X earns its hours because of role Y, pillar Z exists because segment W wants it — and every claim names its evidence.
What this skill does not do: write posts, pick dates, set prices, or manage accounts — it produces the decision layer that makes those downstream tasks fast and coherent.
The quality bar for the output: a strategy document someone else could run in your absence, with no decision left inside your head.
If the finished document cannot pass that test, it is a mood board, not a strategy — go back to section 04 and convert intentions into decisions.
Working definition, stated once and used everywhere: a social strategy is the written set of decisions about where you show up, who you serve, what you publish, how you converse, what you listen for, and what you do when something goes wrong — everything else is content, and content is downstream.
Readers arriving from the sibling skills should note the boundary: this skill ends where a publishing calendar begins, and it assumes `marketing-strategy` has already answered who the customer is and why they buy.

## 02 — When to Use / When Not to Use

This section is the routing layer: it exists so the right questions land here and the wrong ones get sent somewhere better, quickly and without ceremony.
Load this skill when any of the following is true:
**1. A launch is approaching and there is no presence yet.** Pre-launch is the highest-leverage moment: the 8-12 weeks before release decide whether launch day lands in front of anyone or into silence.
- Signals: no accounts, or accounts with a handful of posts and no arc; a launch date exists but nothing ties content to it.
- The skill produces platform roles, an effort split, pillars, and an 8-week pre-launch arc.
- Governing references: `references/strategy-case-studies.md` shows a full 12-month build; `references/content-pillars.md` governs the arc and ratio math.
- Checkpoint: ask what the launch date is and how many hours per week realistically exist.
**2. An existing presence feels random.** Accounts exist, posting is inconsistent, and nobody can answer "what is this account for?"
- Signals: long gaps, apology posts ("sorry for going quiet"), whatever-came-to-mind content, a stalled follower curve.
- The skill runs as an audit: name the implied pillars, set ratios, cut or promote platforms by role.
- Governing references: `references/content-pillars.md` for pillar and ratio design; `references/audience-and-community.md` for deciding who the account serves.
- Checkpoint: ask the user to describe their last ten posts; the pattern (or absence of one) becomes the audit's raw material.
**3. The user asks which platforms to be on.** Platform choice is a role-and-economics decision, not a taste decision.
- Signals: "should I be on TikTok?", "everyone says you need a newsletter", spreading thin across five networks.
- The skill scores candidate platforms on audience fit, format cost, and content durability, then assigns each kept platform one role and an hour cost.
- Governing reference: `references/platform-landscape.md`.
- Checkpoint: ask how many hours per week the user will genuinely commit, and what would make them drop a platform.
**4. Engagement is becoming a burden or a risk.** Replies eat the calendar, DMs blur into unpaid support, or a controversy elsewhere has the user anxious about their own exposure.
- Signals: dread before opening notifications, hours lost to bad-faith threads, no policy for criticism, gifts, or collaborations.
- The skill produces an engagement rulebook — reply priorities, boundaries, escalation paths — and a crisis one-pager.
- Governing references: `references/engagement-strategy.md` and `references/crisis-management.md`.
- Checkpoint: ask what engagement behavior would make the user quit the platform entirely; that line is the boundary to encode.
**5. Growth has plateaued and the user wants honest mechanics.** The account is fine but flat, and the user is tempted by follow-for-follow threads or engagement pods.
- Signals: a flat follower curve over months, content that is good but unseen, temptation toward purchased or reciprocal growth.
- The skill designs organic mechanics: collaborations, cross-platform loops, series with appointment value, community rituals.
- Governing reference: `references/growth-and-reach.md`.
- Checkpoint: ask what the user can offer a peer — collaborations start with an offer, not an ask.
**6. Community signal should be feeding the product.** The same questions, requests, and complaints keep appearing, and nothing systematic captures them.
- Signals: repeated questions in replies, feature requests scattered across platforms, sentiment surprises ("people loved the thing we almost cut").
- The skill builds a minimal listening setup: keywords, cadence, triage buckets, and a route into product and content decisions.
- Governing reference: `references/social-listening.md`.
- Checkpoint: ask where feedback currently goes when someone mentions the product — the answer is usually "nowhere," which is the finding.
**7. The user is about to make a public commitment they cannot keep.** A kickstarter promise, a dated roadmap, or an ambitious cross-posting plan is being drafted right now, and the presence will have to live with it.
- Signals: promises accumulating faster than hours ("weekly videos, daily tweets, monthly newsletters"), no written capacity plan anywhere.
- The skill prices the promise in hours, re-shapes it into cadences the budget survives, and writes the fallback behavior down before anyone is watching.
- Governing reference: `references/platform-landscape.md` for format economics; `references/content-pillars.md` for sustainable cadence.
- Checkpoint: ask the user to halve the promise and say what breaks — the answer reveals which half was load-bearing.
Every trigger above shares one property: the user is asking a question about the system (where, who, what shape, what rules), not about a single artifact. When the question is about one post, route out; when it is about the machine that makes posts, route here.
When two triggers both match, take the later workflow step: fixing an active engagement problem (trigger 4) while a platform decision is also pending (trigger 3) means run steps 1-3 quickly, then land on step 7 — but never run both tracks half-done in parallel.
Usage pattern for agents: match the user's opening question to a trigger, name the trigger out loud ("this is a platform-choice question"), and start the workflow at the step that trigger's checkpoint implies — triggers 1-3 start at step 1, trigger 4 at step 7, triggers 5-6 at steps 8 and 10 respectively, and trigger 7 starts at step 3 because an over-built promise is an hour-budget problem first.
Do not load this skill for:
1. Writing or polishing individual posts. If the ask is "make this post better," the craft is copy, not strategy — use `copywriting-and-messaging`, and treat this skill's voice rules as context only.
2. Building a dated, day-by-day posting calendar. This skill produces the arc and the ratios; turning them into dated slots with dependencies is `content-calendar-planning`.
3. Full go-to-market planning. Pricing, paid acquisition, channel mix beyond social, and competitive positioning are broader — use `marketing-strategy` and import only its channel conclusions.
4. Business-model and revenue decisions. If the underlying question is "can this project support me," that is `business-planning`; social strategy is one input to that, not the decision itself.
5. Designing the product or game itself. Questions about mechanics, scope, or difficulty belong to `game-mechanics-design`; listening surfaces those questions but does not answer them.
6. A single reply to a single comment. No skill is needed — answer helpfully. If the same comment keeps recurring, it has become a listening finding, not a writing task.
7. Managing an existing community space (moderation queues, ban decisions, event scheduling). That is community operations, not strategy; this skill sets the rules such work follows but does not perform it — no sibling skill covers it, and a spreadsheet plus consistency covers most of it.
Borderline rule: "should / which / how much" questions about social presence are strategy (this skill); "write / draft / polish" requests about specific posts are execution (another skill, or none).
Second borderline rule: a request to "go viral" or "grow fast" is still this skill, but the honesty rules in section 05 apply — if the user wants purchased growth or engagement pods, decline that part and say why.
If the user arrives mid-crisis (something is blowing up right now), skip to step 11 of the workflow and `references/crisis-management.md` first; strategy revision waits until the incident is contained.
This skill composes well: run it before `content-calendar-planning` (it sets the ratios the calendar must respect) and alongside `marketing-strategy` (it inherits positioning; it does not create it).
A note on sequencing for returning users: the second run through this skill is usually an audit, not a rebuild. Keep the platform roles that still earn their hours, re-score only what drifted, and treat every existing account decision as data about what the operator will actually sustain — history is the best predictor of cadence.
Another note on scale: everything here is written for one operator with a small hour budget, and that is deliberate. Teams can run the same workflow with assigned owners per platform, but the rules, ratios, and boundaries still need one named person who answers for them — a presence owned by committee decays into a content feed with no voice.
When the user's product is not a game (a service, a consultancy, an art practice), nothing structural changes: the pitch, segments, pillars, and ratios still work; only the example vocabulary shifts — screenshots become case studies, playtesters become pilot customers, and patch notes become change logs.

## 03 — Inputs and Outputs

**Required inputs** (gather these before running the workflow; if any are unknown, the early steps of section 04 will surface them, but the user should not be surprised by the questions)
- The thing being promoted: a product, game, service, or public identity, with a one-sentence pitch a stranger could repeat.
- The launch state: pre-launch with a date, launched and growing, or long-tail maintenance — each changes the arc.
- A real weekly hour budget for social, stated in hours, not vibes; the whole plan is costed against this number.
- Audience knowledge: who already cares (players, peers, press, customers), and where those people already gather online.
- Current presence: existing accounts with rough follower counts and posting history, or a clean slate.
- Constraints: platform aversions, NDAs or embargo dates, bandwidth limits, and anything the user will not post about.
- Risk surface: two or three things that could go wrong publicly — a bug, a delay, a controversial design choice, past community friction.
- A success definition for the next 90 days in the user's own words; follower counts alone are not an acceptable answer.
- Missing-input rule: ask for these directly and record the answers verbatim in the document's inputs section — guessed budgets and audiences are the leading cause of strategies that fail in week two.
- Any nice-to-have that turns out to be load-bearing (for example, an embargo date hiding inside "constraints") gets promoted to required and the document says so.
Name any missing required input explicitly and ask for it before proceeding — an invented hour budget or guessed audience is the most common way this skill produces documents that fail in week two.
The workflow can survive missing nice-to-have inputs; it cannot survive missing required ones, because every later decision prices against them.
Required inputs are also the audit's anchors: when re-running this skill on an existing presence, the first move is re-confirming the hour budget and success definition, because those two drift fastest and everything else was priced against them.
Record the answers verbatim in the strategy document's inputs section, so future audits can check which assumptions held and which drifted.
When the user says "just pick for me," pick, but write down the assumption each pick depends on and flag it at the final checkpoint — defaults are allowed to be provisional, never invisible.
**Nice-to-have inputs (ask if missing, proceed without if refused)**
- Competitor or peer accounts the user admires or resents, and why.
- Any existing content library that could be re-cut: devlogs, screenshots, stream clips, essays.
- Historical engagement data — which past posts performed and why, if the user can tell.
- Access constraints: who else posts, whether anyone else must approve, shared-account logistics.
Nice-to-haves are asked for once, in a single batch, and then never re-litigated mid-workflow — the run must not stall on optional context the user has already declined to provide.
**Output — the strategy document**
The deliverable is one strategy document assembled on `templates/social-strategy-template.md`, plus the intermediate artifacts below. Every output is plain markdown that lives with the project.
- Platform role table: each kept platform with its role, posting cadence, and weekly hour cost; the hour costs must sum under the user's budget.
- Audience segment cards: two to four named segments, each with who they are, what they want, and what they will do when they see your content.
- Content pillar table: three to five pillars, each with a name, definition, example posts, and a percentage share; percentages sum to 100 and multiply out to real weekly counts.
- Ratio math: the arithmetic connecting pillar shares to actual weekly posts at the stated cadence — shown, not implied.
- Engagement rulebook: ten to fifteen numbered rules covering reply priorities, DM policy, criticism handling, and personal boundaries, each with a one-line reason.
- Content arc: six to twelve weeks (eight is the default), one theme per week, each week naming its pillar emphasis and one proof artifact.
- Listening setup: a keyword list, a check cadence, three to four triage buckets, and the route from findings to decisions.
- Crisis one-pager: severity tiers, first-hour actions, a holding-statement skeleton, and a do-not-do list.
- Decisions log: a dated list of every non-obvious call with its reason, kept inside the strategy document.
What the skill does not hand back: published posts, scheduled dates, follower counts, or a promise of outcomes — those belong to the calendar, the platforms, and time.
**Files this skill reads and writes**
- Reads, for method: all eight files under `references/` — `platform-landscape.md`, `audience-and-community.md`, `content-pillars.md`, `engagement-strategy.md`, `growth-and-reach.md`, `social-listening.md`, `crisis-management.md`, `strategy-case-studies.md`.
- Writes from: `templates/social-strategy-template.md` — the scaffold with instructional comments; the final strategy document is a filled copy of it.
- Calibration: `examples/worked-social-strategy.md` — a complete filled strategy document for the Voidbreak Games scenario; hold your output against it.
**Output shapes, precisely** (hold every artifact to these forms; shape failures are how strategy documents become unreadable half-document, half-chat-log hybrids)
- A platform role table row looks like: `Twitter/X | primary stage | 3 posts/week + daily replies | 2.5 h/week | launch runway and press`.
- A segment card looks like: `Wishlist-curious players — want to see the game feel alive; will buy when they see a moment of play, not a feature list`.
- A pillar row looks like: `Build-in-public (40%) — progress with numbers and process; example: "physics rope, three weeks of failures"`.
- An arc week looks like: `Week 3 — systems showcase (pillar 2 emphasis): physics deep-dive devlog, three clips, weekly reply sprint`.
If the user wants outputs as separate files rather than one document, that is fine — keep the template's section order so the pieces stay auditable against each other.
One more output convention: the strategy document should carry a short decisions log — a dated list of the non-obvious calls (why that platform was dropped, why press ranks last, why the buffer exists) — because six weeks from now the reasons will not be recoverable from the artifacts alone, and an audit that cannot see the reasons starts re-litigating settled questions.
The decisions log is also the cheapest handoff artifact: when the operator takes a break or a second person joins, the log is what lets them inherit judgment instead of guessing at it.
A final note on what "done" means for this section: the hand-back is complete when every artifact above exists in the document, each one satisfies its shape line, and the user has seen the arithmetic at least once — silent math is how strategies acquire numbers nobody trusts.

## 04 — Workflow

The workflow's logic is a funnel: goal first, audience second, platforms third — because platform choice without audience ranking is taste, and pillar design without platform cadence is wishful arithmetic.
Run these steps in order for a new strategy. For an audit of an existing presence, run steps 1-3 as a diagnosis of what exists, then amend decisions rather than inventing new ones.
Each step names the reference file that governs it — read that reference before executing the step, not after, because the reference carries the method and the quality checks.
Every step ends with a checkpoint question for the user. Ask it, get an answer, and record it; each checkpoint encodes a decision that belongs to the operator, not to you.
Timebox: a full run is two to three hours of agent work plus checkpoint time. If you arrive mid-crisis, jump to step 11 and run `references/crisis-management.md` first; strategy revision waits until the incident is contained.
You will need the section 03 inputs before step 1. If any are missing, ask now — do not invent an hour budget or an audience.

### Step 1 — Orient on goal, pitch, and constraints
**Action.** Fix the one-sentence pitch, a 90-day success definition, the launch state, and hard constraints before any platform discussion.
**Reference.** `references/audience-and-community.md` — the pitch test and the who-you-serve method.
**Do this.**
- Write the pitch in one sentence a stranger could repeat; if it needs two sentences, the product story is not ready and everything downstream wobbles.
- Test the pitch by saying it aloud once: if it lists features instead of naming a feeling or a promise, rewrite it — audiences repeat stories, not feature lists.
- Convert "success" into one observable 90-day outcome — wishlists, demo downloads, mailing-list signups, three real peer relationships; raw follower counts are rejected.
- Write the success definition as a number plus a date plus a mechanism ("200 demo wishlists by March 1, via the itch.io page"), because unmeasurable goals cannot steer the arc later.
- List hard constraints as rules: embargo dates, topics off-limits, platform aversions with reasons.
- Separate soft preferences from hard constraints — "I dislike short video" is a preference to weigh in step 3, not a rule; "we are under NDA until June" is a rule the arc must respect.
**Checkpoint.** "Say the pitch back to me in one sentence, and name what would make the next 90 days a success."
**If blocked.** If the user cannot answer, stop and interview; a guessed pitch poisons every later step.
**If partially answered.** Proceed with the answered parts, mark the rest as open in the inputs section, and re-ask them at the step 3 checkpoint — do not let silence calcify into a default nobody chose.

### Step 2 — Segment the audience into named groups
**Action.** Split everyone who might care into two to four named segments, each with a want and a likely action, and rank them.
**Reference.** `references/audience-and-community.md` — the segment card method and the audience-versus-community distinction.
**Do this.**
- Write one segment card per group: name, who they are, what they want from you, what they will do when they see your content.
- Test each card against reality: if you cannot name where this segment already gathers, or what they would actually do after seeing a post, it is a demographic guess, not a segment.
- Mark each segment as audience (you broadcast to them) or community (you belong among them); the two get different behavior.
- Rank the segments explicitly; the strategy names which one gets dropped when time runs short, so the cut is pre-decided rather than guilt-driven.
- Sanity-check the ranking against the success definition from step 1: the segment whose action drives the 90-day outcome usually ranks first, and if it does not, the strategy should say why in writing.
**Checkpoint.** "If you only had time to serve one segment this month, which one — and who would you knowingly disappoint?"
**Common trap.** Peers are easy to serve and easy to over-serve, because they are pleasant and they reply; players buy the product but stay silent. Rank by what moves the goal, not by who is nicest to talk to.

### Step 3 — Score platforms and assign roles and hours
**Action.** Build a shortlist of four to six candidate platforms, score each on audience fit, format cost, and content durability, keep two or three, and give each a role and an hour cost.
**Reference.** `references/platform-landscape.md` — the platform catalog, role taxonomy, and effort economics.
**Do this.**
- Score candidates in a table before deciding; the scores, not enthusiasm, justify keep and drop calls.
- Score against the ranked segments from step 2, not against the internet's general platform advice — a platform full of the wrong people scores 1 on audience fit no matter how big it is.
- Assign each kept platform exactly one primary role: primary stage, community home, storefront and archive, or watchlist.
- Price each kept platform in weekly hours including replies and community upkeep, not just production time — reply load is where hour estimates usually fail.
- Sum the weekly hour costs across kept platforms plus listening and buffer; the total must fit the user's budget or a platform gets cut now, not in week three.
- Write the drop reasons into the decisions log; "we can add it later" needs a dated trigger (e.g. "add Discord when the demo ships and playtesters need a home") or it becomes permanent drift.
**Checkpoint.** "Here is the keep/drop table and the hour split — does this match the hours you actually have on a bad week?"
**Arithmetic gate.** If the sum exceeds the budget, cut the lowest-scoring kept platform immediately and re-sum; never resolve a budget overrun by assuming future efficiency.

### Step 4 — Design the content pillars
**Action.** Draft three to five pillars, each a repeatable content territory with a name, a definition, and example posts; kill drafts that fail the tests.
**Reference.** `references/content-pillars.md` — pillar design, the four tests, naming.
**Do this.**
- Draft pillars from the intersection of what the product gives you proof of, what the segments want, and what you can sustain on a bad week.
- Draft at least two more pillars than you plan to keep, then cut — the discarded drafts are where overlap hides.
- Run the four tests on every draft: distinct (does not overlap another pillar), valuable (a segment wants it), sustainable (bad-week survivable), provable (you can show it, not just claim it).
- Rename any pillar whose name would not survive on a calendar: "marketing" and "misc" are not pillars, they are intentions with fonts.
- Write two or three example post titles per pillar; a pillar with no examples is a vibe, not a pillar.
**Checkpoint.** "Read the pillar list — which one would you still enjoy producing in week six of a crunch?"
**Cut rule.** If two pillars overlap on more than half their example posts, merge them and redistribute the share; four distinct pillars beat five blurry ones.
**Ordering note.** Run this step after platforms are priced (step 3), because a pillar's sustainable cadence depends on where it will be published — a long-form pillar is only sustainable where long-form belongs.

### Step 5 — Set the ratio math
**Action.** Give each pillar a percentage share summing to 100, multiply against the weekly cadence, and resolve rounding with an explicit rule.
**Reference.** `references/content-pillars.md` — ratio planning, the rounding procedure, and the ratio rubric.
**Do this.**
- Allocate shares by segment priority and production cost, not by what is easiest to make; ease-weighted ratios are how accounts become 90% screenshots.
- Multiply shares by weekly content units; use largest-remainder rounding and track any fractional pillar on a rolling four-week window.
- Write the rounding rule itself into the strategy, not just its result, so a later editor reproduces the counts instead of re-inventing them.
- Write the resulting per-week and per-month counts into the strategy; these numbers are what `content-calendar-planning` will schedule against.
- Cross-check the counts against the hour budget from step 3: if the weekly unit count implies more production hours than the platforms were priced for, the ratio is fantasy and must shrink.
**Checkpoint.** "Does the weekly post count still fit your hours once reply time is included?"
**Sanity test.** Divide the month's units by four and compare against the platform cadence; if the two disagree by more than one unit per week, fix the ratio, not the calendar.
**Worked micro-example.** Four units per week with shares 40/25/20/15 gives 1.6/1.0/0.8/0.6; floors are 1/1/0/0, and the two leftover units go to the largest remainders — write that sentence into the document, because the rule is the deliverable, not the numbers it produced.

### Step 6 — Design series and formats
**Action.** Convert pillars into one or two named recurring series per platform plus a format rotation, so production is a slot to fill rather than a blank page.
**Reference.** `references/platform-landscape.md` for format economics per platform; `references/content-pillars.md` for series thinking.
**Do this.**
- Name each series — a weekly systems teardown, a monthly progress devlog — and fix its slot, length, and template.
- Give each series a production recipe: inputs needed, steps, and target production time — a series without a recipe is a new project every week.
- Define the cheap default format for bad weeks: a screenshot with two honest sentences beats a missed week.
- Confirm every series maps to a pillar; a series with no pillar is drift waiting to happen.
- Set a retirement condition per series ("pause after six editions if median replies stay under three") so endings are planned rather than guilted.
**Checkpoint.** "Which series will you protect even in a bad week, and what is the cheap fallback format?"
**Naming tip.** A named series earns recognition and anticipation — "Lantern Log #7" tells the audience where to look next, which is appointment value no one-off post can buy.
**Volume cap.** No more than two named series per platform; beyond that, production recipes stop being followed and the series become a backlog of guilt rather than a schedule.

### Step 7 — Write engagement rules and boundaries
**Action.** Write ten to fifteen numbered engagement rules covering reply priorities, DM policy, criticism handling, and personal boundaries, each with a one-line reason.
**Reference.** `references/engagement-strategy.md` — reply craft, conversation design, boundaries, and the engagement checklist.
**Do this.**
- Set reply priorities: questions first, thoughtful critics second, peers third, drive-by praise last.
- Write the DM policy: who gets access, expected response times, and what never happens in DMs — pricing, support, sensitive topics.
- Encode criticism handling: one honest public reply, then escalate to a private channel or stop; never a thread war.
- Encode personal boundaries as rules with reasons: posting hours, no-go topics, block criteria, and the signals that mean "log off now."
- Add the reciprocity rule if absent: for every self-promotional post, boost or engage with someone else's work — feeds that only promote curdle.
**Checkpoint.** "Which of these rules will be hardest for you to keep — and should we make that one stricter, not looser?"
**Why stricter.** The rule you break is the one that was already too soft; tightening the tempting rule costs nothing today and saves the week it matters.
**Count check.** Ten rules is the floor, not a target: write the rules the operator's actual risks demand — a dev with a live build needs different rules from an artist with a commission queue.

### Step 8 — Plan organic growth mechanics
**Action.** Choose two or three growth mechanics — collaborations, cross-platform loops, community rituals — and define the operator's offer to peers.
**Reference.** `references/growth-and-reach.md` — organic mechanics, collaboration patterns, and honesty guardrails.
**Do this.**
- Pick mechanics that fit the hour budget; a collaboration pattern that costs a weekend is not a growth plan, it is a special event.
- Write the offer: what the operator gives a peer — playtesting, an asset, an audience, a skill — because collaborations start with offers, not asks.
- Define one community ritual if the platforms support it — a recurring feedback thread, a monthly playtest call — rituals give quiet followers a way in.
- Copy the banned-shortcuts list into the strategy: purchased followers, follow-back chains, engagement pods, and the reason each poisons signal.
**Checkpoint.** "What can you genuinely offer the first three peers you want to approach?"
**Honesty note.** If the only offers available are "I will be your friend," the growth plan is not ready — go build proof-of-work first, because peers collaborate with leverage, not enthusiasm.
**Sequencing note.** Schedule collaborations no earlier than the arc's midpoint; approaching peers before the feed has proof-of-work converts at a fraction of the rate and burns the introduction.

### Step 9 — Build the content arc
**Action.** Lay out six to twelve weeks (eight by default) with one theme per week, a pillar emphasis, and one proof artifact per week; map the arc to the launch date.
**Reference.** `references/content-pillars.md` for series and ratio constraints; `references/strategy-case-studies.md` for a full 12-month arc to calibrate against.
**Do this.**
- Work backwards from launch: the final weeks carry the strongest proof — demo, trailer beat, press kit — while early weeks build vocabulary and recognition.
- Reserve the heaviest production week against the operator's real calendar, not the ideal one; a launch week that also assumes a family holiday is a plan that will fail beautifully.
- Give every week exactly one flagship artifact; themes without artifacts are wishes.
- Check each week against the pillar ratios; a week that abandons the ratios for pure promotion needs a written reason in the decisions log.
- Leave one flex week unthemed near the end of the arc; it absorbs slippage, feedback surprises, or a necessary breath without rewriting the whole arc.
**Checkpoint.** "Look at the eight weeks — is there one artifact per week you would be proud to show, and does the heaviest week match your real calendar?"
**Arc test.** Read only the flagship artifacts back to back: if they alone do not tell a story from "what is this" to "this exists, here is proof, here is where to get it," the arc has a hole.

### Step 10 — Set up minimal listening
**Action.** Configure the smallest listening setup that works: a keyword list, a check cadence, triage buckets, and a route from findings into decisions.
**Reference.** `references/social-listening.md` — setup, keyword design, triage, and the feedback route.
**Do this.**
- Build the keyword list: product name, misspellings, handle, genre terms, and peer titles whose audiences overlap.
- Fix a cadence — fifteen to thirty minutes, once or twice a week — and put it on the calendar as an appointment, not an intention.
- Define triage buckets — praise, questions, bugs, feature asks, drama — and name where each bucket goes: content ideas, known-issues list, or the product backlog owner.
- Decide the escalation rule now: what turns a listening pass into a crisis call (step 11), so drama is classified once instead of re-litigated weekly.
- Keep a single findings note, dated per session, rather than scattered screenshots — findability is what makes listening compound.
**Checkpoint.** "Which day and time is the weekly listen happening, and where do bug reports found in replies go?"
**Scope guard.** If listening starts eating more than its budgeted hours, shrink the keyword list before shrinking the cadence; volume is usually the problem, not frequency.
**Anti-pattern.** Do not turn the listening pass into a metrics review — follower counts and impressions are platform dashboards' job; listening is for what people are saying, not how the numbers are moving.

### Step 11 — Write the crisis one-pager
**Action.** Draft the crisis one-pager before it is needed: severity tiers, first-hour actions, a holding-statement skeleton, and a do-not-do list.
**Reference.** `references/crisis-management.md` — triage tiers, response windows, apology anatomy, and when to go quiet.
**Do this.**
- Define three or four severity tiers with observable triggers — one offended commenter is not an incident; a screenshot spreading is.
- Write the first-hour checklist: stop scheduled posts, assess the tier, draft a holding line, pick a second opinion.
- Draft the holding-statement skeleton and the apology anatomy now, while nobody is upset; fill-in-the-blanks beats improvisation under adrenaline.
- Pre-commit the do-not list: no arguing in threads, no deleting criticism, no subtweeting, no over-promising — each line is a rehearsed failure people recognize when they see it.
- Decide the platform for serious statements (the same place launch news goes) and rehearse how a scheduled post gets paused on each kept platform; both are muscle memory best built in peacetime.
**Checkpoint.** "Who is your second opinion when you cannot tell whether something is a tier-two or tier-three incident?"
**Test the pager.** Hand it to someone else with a hypothetical incident and time them: if they cannot classify and draft a holding line from the document alone in under ten minutes, the pager is not done.
**Scope note.** The one-pager covers public communication incidents; site outages, payment failures, and store-platform problems get an ops fix plus a status post, and only become tier-two or tier-three when public sentiment is actually moving.
**Storage note.** Keep the one-pager where it can be reached without the strategy document — a pinned note, a printed page — because the incident will not wait for the right file to open.

### Step 12 — Assemble, verify, and stress-test
**Action.** Fill `templates/social-strategy-template.md` with every decision, then run the section 05 rules and each reference's rubric as a checklist before declaring the work done.
**Reference.** `templates/social-strategy-template.md` for document structure; `references/strategy-case-studies.md` as the calibration target.
**Do this.**
- Verify the arithmetic: pillar percentages sum to 100, weekly counts match the cadence, and hour costs sum under budget with slack.
- Verify every section contains decisions with reasons, not intentions — someone else must be able to run this plan without asking you anything.
- Run the rubrics named in section 05 as literal checklists, marking each item pass or fail; a rubric run from memory silently becomes a rubber stamp.
- Re-read the decisions log against the final document: every non-obvious call should have a line, or the log has started lying.
- Run the failure-mode list in section 07 against the draft and fix every mode whose early signal is already present.
Storage note (kept here because step 12 is where documents get shipped): the finished strategy lives next to the product's planning notes, not in a marketing folder nobody opens — placement is part of the deliverable, because a document that is not where the operator already works will not be re-read.
**Checkpoint.** "Could a trusted friend run this plan for two weeks while you are offline — what would they get wrong?"
**If the answer is specific.** Fix those things now; the friend's wrong guesses are the exact spots where the document still holds decisions hostage in your head.

## 05 — Rules and Quality Bar

These rules separate a professional strategy from a generic one. Generic output names platforms and lists post ideas; professional output makes priced, reasoned decisions and ships its own checks.
Every rule exists because its absence is a documented failure mode — section 07 describes what happens when each is skipped.
The rubrics named below live in the reference files; run them as checklists in step 12, not from memory.
How to read the rules: each names its check, and the checks are the actual gate — a strategy is finished when every check passes, not when the document merely mentions the topic.

**1. The plan fits the stated hour budget with slack.**
Why: strategies die on the first busy week; slack is design, not padding.
Check: sum every platform cadence, listening block, and buffer against the user's number before approving the plan.

**2. Two platforms with distinct roles beat five with vibes.**
Why: attention compounds where you show up consistently, and half-effort accounts read as abandonment to both people and press.
Check: each kept platform in the role table has exactly one role and one hour cost; everything else goes on a dated watchlist.

**3. Ratios are arithmetic, not adjectives.**
Why: "mostly devlogs" cannot be scheduled, audited, or defended; 40% can.
Check: the ratio rubric in `references/content-pillars.md` — percentages sum to 100 and multiply out to real weekly counts.

**4. Every pillar is bad-week survivable.**
Why: pillars that require inspiration collapse exactly when the calendar gets hard, and that is when consistency matters most.
Check: each pillar names its cheap fallback format from step 6.

**5. Conversation is a line item, not a byproduct.**
Why: presences grow through replies and community loops; broadcast-only accounts plateau and calcify.
Check: the engagement checklist in `references/engagement-strategy.md` — reply priorities exist and reply time is budgeted.

**6. Show proof of work before claims.**
Why: process artifacts — builds, clips, postmortems — earn trust that adjectives cannot buy.
Check: every week in the arc has one concrete artifact; no week is theme-only.
Extension: this rule also governs growth claims — a presence that has shipped eight weeks of visible work has earned a wishlist ask, and one that has not has earned a quieter ask or none at all.

**7. Name the segment you disappoint.**
Why: a plan that serves everyone serves no one; pre-deciding the cut is what makes focus real instead of rhetorical.
Check: the segment ranking from step 2 names who gets dropped when time runs short.
Extension: the disappointed segment also defines what the strategy refuses to do — if press is the cut, the plan does not chase journalist accounts, and the decisions log records that refusal.

**8. No purchased or reciprocity-based growth.**
Why: pods, follow-back chains, and fake followers poison your signal, waste your engagement hours, and put platform standing at risk.
Check: the guardrails list in `references/growth-and-reach.md` is copied into the strategy verbatim.

**9. Listening findings get a destination.**
Why: feedback without a route into decisions is entertainment, and the same question asked five times in public is a bug report you are ignoring.
Check: the listening rubric in `references/social-listening.md` — every triage bucket has a named destination.

**10. The crisis one-pager exists before the crisis.**
Why: first-hour decisions made under adrenaline are the ones people regret; blanks filled in advance stay calm.
Check: the severity rubric in `references/crisis-management.md` — tiers, first-hour list, and holding skeleton are all present.

**11. Boundaries are written rules, not moods.**
Why: DM policy and no-go topics decided in advance protect the operator at the exact moment willpower is lowest.
Check: the rulebook from step 7 includes posting hours, DM policy, and block criteria, each with a reason.

**12. Numbers must reconcile.**
Why: a strategy whose percentages, counts, and hours disagree is three documents pretending to be one.
Check: step 12's arithmetic verification passes, and any later edit re-runs it.

**13. Durable mechanics over algorithm chasing.**
Why: feed algorithms change without notice; conversation quality, series, and proof-of-work keep paying across every change.
Check: no strategy line depends on a specific ranking behavior; every mechanic would work the same on a chronological feed.

**14. The document is written for a stranger.**
Why: the operator will be a stranger to this plan in six weeks — tired, mid-crunch, and missing context — so the document that serves that stranger also serves any collaborator.
Check: no section relies on unexplained shorthand, and every named thing (series, buckets, tiers) is defined where it is first used.
**15. Every strategy carries its own audit date.**
Why: strategies age quietly; a document with a written review date gets audited, and one without gets abandoned or defended on vibes.
Check: the document's final section names the review date (typically launch plus two weeks, then monthly) and the three checks the audit replays.

When in doubt, choose the boring durable option. The strategy's job is to still be running in month six, not to look clever today.
Two rules matter most in practice and are worth restating in plain terms: never spend hours the budget does not contain, and never let the feed become only advertising — nearly every failed presence violates one of these two, and most violate both.

## 06 — Worked Example

The scenario: Voidbreak Games is the working alias of a solo developer eight weeks from announcing Emberwake, a hand-painted 2D roguelike about rekindling a drowned lighthouse.
The developer has no public accounts beyond a dormant personal one, can commit six hours per week, and wants a pre-launch presence on exactly two platforms.
Agreed deliverables (section 03): platform roles and effort split, audience segments, four content pillars with ratio math, engagement rules and boundaries, an 8-week pre-launch arc, a minimal listening setup, and a crisis one-pager.
What follows are the intermediate artifacts a real run produces, condensed. The full strategy document lives in `examples/worked-social-strategy.md`.
Read the artifacts in order and note how each one constrains the next: the success definition ranks the segments, the ranked segments score the platforms, the kept platforms set the cadence, and the cadence turns pillar shares into counts. Strategy documents fail when these links are skipped — every artifact here exists because a later one needs it.
The run took two sessions: the first produced the pitch, segments, and platform table and stalled on pillars (draft v1, below); the second killed the bad draft, fixed the ratios, and completed the arc. The stall is shown because it is the instructive part — v1 failing the four tests is what pillar design looks like before it is pillar design.

**Step 1-2 artifacts — pitch and segments.**
Pitch: "Emberwake is a hand-painted roguelike about relighting a drowned lighthouse, one run at a time."
Success for 90 days: demo downloads and a mailing list — not follower counts.
Segment cards (ranked, with the disappointment made explicit):
1. `Roguelike-curious players — want to see the game feel alive; will wishlist and download the demo when they see a moment of play.`
2. `Indie dev peers — want honest process; will amplify, give feedback, and collab when they see real numbers.`
3. `Small press and curators — want a story and assets; will cover when handed a kit, not a cold pitch.`
Named disappointment: press ranks last pre-launch; they get the kit at week 7, not weekly attention.
Why the ranking holds: the success definition (demo downloads) is driven almost entirely by players, assisted by peers, and only assisted by press — so the segments rank in that order, and the strategy says so instead of pretending to serve all three equally.

**Step 3 artifact — platform shortlist scoring.**
Six candidates scored before deciding (1 = weak, 3 = strong):

| Candidate | Audience fit | Format cost | Durability | Weekly cost | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Twitter/X | 3 — devlog culture, peers, press lurk here | 3 — text plus clips | 1 — feeds decay in hours | 2.5 h | keep: primary stage |
| itch.io | 3 — buyers, jam players, devlog readers | 3 — devlog posts on the page | 3 — pages persist and convert | 1.5 h | keep: storefront and archive |
| YouTube | 2 — discovery strong, audience early | 1 — heavy editing | 3 — search persists | 6+ h | defer to post-launch |
| TikTok | 2 — reach large, format mismatch | 1 — vertical video cadence | 1 | 4 h | drop — operator discomfort |
| Discord | 3 — superfans and playtesters | 2 — moderation is ongoing | 3 | 2 h | defer — open with the demo |
| Bluesky | 2 — growing dev community | 3 — text plus clips | 1 | 0.5 h | watchlist — mirror X posts |

Effort split from the keep/drop calls: X 2.5 h + itch.io 1.5 h + listening 1.0 h + buffer 1.0 h = 6.0 h — the buffer is the slack, reserved for reply overruns and bad weeks.
Two platforms with distinct roles: X carries conversation and press; itch.io carries persistence and purchase intent.

**Step 4 artifact — pillar drafts, v1 and v2.**
Draft v1 from the first session: `Devlogs`, `Marketing`, `Fun stuff`, `Art when I have it`, `Thoughts`.
Critique that killed the draft: "Marketing" is a goal, not a territory; "fun stuff" and "thoughts" overlap everything; "when I have it" is not a cadence; nothing here is distinct, provable, or bad-week safe.
Why v1 died in one sentence: every draft pillar was either a mood or a wish, and none could name two example posts the operator could produce in a tired week.
Draft v2 after the four tests:

| Pillar | Share | Definition | Example posts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build-in-public | 40% | honest progress with numbers and process | "water physics: three weeks of failures"; "week 14 in one number: 41 rooms" |
| Systems deep-dives | 25% | one mechanic taken apart for peers | "how the lantern light budget works"; "procedural drowning, explained" |
| Art and world | 20% | the drowned world, shown not told | "the chapel that floods at high tide"; palette studies |
| Community and signal | 15% | replies with substance, peer boosts, feedback asks | playtest callouts; "what should the tide cycle do?" |

**Step 5 artifact — ratio math.**
Cadence: 4 content units per week (3 X posts + 1 itch.io devlog).
Exact shares: 40% = 1.6, 25% = 1.0, 20% = 0.8, 15% = 0.6 — sums to 4.0.
Rounding rule (largest remainder over a four-week window): floors are 1/1/0/0; the two extra units go to the largest remainders (art 0.8, then the 0.6 tie broken toward build-in-public).
Four-week plan: 6 / 4 / 3 / 3 = 16 units = 4 weeks x 4. The fractional 15% pillar is tracked on the rolling window, not force-rounded weekly.
The arithmetic is in the strategy document so any future edit re-derives the counts instead of guessing.
Cross-check that caught a real error: the first ratio draft implied 5 weekly units, which priced at 7.5 hours against a 6-hour budget — the cadence dropped to 4 units before pillar shares were finalized, which is exactly the order the workflow prescribes.

**Steps 6-8 artifacts — series, engagement rules, growth mechanics (condensed).**
Series: `Lantern Log` (weekly itch.io devlog, build-in-public), `One Mechanic` (biweekly X thread, systems), `Postcard` (weekly X image, art and world).
Engagement rules, 6 of 12 shown:
**1. Questions get answered first, within a day when possible — every answer is public unless privacy requires otherwise.** Public answers compound: they read as proof the developer shows up, and they become searchable FAQ content for every future player with the same question.
**2. Criticism gets exactly one public reply; then it moves to a thread of one, or to the known-issues list.** One honest reply earns respect; a thread war screenshots badly and eats the day, and the known-issues list converts the complaint into roadmap signal.
**3. No pricing or feature promises in DMs or replies — dates are said once, when they are real.** Repeated soft commitments become quotable failures; the demo ships when it ships, and the feed says so once.
**4. Never reply while hungry, furious, or past midnight; the draft can wait for morning.** Almost every screenshot-level incident begins as a late-night reply that felt correct at the time.
**5. Block criteria are fixed in advance: slurs, doxxing energy, bad-faith repetition after one good answer.** Fixed criteria make blocking boring policy instead of a mood-driven act that screenshots as "dev silences critic."
**6. Boost a peer for every self-promotional post — the 1:1 reciprocity keeps the feed from curdling into advertising.** Feeds that only promote train their own audience to scroll past, which quietly zeroes out every launch post later.
Growth mechanics chosen: two peer collaborations (a shared playtest swap and one guest devlog), plus mirroring X posts to the watchlist platform — all inside the buffer, not new hours.
Both collaborations were scheduled for weeks 4 and 6, past the midpoint the workflow requires, and each was paired with a written offer: playtest hours, an asset, and a guest slot in return.

**Step 9 artifact — the 8-week arc (condensed).**

| Week | Theme | Pillar emphasis | Flagship artifact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | vocabulary | build-in-public | lantern rig clip + intro post |
| 2 | first systems | deep-dives | `One Mechanic` thread: the tide clock |
| 3 | the world | art and world | flooded chapel screenshot set |
| 4 | feedback | community and signal | tide-mechanic ask + reply sprint |
| 5 | demo prep | build-in-public | demo build slice: one full run |
| 6 | difficulty | deep-dives | tuning postmortem thread |
| 7 | the kit | art and world | press kit + trailer beat |
| 8 | announcement | build-in-public | announcement post + demo launch |

Every week carries one flagship artifact; weeks 4 and 8 deliberately bend the ratios toward community and announcement, and the decisions log says so.
What the condensation hides: each week also carries the cheap fallback formats (two posts can be one screenshot and two honest sentences), and the arc was stress-tested by moving every flagship artifact one week later to confirm nothing collides with the launch date.
How the arc respects the ratios: 16 planned units over weeks 1-4 plus 16 over weeks 5-8, with the flagship artifacts double-counted inside their pillars — week 4's ask counts as community, week 8's announcement counts as build-in-public because it is the honest number "the demo exists."

**Step 10 artifact — listening setup (condensed).**
Keywords: `Emberwake`, `voidbreak`, `lighthouse roguelike`, `itch.io roguelike`, plus two peer titles with overlapping audiences.
Cadence: Tuesday and Friday, 20 minutes, on the calendar.
Buckets and destinations: praise goes to a testimonials note; questions go to a growing FAQ; bugs go to the known-issues list; feature asks go to a backlog note reviewed monthly.
Escalation rule: a listening pass becomes a crisis call only when a keyword result is from someone the operator does not know and is spreading beyond its original thread — everyone else gets the normal triage buckets.
First listening pass finding (real, from the run): a peer asked publicly how the water rendering worked — the question became week 2's `One Mechanic` thread, which is the route from listening to content working as designed.

**Step 11 artifact — crisis one-pager (skeleton).**
Tier 1 — one-off criticism: reply once, honestly, move on; no announcement.
Tier 2 — a pattern or a spreading screenshot: stop scheduled posts, assess within 2 hours, holding line, second opinion.
Tier 3 — safety, legal, or existential: quiet on the topic, gather facts, respond once with counsel; silence is a decision and gets logged.
Holding line skeleton: "We see this and are looking into it — next update by [time]."
Do-not list: argue in threads, delete criticism, subtweet, over-promise, blame users.
Why the pager is drafted before announcement week: the launch is itself the riskiest window (bugs, delays, a contentious design choice surfacing to a much larger audience), so the one-pager ships with the strategy rather than after the first scare.

**Step 12 artifact — verification record.**
The strategy's verification section records the checks as run, not just as passed: percentages 40+25+20+15 = 100; four-week units 6+4+3+3 = 16 = 4 per week; hours 2.5+1.5+1.0+1.0 = 6.0 against a 6.0 budget; rubrics run on the date of assembly with one line each.
An audit six months later replays these same checks against the account's actual output — the record is what makes drift measurable instead of arguable.

**Calibration and stress-test.**
The draft was held against `examples/worked-social-strategy.md` and the section 05 rules: percentages sum to 100, counts sum to 16 over four weeks, hours sum to 6.0 against a 6-hour budget with the buffer as slack.
Failure-mode sweep: early signals of "promo gravity" and "hour-budget fantasy" were found and fixed by re-weighting pillar 1 and cutting YouTube from the plan rather than deferring it indefinitely.

## 07 — Failure Modes and Recovery

Failure modes are listed in the order they tend to appear in a run: platform sprawl and hour-budget fantasy strike during planning, promo gravity and ratio collapse strike during execution, and the rest are chronic risks that surface in audits. Sweep this list against any draft in step 12; run it again at every monthly audit, because modes rotate in as strategies age.
Each mode below lists the early signal, the corrective move, and the prevention that stops it from recurring.
Treat the "in the wild" lines as field references: they describe what each failure looks like from the outside, in accounts the reader has almost certainly seen without knowing why they felt off.
A mode that has fired once gets a prevention note in the decisions log, so the fix survives the operator's memory of the incident.

**1. Platform sprawl.**
Early signal: three or more "we should also be on..." ideas in a single session, and the hour math already broken.
Corrective move: cut back to the two platforms with distinct roles; move the rest to a dated watchlist with revisit conditions.
What it looks like in the wild: five accounts, each with a handful of posts, all clearly abandoned at the same time.
Prevention: step 3's scoring table and rule 2 — every platform needs a role and an hour cost to exist at all.

**2. Promo gravity.**
Early signal: the pillar table says 40% build-in-public, but the last eight posts were all "wishlist now."
Corrective move: re-run the ratio math against the last month of posts, then schedule the neglected pillars first for two weeks.
What it looks like in the wild: an account that becomes a billboard; followers stop replying because there is nothing to reply to.
Prevention: rule 3's ratio rubric and the monthly pillar audit in `references/content-pillars.md`.

**3. Ratio collapse into broadcast.**
Early signal: reply time quietly vanishes from the week; the operator "hasn't had time to answer people."
Corrective move: restore a budgeted reply block before adding any new content commitment; cut a post, not the conversation.
What it looks like in the wild: steady posting, zero replies, a feed that reads like a press wire.
Prevention: rule 5 and the engagement checklist in `references/engagement-strategy.md`.

**4. Hour-budget fantasy.**
Early signal: the plan assumes best-case weeks — no crunch, no illness, no day-job spike.
Corrective move: re-cost the plan against the worst recent month; cut until it fits, and write down what gets dropped first.
What it looks like in the wild: a two-week posting streak followed by a three-week silence and an apology post.
Prevention: rule 1's slack check and step 3's bad-week question.

**5. Vanity listening.**
Early signal: the weekly listen is a follower-count glance, and triage buckets exist but nothing has ever moved through them.
Corrective move: route one concrete finding into a real destination this week — a FAQ entry, a known-issue, a backlog note.
What it looks like in the wild: the same question answered ad hoc ten times while the FAQ waits.
Prevention: rule 9 and the listening rubric in `references/social-listening.md`.

**6. Crisis improvisation.**
Early signal: the one-pager section is still an outline when the first real incident lands.
Corrective move: run `references/crisis-management.md`'s first-hour checklist immediately; write the full one-pager in the aftermath, not instead of responding.
What it looks like in the wild: a defensive 1 a.m. reply that screenshots worse than the original complaint.
Prevention: rule 10 — the one-pager is drafted in step 11 while nobody is upset.

**7. Borrowed economics.**
Early signal: the plan copies a large account's cadence — daily videos, hourly presence — without that account's team or library.
Corrective move: re-price every borrowed behavior in hours; keep the mechanics, drop the volume, and say so in the decisions log.
What it looks like in the wild: a solo operator burned out by week five trying to run a media company alone.
Prevention: rule 1 and the effort economics in `references/platform-landscape.md`.

**8. Series abandonment.**
Early signal: a named series is two editions old with a three-week gap and no written reason.
Corrective move: either resurrect it with a smaller slot or retire it explicitly and fold its pillar share elsewhere.
What it looks like in the wild: "part 1" of things, never "part 2" — appointment value that stood people up.
Prevention: step 6's protected-series question and the cheap fallback format.

**9. Watchlist drift.**
Early signal: the watchlist platform keeps getting "just one more post" every week without ever graduating to a scored role or a dated revisit trigger.
Corrective move: either promote it through step 3's scoring table or freeze it to pure mirroring until the trigger date fires.
What it looks like in the wild: an account that is technically alive, technically earning nothing, and quietly costing an hour a week.
Prevention: the decisions-log rule from step 3 — every "later" carries a dated trigger.

A draft that triggers no early signals is rare; if it happens, verify the arithmetic anyway — silence from this list usually means the numbers were never actually checked.
How to use this section in an audit: run the early-signal lines against the account's last four weeks of output and its strategy document side by side; the signals are defined so that both drift (document says one thing, account does another) and decay (both quietly stopped) are catchable.

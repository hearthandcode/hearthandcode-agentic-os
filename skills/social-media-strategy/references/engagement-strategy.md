# engagement-strategy.md — Reply craft, conversation design, boundaries

This reference backs workflow step 7. It carries reply priorities, conversation craft, the DM policy frame, criticism handling, and the boundary rules that keep engagement from eating the operator.

## Why engagement is a designed system

Replies are the highest-trust surface an operator owns: a good reply is seen by the original poster and by every silent reader, and it compounds into reputation in a way posts alone do not. But the same surface is the easiest to lose hours to and the easiest to turn into a liability. The rulebook exists so behavior is decided in peacetime.

## Reply priorities

Fixed order, written into the rulebook:

1. **Questions** — answered first, ideally within a day. Public by default: answers compound into searchable FAQ content and read as proof the operator shows up.
2. **Thoughtful critics** — second. One honest public reply, then stop or move private; the goal is signal, not victory.
3. **Peers and collaborators** — third, and reciprocity lives here: boosts, feedback trades, shared playtests.
4. **Drive-by praise** — acknowledged last and lightly; hearts are cheap and welcome, but hours are not.

Reply time is budgeted, not stolen: the platform hour costs in the strategy include the conversation the posts will generate. If replies regularly exceed the budget, the fix is cadence, not heroics.

## Reply craft

- Answer the question asked, once, plainly. Public answers are content; write them for the silent majority reading later, not only for the asker.
- Add something: a number, a cause, a next step. "Thanks!" is a receipt, not a reply.
- Correct mistakes plainly and once. Audiences trust operators who say "that broke, fixed in this build" over those who quietly patch.
- Never quote-dunk a small account, never punch down, never turn a correction into a performance — every thread is written with its future screenshot in mind.
- Ask questions back sparingly and honestly; engagement-bait questions read as engagement bait, and audiences learn the pattern fast.

## Conversation design

Recurring conversation structures beat one-off exchanges:

- **The public FAQ loop**: same question twice in public means one honest answer plus a growing FAQ; the FAQ then feeds a pillar post.
- **The feedback ask**: specific, scoped, and answerable ("which of these two tide behaviors feels better?") — vague asks ("thoughts?") collect silence or noise.
- **The peer exchange**: boost-for-reciprocity, playtest swaps, and honest signal-boosting, governed by the 1:1 rule — for every self-promotional post, engage with someone else's work.
- **The ritual thread**: a fixed recurring prompt (monthly goals, weekly wins) that gives quiet members a predictable way in.

## DM policy

Decide three things in advance:
1. **Access** — who can reach the operator by DM (open, followers-only, closed), and where the exceptions go (email, form, support channel).
2. **Response times** — what is promised and to whom; under-promise deliberately, because DM response time is invisible until it disappoints.
3. **Never-in-DM content** — pricing, feature promises, support obligations, sensitive or legal topics. These move to a public answer or a proper channel, once, with a reason. A private promise is a quotable failure with no witnesses to the context.

## Criticism handling

The default ladder:
1. One honest public reply acknowledging the specific point — no defensiveness, no counterattack.
2. If it continues: disengage from the thread; the point is already on record.
3. If it recurs or grows: it has become a listening finding (bugs, feature asks) or a crisis trigger (tier two), and the corresponding runbook takes over — never a thread war.

Tone rules: thank the specific useful critic once; never explain the same correction three times; never relitigate in public what a private channel settled. Deleting criticism is never the move — it screenshots worse than the criticism and converts a critic into a story about you.

## Personal boundaries

Written as numbered rules with reasons, in the step 7 rulebook:
- **Posting hours** — the operator names the hours the account is allowed to be "on" (including the hungry-furious-midnight rule), because feeds manufacture urgency that is not real.
- **No-go topics** — subjects the operator will not post about, even when baited: politics by default unless it is the product's business, other developers' launches, pricing internals, unreleased partner work.
- **Block criteria** — fixed in advance (slurs, doxxing energy, bad-faith repetition after one good answer) so blocking is boring policy, not a mood that screenshots as "dev silences critic."
- **Log-off signals** — the physical tells (scrolling while angry, drafting reply number four) that mean the session ends now, and the hour it happens is the rule, not the feeling.

## The engagement checklist (run in step 12)

- Reply priorities exist and rank at least questions, critics, peers.
- Reply time is inside the platform hour budgets.
- DM policy states access, response times, and never-in-DM content.
- Criticism ladder is written (one reply, then stop or escalate).
- Posting hours and no-go topics are rules with reasons, not intentions.
- Block criteria are fixed and brief.
- The reciprocity rule exists (1:1 with self-promotion).
- Every rule has a one-line reason — reasons survive when memory does not.

## Anti-patterns

- **Reply-hour sprawl**: treating conversation as free time until it consumes the week; budget it and cut cadence before cutting presence.
- **The martyr loop**: answering everything instantly and personally until resentment builds; the priority order exists precisely so some things are allowed to wait.
- **Snark as brand**: a funny hostile voice is expensive the day it is screenshotted at the operator's expense.
- **Boundary by exception**: every exception arguable in the moment becomes the actual policy; write the rule, let the rule take the heat.

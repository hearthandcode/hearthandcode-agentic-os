# crisis-management.md — Triage, first hours, apology anatomy, going quiet

This reference backs workflow step 11. It is the playbook behind the crisis one-pager: severity tiers, the first-hour checklist, holding statements, the anatomy of an apology that repairs, and the deliberate use of silence.

## Triage first, always

The most expensive crisis behavior is treating everything as a five-alarm fire. Classification is the first act:

| Tier | Definition (observable triggers) | Response posture |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One-off criticism or a bad-faith dunk from a small account | normal engagement rules: one honest reply, then stop; no announcement, no schedule change |
| 2 | A pattern or spreading item: repeated complaints, a screenshot traveling, an influencer with reach piling on | pause scheduled posts; assess within 2 hours; holding line if needed; second opinion engaged |
| 3 | Safety, legal, or existential: doxxing, threats, credible legal exposure, conduct allegations, or a crowd demanding answers across platforms | quiet on the topic, gather facts, one response with counsel, all other activity paused |

The one-off versus pattern test, stated plainly: one offended commenter is not an incident; a screenshot spreading beyond its original thread is. Tier two becomes tier three when claims cross into safety, legality, or conduct — not when volume alone grows.

## The first-hour checklist (tier two and up)

1. **Stop the schedule** — pause every scheduled and drafted post on all kept platforms; a cheerful automation posting through an incident is the second crisis.
2. **Classify the tier** — against the table above, in writing, with the trigger observed.
3. **Get the second opinion** — the named person from the one-pager; tier-three incidents always use them.
4. **Preserve context** — screenshots and links of the original item and the spread, before anything deletes or edits; context is the resource the response will need.
5. **Draft the holding line** — from the pre-written skeleton, edited to name the actual situation.
6. **Say nothing else on the topic** — one channel, one message, until the assessment completes.

## Holding statements

A holding line does three jobs: acknowledges, commits to a time, and promises nothing it cannot keep.

Skeleton: "We see this and are looking into it — next update by [specific time]."

Rules:
- Name a time and meet it or update before it; missed self-imposed deadlines are the fastest credibility burn.
- Never speculate about cause, blame, or scope in a holding line; facts arrive later, the line only buys time honestly.
- Post the holding line where the audience actually is (the primary stage), not only where the incident started.

## The apology anatomy

A real apology has a fixed anatomy, and each part is load-bearing:
1. **What happened** — plainly, in one sentence, without hedging adjectives.
2. **Who was affected and how** — the specific harm, not "some concerns were raised."
3. **Own the cause** — "we shipped X without testing Y" beats "mistakes were made"; passive voice is where accountability goes to hide.
4. **What changes** — the concrete action, with a date where honest, and nothing performed for the feed.
5. **What will not happen again** — the mechanism, not the promise ("payouts now get a second review," not "we take this seriously").

Omitted parts are the usual failure: the apology that skips owning the cause reads as PR; the one without an action reads as theater. Length is not repair — a tight apology with all five parts beats a essay with none. And never attach a defense to an apology; "we're sorry, but" is a counterargument wearing an apology as a hat.

Timing: apologize when the facts are known, not instantly and not on the critic's clock; a wrong apology requires a second apology, which is the compound interest of crises.

## When to go quiet

Silence is a decision, and it gets logged like one:
- **Tier three by default** — while facts are gathered or counsel is engaged, say nothing beyond the holding line; a wrong sentence at tier three cannot be unsaid.
- **Topic silence, not total silence** — go quiet on the incident, not on the presence: unrelated scheduled content can continue if it was already planned and reads as unrelated (check honestly).
- **Never delete-and-pretend** — deleting criticism or quietly locking everything reads as admission of the worst interpretation; locking a thread is legitimate when it stops harassment, and saying so is better than doing it silently.
- **End quiet loudly** — when the incident closes, one closing note (fix shipped, decision reached, resolution posted) closes the loop; incidents that end in silence become "and then they just went quiet," which is its own story.

## Post-incident

Every tier-two and tier-three incident gets a short written debrief within a week: what triggered it, what the tier was, what the response cost in hours, what the one-pager got wrong. The one-pager is updated in the same week — the playbook that stays unchanged through a real incident was not actually used.

## The severity rubric (run in step 12)

- Three or four tiers with observable triggers, not feelings.
- First-hour checklist present, beginning with stopping the schedule.
- Holding-statement skeleton pre-written with a fill-in time.
- Apology anatomy present with all five parts.
- Do-not list present: argue in threads, delete criticism, subtweet, over-promise, blame users.
- Named second opinion and the platform for serious statements.
- Silence policy written (when, what kind, how it ends).

## Anti-patterns

- **The 1 a.m. reply** — the defensive late-night response that screenshots worse than the original complaint; the posting-hours rule exists for this exact moment.
- **The vague acknowledgment** — "we're aware of some concerns" amplifies by confirming nothing; name the thing or say nothing yet.
- **The apology with a lawyer in its mouth** — legal-scrubbed text that concedes nothing converts an incident into a story about cowardice.
- **Quiet as avoidance** — silence chosen to avoid discomfort rather than to gather facts; the one-pager's tiers decide quiet, not the operator's dread.

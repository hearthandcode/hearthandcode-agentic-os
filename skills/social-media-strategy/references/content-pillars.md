# content-pillars.md — Pillar design, ratio math, series thinking

This reference backs workflow steps 4, 5, 6, and 9. It carries the four tests, the naming rule, the ratio procedure with rounding, and the arc constraints that turn pillars into a calendar-ready plan.

## What a pillar is

A pillar is a repeatable content territory: named, defined, provable, and sustainable. Pillars are the intermediate layer between strategy and calendar — the calendar schedules units, pillars say what those units are for, and ratios say how many of each.

A pillar is not: a goal ("marketing"), a mood ("fun stuff"), or a frequency ("when I have it"). If a draft pillar cannot name two example posts a tired operator could produce this week, it is a vibe.

## The four tests (step 4's gate)

Run all four on every draft pillar; a draft failing any test is cut or merged:

1. **Distinct** — it does not overlap another pillar's territory. Overlap check: list each pillar's example posts; if two pillars share more than half their examples, merge them and redistribute the share.
2. **Valuable** — at least one ranked segment demonstrably wants it. The card says what they take from it.
3. **Sustainable** — it is bad-week survivable: the operator could produce an instance at minimum energy. Pillars that require inspiration collapse exactly when consistency matters most.
4. **Provable** — the operator can show it, not just claim it. Proof lives in artifacts: builds, clips, studies, numbers.

Draft at least two more pillars than you plan to keep, then cut — the discarded drafts are where overlap and wishful thinking hide.

## Naming rule

A pillar's name must survive on a calendar and in an audit. Names that fail: "Marketing" (a goal, not a territory), "Misc" (a drawer), "Thoughts" (a mood). Names that pass name the territory itself: build-in-public, systems deep-dives, art and world, community and signal. Test: if a stranger read only the pillar names, could they predict which posts belong where?

## Ratio planning (step 5's procedure)

1. Give each kept pillar a percentage share. Allocate by segment priority and production cost, not by what is easiest to make — ease-weighted ratios are how accounts become 90% screenshots.
2. Sum the shares; they must equal 100. If they do not, the plan is three documents pretending to be one.
3. Multiply shares by the weekly content unit count (set in step 3 from platform cadences). Example: 4 units at 40/25/20/15 gives 1.6 / 1.0 / 0.8 / 0.6.
4. Resolve rounding with largest-remainder, written as a rule: floors first, then leftover units to the largest remainders, ties broken toward the pillar driving the success definition. Write the rule itself into the strategy, not just its result.
5. Any pillar that stays fractional across weeks is tracked on a rolling four-week window rather than force-rounded weekly.
6. Cross-check against hours: units x production cost per unit must fit the platform-priced budget. If it does not, shrink the ratio or the cadence — never assume future efficiency.

The output of ratio planning is per-week and per-month counts per pillar. Those numbers are the contract `content-calendar-planning` schedules against; the workflow's sanity test is that month units divided by four agree with the weekly cadence within one unit.

## Series thinking (step 6's method)

A series is a named, slotted, templated recurrence of one pillar. Series convert production from a blank page into a slot to fill.

Per series, write:
1. **Name** — recognition and anticipation live here; a numbered name ("Lantern Log #7") tells the audience where to look next.
2. **Slot** — which platform, which day or week, how long.
3. **Recipe** — inputs, steps, target production time. A series without a recipe is a new project every week.
4. **Fallback** — the cheap default that keeps the slot alive in bad weeks; a screenshot with two honest sentences beats a missed week.
5. **Retirement condition** — a written trigger ("pause after six editions if median replies stay under three") so endings are planned rather than guilted.

Caps: no more than two named series per platform; every series maps to exactly one pillar; a series with no pillar is drift waiting to happen.

## The content arc (step 9's constraints)

The arc is six to twelve weeks (eight by default) with one theme per week and one flagship artifact per week, built backwards from launch:

1. Final weeks carry the strongest proof: demo, trailer beat, press kit, announcement.
2. Early weeks build vocabulary and recognition: introduce the series names and the pillars so the audience knows what the feed is.
3. The heaviest production week is placed against the operator's real calendar, not the ideal one.
4. One flex week stays unthemed near the end to absorb slippage without rewriting the arc.
5. Every week still respects the ratios except where a written decision-log entry says otherwise (feedback weeks and announcement weeks legitimately bend them).
6. Arc test: read only the flagship artifacts back to back; they alone should tell a story from "what is this" to "this exists, here is proof, here is where to get it."

## Ratio rubric (run in step 12)

- Percentages sum to 100.
- Counts multiply out to real weekly units at the stated cadence.
- Counts fit the hour budget with replies included.
- Every pillar names its bad-week fallback format.
- Every week in the arc has one concrete artifact; no week is theme-only.
- Fractional pillars have a written rolling-window rule.
- The arc's bent weeks each carry a decisions-log entry.

## Anti-patterns

- **Ease-weighted ratios**: the feed drifts to whatever is cheapest, and the strategy's stated pillars become fiction.
- **Pillar sprawl**: five-plus blurry territories that overlap; four distinct pillars beat five vague ones.
- **Series guilt**: named series without recipes or retirement conditions become backlogs of shame; retire or shrink on the written trigger.
- **Theme-only weeks**: weeks with themes but no artifacts produce motion without evidence; the audience cannot see intentions.

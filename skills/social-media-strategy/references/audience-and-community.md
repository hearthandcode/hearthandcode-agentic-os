# audience-and-community.md — Who you serve, audience vs community, listening posture

This reference backs workflow steps 1 and 2. It contains the pitch test, the segmentation method, and the audience-versus-community distinction that decides behavior everywhere else in the strategy.

## The pitch test (step 1's first gate)

A product pitch that social strategy can use is one sentence, contains a promise or a feeling rather than a feature list, and could be repeated by a stranger after hearing it once.

Run the test:
1. Say the pitch aloud once.
2. Does it name who it is for or what it does to them ("about relighting a drowned lighthouse"), or does it list capabilities ("physics-based, procedurally generated, 60 fps")?
3. Could a stranger who heard it once repeat it without losing the point?
4. If it failed: the product story needs one more pass before social strategy starts, because every pillar, segment, and post inherits this sentence.

A pitch that survives the test becomes the strategy's north star: segment cards are read against it, pillars are drafted from it, and the announcement week is judged by whether it says the sentence plainly.

## Turning "success" into something steerable

The workflow rejects follower counts as a success definition. The working form is: one number, one date, one mechanism.

- Weak: "grow the audience."
- Better but still weak: "10,000 followers by March."
- Working: "200 demo wishlists by March 1, via the store page" — because it names the action, the deadline, and the surface the presence is supposed to move.

The success definition ranks the segments: whichever segment's action most directly drives the number ranks first. If the user's stated success and their ranked segments disagree, that contradiction is the single most valuable finding of step 1; surface it instead of smoothing it.

## Segmentation into named groups (step 2's method)

Split "everyone who might care" into two to four named segments. More than four is not segmentation, it is a directory.

Each segment card has four fields:
1. **Name** — a working label, specific enough to appear in sentences: "wishlist-curious players," not "the audience."
2. **Who they are** — where they already gather, what they read, who they follow. If you cannot name the place they gather, this is a demographic guess, not a segment.
3. **What they want from you** — the specific thing they will take: a moment of play, honest process, assets, a story.
4. **What they will do** — the observable action: wishlist, download, reply, share, cover, buy.

Then mark each segment as audience or community (next section) and rank them. Ranking must be explicit, because the strategy spends its scarcest resource — hours — in rank order.

The disappointment is part of the card: write down which segment gets dropped when the week goes sideways. Pre-deciding the cut is what makes focus real; guilt-driven triage in the moment produces the opposite — serving whoever complained last.

## Audience vs community

- **Audience**: people who consume your output. They see posts, maybe click, mostly stay silent. You serve them with consistency and artifacts.
- **Community**: people who know each other and you. They reply to each other, they carry the work to strangers, they forgive delays. You serve them with presence, participation, and names.

Both are legitimate; they get different behavior and different metrics. An audience counts downloads; a community counts return visits and unmoderated conversation between members. Confusing them produces the classic failure: broadcasting at a community (they leave) or demanding audience-scale numbers from a community (they were never going to provide them).

Practical split for a small operator: one or two segments can be community-scale — peers, playtesters, superfans — and the largest segment is almost always audience-scale. The engagement rulebook (step 7) treats the two differently: audience gets artifacts and public answers, community gets rituals, direct participation, and access.

A useful test of whether a space is a community or an audience wearing a costume: when the operator does not post for a week, do members talk to each other (community) or does the space go silent (audience)?

## The segment-to-platform link

Segments gather somewhere specific. Step 3's scoring is mostly the mechanical consequence of step 2: a platform full of the wrong people scores low on audience fit no matter how large it is. Each kept platform should serve its ranked segment explicitly, and the strategy should be able to say which segment each platform is for in one sentence.

## Listening posture (what you owe them in return)

Serving segments means hearing them. Even before the formal listening setup (step 10), adopt the posture:

- Read replies as data, not as applause — a repeated question is a FAQ entry being requested, and a repeated complaint is a known-issues list forming.
- Treat peers as colleagues, not as distribution — the segment that amplifies you deserves amplification back, and it is also the segment that tells you the truth.
- Note what people say *around* the product, not only *to* it — the unsolicited comparison ("this reminds me of X") is positioning data the operator cannot generate alone.
- Never argue with a fact that keeps coming back; collect it. The route from listening findings into decisions is step 10's job; the posture is what makes those findings honest.

## Common traps

- **Peer capture**: over-serving the pleasant, reply-heavy peer segment because it is rewarding to talk to, while the silent buyer segment starves. Rank by what moves the goal, not by who is nice.
- **The segment of one**: building a card for a single loud individual; cards describe groups, and individuals become feedback, not segments.
- **The mirror audience**: segmenting by what the operator wishes people wanted, rather than where demonstrable gathering and action exist.
- **Metric transplants**: judging a community with audience metrics (reach) or an audience with community metrics (warmth) — each segment keeps its own yardstick, written into its card.

# strategy-case-studies.md — Case study: an indie maker's 12-month presence build

This reference backs workflow steps 9 and 12 as calibration material. It is a realistic fictional scenario — a solo indie maker building a presence from zero over twelve months — with the numbers a real operator would see. Nothing here describes a real company or a current platform algorithm; the mechanics are durable.

## The maker and the setup

A solo developer building a small atmospheric puzzle game — call it *Quiet Hours* — with a target release in month nine and no audience: a personal account with zero posts, a store page with a placeholder, no list. Time budget: five hours a week. Goal at the start of the year: a demo with 500 wishlists by month nine, stated as number, date, and mechanism.

The maker is a competent practitioner new to presence-building, which is exactly the reader this skill serves.

## Months 1-3 — Foundation and the first plateau

**Decisions made.** Two platforms: a microblog stage (devlog culture, peers, press) at 3 hours a week and a store-front devlog page (persistence, purchase intent) at 1 hour, plus a weekly 30-minute listening slot inside a 5-hour budget. Four pillars: build-in-public 35%, systems explainers 25%, the world shown 20%, community signal 20%.

**What the feed looked like.** Flat. Weeks 1-8 averaged single-digit replies, and the maker's notes from the period record the temptation to add platforms or chase trends. The strategy held because the ratios and the hour costs were written down and the audit said the plan was being executed, not failing — execution and results are different checks.

**What actually built value:** two named series started in month two (a weekly build log, a biweekly explainer thread); the first explainer thread — about a load-time trick — earned its first share from a larger peer, worth more than a month of posting into the void.

**Plateau lesson:** month three's audit showed the pillar ratios had drifted to 55% build-in-public because screenshots were easy. The fix was the ratio rubric, not guilt: restore the written shares, add the bad-week fallback format to the explainer series, and log the drift in the decisions log.

## Months 4-6 — The first lift

**Collaboration past the midpoint.** In month five — with four months of proof-of-work visible — the maker traded playtests with two peer devs. Cost: about two hours each. Result: modest follower movement but two lasting relationships, which is the actual conversion collaboration offers.

**The artifact that traveled.** A systems explainer thread on audio design was picked up and shared by a larger account in month six: roughly 30,000 impressions in two days, 800 store-page visits, 90 demo wishlists in a week — the largest single lift of the year, and instructive precisely because it could not have been scheduled. The honest accounting went into the audit: reach with cause ("shared by a peer with a larger audience"), not reach as a personal metric.

**Listening starts earning.** The monthly pattern review found the same question about save systems appearing four times in a month. Disposition: it became the next explainer thread. That loop — question to content to answer — became the maker's cheapest content source for the rest of the year.

## Months 7-9 — Crunch, and the plan holds

**The bad quarter.** Development crunch cut real availability to about two hours a week in month seven. The strategy survived because it had priced failure in advance: the cheap fallback format (a screenshot plus two honest sentences) kept both series alive at reduced length, the flex week absorbed a missed devlog, and the written disappointment rule (peers first, press last) triaged where the remaining hours went.

**One real incident.** Month eight: a reply drafted late at night, defending a difficulty choice sarcastically, screenshotted and passed around. The response followed the one-pager — no follow-up reply, a next-morning one-line acknowledgment ("that was a bad-tempered reply from me, sorry — the point stands, the tone didn't"), and the posting-hours rule got a note in the debrief. Cost of the incident: a day of goodwill; cost of the follow-up: one post. The pre-decided block criteria and one-reply rule prevented the thread war that would have made it a week.

**Number check at month nine:** 310 demo wishlists, 62% of the annual goal, with three months to run — on plan, tracked in the audit rather than in the maker's mood.

## Months 10-12 — Launch window

**Weeks before release.** The arc's final four weeks ran as designed: press kit month ten (written once, linked everywhere), trailer beat and demo polish, the announcement post with the pitch sentence, launch-day thread with the demo link and one honest number ("four years, 41 rooms, one wish: light something").

**Launch week reality.** Sales arrived smaller than hoped and the demo conversion beat expectations; the strategy's success definition — wishlists by a date via a mechanism — was met at 540 against 500 in month eleven. The feed's follower count finished around 3,400: modest, durable, and pointed at surfaces the maker owns.

**The year's audit, honest numbers:**
- Hours: about 210 across twelve months (5/week average, with the crunch quarter at 2-3).
- Output: 96 short posts, 15 long-form devlogs, 9 explainer threads, 6 collaborations, 1 press kit, 2 series surviving to launch.
- Conversions: 500+ demo wishlists, 3 press touches from the kit, 2 recurring collaborators, one FAQ post route running monthly.
- What did not work: a third platform opened month four ("just mirroring" became 2 hours a week of drift) — cut in month six's audit; a series retired on its written trigger; two pillar ratios rewritten once.

## The transferable numbers

| Pattern | This run | Why it transfers |
|---|---|---|
| Platforms | 2 kept, 1 drifted and cut | small budgets feed two surfaces honestly; the third becomes drift |
| Hour split | 60% stage, 20% storefront, 10% listening, 10% buffer | reply load makes the stage expensive even with cheap formats |
| Plateau length | 8 weeks before first real lift | consistency is the price of admission, not a guarantee |
| Lift source | one shared thread + collaborations | reach arrives via peers and artifacts, not cadence |
| Incident cost | one bad reply, contained by pre-written rules | boundaries written in peacetime are the cheap insurance |
| Success metric | mechanism-based (wishlists via page) | survived the year un-poisoned by vanity numbers |

## Reading the study against the workflow

- Steps 1-3 produced the year's spine in one session; every later hour was spent inside its decisions.
- Steps 4-5's ratios were violated once (month three) and repaired by rubric — drift is normal, repair is the craft.
- Step 8's midpoint rule was honored and paid; the month-four third platform violated it and paid too.
- Step 11's one-pager turned a tier-one-adjacent wobble into a contained incident instead of a thread war.
- The year's single best-performing asset was an explainer thread born from a listening finding — the sensor loop paying for itself.

Use this study for calibration, not imitation: the numbers are one operator's, the mechanics are the transferable part, and any real run should expect its own plateau length, lift sources, and crunch quarters.

# marketing-strategy

## 01 — Purpose

Small teams do not fail at marketing for lack of channels — they fail by
choosing channels before positioning, messaging before audience, and budgets
before objectives. This skill exists to enforce the order that makes limited
resources compound: understand the market (STP), decide what the product
means (positioning), learn who the buyer is (audience research), decide what
to say (messaging), decide where to say it (channels), plan the time-bounded
push (campaign), and then measure only what informs the next decision
(metrics).

The skill is built for the constraints of a solo founder or small team: no
brand team, no agency, no measurement department. Every framework in it has
been reframed for that reality — segments you can count, channels you can
work by hand, budgets in the hundreds rather than the millions, and metrics
narrow enough to fit on one dashboard. The output of this skill is a strategy
document plus the campaign briefs that execute it; the copy that fills the
channels is a different craft, named and handed off.

**Three outcomes this skill owns:**

1. A positioning decision that names the buyer, the category frame, the
   differentiated value, and the rejected alternatives — so the next person
   to touch messaging inherits a decision, not a vibe.
2. A launch or campaign plan: channels selected by scorecard, sequenced
   across weeks, budgeted to the dollar, each with an objective and a
   measurable success criterion.
3. A metrics dashboard of five to seven numbers with cadence, targets, and
   pre-agreed recovery rules — so the first weak week produces a decision
   instead of a panic.

This skill treats marketing as a decision discipline, not a content chore.
Every artifact it produces is judged by one question: does this let the team
make the next decision — spend, stop, or double-down — without guessing?

**Scope boundary.** This skill owns strategy: segment, position, message
architecture, channel choice, campaign structure, and measurement. Adjacent
craft is adjacent: writing the actual words is copywriting-and-messaging;
running the social accounts day to day is social-media-strategy; pricing and
business-model questions belong to business-planning; scheduling the work
across a team belongs to operations-and-process-design. Handoffs are
explicit: this skill hands the copywriter a message hierarchy and proof
points, and hands the operator a calendar and budget — not the other way
around.

**Operating stance.** Three habits run through everything here:

- **Strategy before channels.** A channel is a delivery path for a message
  to a segment; choosing it before the segment and message exist is
  decoration. The scorecard only works after STP.
- **Cheapest learning first.** Six interviews before a landing page; a
  positioning test thread before a product hunt push. Money follows
  evidence, never leads it.
- **Measure to decide, not to feel.** Every metric on the dashboard must
  name the decision it informs. Numbers without decisions are anxiety with
  a graph.

**Vocabulary used throughout.** These terms are used with these meanings in
every file in this skill:

- *Segment* — a group of buyers with a shared, nameable pain and a reachable
  channel; large enough to matter, narrow enough to message.
- *STP* — Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning: the three-act structure of
  market strategy.
- *Positioning statement* — the one-sentence decision: for [buyer], [product]
  is the [category frame] that [differentiated value], unlike [alternative].
- *Message hierarchy* — the ordered stack: one core promise, three to five
  supporting benefits, proof for each.
- *Channel* — any path by which the message reaches the audience (subreddit,
  search, email list, launch site, marketplace).
- *Owned / earned / paid* — media you control, media you earn, media you
  buy; the flywheel usually runs earned → owned → paid.
- *Flight* — a time-bounded burst of activity within a campaign with its own
  objective and budget.
- *CAC / LTV* — cost to acquire one customer; total value one customer
  brings. For one-time products, LTV is revenue per customer plus referral
  value.
- *Activation* — the first moment a buyer gets the promised value; for a
  focus app, the first completed session, not the install.

## 02 — When to Use / When Not to Use

**Use this skill when any of these are true:**

1. **Positioning a product.** You can describe the features but not the
   sentence — who it is for, what category it plays in, why it wins. Use
   before any landing page, store listing, or launch post is written.
2. **Planning a launch.** A date exists (or should) and the work needs
   sequencing: what ships when, on which channels, with what budget and
   what KPIs per week.
3. **Choosing channels.** The options feel infinite and the hours are not —
   you need a scored comparison of reach, effort, cost, noise, and lifespan
   before committing.
4. **Building a campaign.** A time-bounded push (a launch, a seasonal push,
   a feature announcement) needs a brief: objective, audience, core
   message, flight plan, budget math, and stop conditions.
5. **Diagnosing marketing that is not landing.** Traffic without conversion,
   signups without purchases, purchases without referrals — the funnel is
   leaking somewhere and you need a structured diagnosis before changing
   anything.
6. **Setting up measurement.** You need the five to seven numbers that
   matter, their cadence, targets, and recovery rules — not a wall of
   vanity charts.
7. **Deciding where to spend a small budget.** A few hundred dollars exists
   and must be allocated across tools, paid tests, and assets with
   justification per dollar.
8. **Writing or auditing a marketing strategy document.** A founder,
   advisor, or collaborator asked for "the strategy" and it needs a
   complete, coherent artifact a stranger could execute from.

**Do not use this skill when:**

1. **The copy is the job.** Writing the landing page, the launch post, the
   email sequence, or the ad variants belongs to copywriting-and-messaging.
   This skill produces the message hierarchy that copy serves, not the
   sentences.
2. **The business model is the job.** Pricing architecture, revenue
   projections, unit economics beyond CAC/LTV, and market sizing for
   investors belong to business-planning. This skill consumes a price; it
   does not set one.
3. **Day-to-day community operations are the job.** Posting cadence,
   reply strategy, and content calendars for social accounts belong to
   social-media-strategy. This skill decides *whether* and *where* social
   matters, then hands off.
4. **Content calendars are the job.** Scheduling posts and planning an
   editorial pipeline week by week belongs to content-calendar-planning;
   this skill's calendar is a launch calendar with objectives, not an
   editorial one.
5. **A finished story is the job.** Narrative design for brand or product
   storytelling belongs to story-and-narrative-design; a product story's
   *structure* touches messaging, but the craft of the tale is theirs.
6. **UI or landing-page design is the job.** Visual hierarchy and
   presentation belong to ui-design-critique and design-system-foundations.
   Conversion problems that are actually legibility problems get handed
   there after diagnosis.
7. **Writing the actual software is the job.** Building the app, the site,
   or the integration belongs to engineering; this skill stops at the
   brief. No skill in this repo is needed for implementation planning.
8. **A single one-off post is the job.** One announcement needs no strategy
   — write it and post it. When a habit of posting forms a channel, return
   here for the channel decision.

When in doubt: if the deliverable is a *segment*, a *position*, a *message
stack*, a *channel plan*, a *budget*, or a *dashboard*, this skill applies.
If it is a *sentence*, a *screen*, or an *account's daily rhythm*, hand off
at the boundary with the artifacts the neighbor needs.

## 03 — Inputs and Outputs

**Inputs this skill consumes:**

- A product brief: what it does, what it costs, what platform it runs on,
  and who built it. One paragraph is enough to start.
- Constraints: budget ceiling, time available per week, launch date if
  fixed, and any hard limits (no paid ads, single platform, no user data
  collection).
- Market context: known competitors, existing customer conversations,
  support emails, community threads, or past marketing attempts with their
  results.
- Access for research: the ability to run six to ten customer interviews or
  a comparable survey; without any contact with real buyers, outputs carry
  a stated assumption risk.
- For an existing product: current analytics (traffic sources, conversion
  rates, churn or refund notes) and any prior strategy documents.

**Input readiness checklist.** Before starting the workflow, confirm:

- The price is decided, even provisionally — every channel scorecard and
  budget math depends on it.
- The founder can name at least one segment candidate, or step 2 will
  invent one from thin air and call it research.
- Competitors are listed with their actual prices and positioning, not
  memory of them.
- Any past marketing data is dated and sourced; undated "we tried ads"
  claims get re-run as tests or treated as rumor.
- The budget ceiling is explicit; a strategy without one optimizes for
  nothing.

**Outputs this skill produces:**

- `templates/marketing-strategy-template.md` — the master artifact:
  product overview, segmentation table, positioning with rejected variants,
  audience research summary, message hierarchy, channel scorecard, launch
  calendar, budget, metrics dashboard, and contingency table.
- `templates/campaign-brief-template.md` — one per campaign: objective,
  audience, core message, flight plan, budget math, success criteria, and
  stop conditions.
- Positioning record: final statement plus rejected variants with reasons,
  ready to defend in a review.
- Channel plan: the scorecard, the selected channels with type (owned,
  earned, paid), budget per channel, and expectation per channel.
- Metrics dashboard definition: the five to seven metrics, each with
  target, cadence, and the decision it informs, plus recovery rules.
- Contingency table: IF/THEN plans for the launch's most likely failures.

**Acceptance criteria per output.** An output is done when:

- A *segmentation table* names pain, estimated size, reachable channels,
  and purchase readiness per segment — and the selected segment survives
  the viability rubric in the audience reference.
- A *positioning statement* passes the template (buyer, category,
  differentiated value, alternative) and its rejected variants are written
  down with reasons.
- A *message hierarchy* has one core message, three to five supporting
  benefits, and at least one proof point per benefit.
- A *channel scorecard* scores every candidate on the same axes, and the
  selected channels have expectations stated as numbers, not hopes.
- A *launch calendar* has one objective per week, an owner, the asset each
  week requires, and a KPI per row.
- A *dashboard* names the decision each metric informs; a metric with no
  decision is cut.

**Artifact conventions.** How outputs are named, stored, and linked:

- One strategy document per product per market; campaign briefs named for
  the campaign (`focus-forge-launch.md`) and linked from the strategy.
- Every artifact carries version, date, and status in its frontmatter;
  strategies are versioned like code because they are revised like code.
- Rejected options stay in the document (positioning variants, channel
  trade-offs) — the reasons are the asset, and deletion invites relitigation.
- The calendar is the operational source of truth: if the strategy and the
  calendar disagree, the calendar wins and the strategy gets updated.
- Metrics definitions live in one place; a number redefined mid-flight is
  marked with the date of redefinition and why.

Everything the skill produces is a markdown file; nothing requires a special
toolchain, and a collaborator who has never seen this repository can execute
the calendar without asking questions.

## 04 — Workflow

The steps below are the full arc from product brief to measured launch. A
narrow job (a single campaign, a metrics audit) enters at the step that
matches the need and exits at step 12. Greenfield launches run the whole
list. Each step names its reference file; load the reference before doing
the step's work.

1. **Frame the product in STP terms.** Write the product overview and draft
   the first-pass segmentation: who has the pain, what the pain costs them,
   and which groups are reachable by channels you can actually work. Read
   `references/marketing-fundamentals.md` first — the STP structure governs
   every later step, and the 4Ps reframed for small teams set the working
   constraints.
2. **Run audience research before committing.** Six to ten interviews or a
   structured survey with the candidate segment: jobs-to-be-done, current
   workarounds, purchase history for adjacent tools. Read
   `references/audience-research.md`; apply the segment viability rubric
   and cut segments that fail it. Six real conversations beat a 2,000-word
   persona invented in an afternoon.
3. **Target: choose the segment(s).** Weigh pain intensity, reachability,
   purchase readiness, and competition. One primary segment, at most one
   secondary. Write the selection reasoning into the strategy document —
   the segment you did not choose will look attractive at week three, and
   the recorded reason is the antidote.
4. **Position: write the statement and kill the weak variants.** Draft
   three or more positioning statements using the template; pass each
   through the differentiation and credibility checks; keep the winner and
   record two rejected variants with reasons. Read
   `references/positioning-and-differentiation.md`. Check the category
   frame honestly: "the only X that Y" must be true, or it is a liability.
5. **Build the message hierarchy.** From the positioning, derive one core
   message, three to five supporting benefits ordered by buyer priority,
   and proof points for each. Read `references/messaging-frameworks.md`
   for the canvas and the hierarchy template. This artifact is the
   copywriter's input; hand it off with the audience's stage mapping.
6. **Score and select channels.** List every channel you can actually work,
   score each on audience fit, effort, cost, noise, and lifespan, and
   select the two or three that clear the bar. Read
   `references/channel-strategy.md`; the owned-earned-paid flywheel and the
   sequencing rules govern what starts first.
7. **Plan the launch campaign.** Using the campaign brief template, write
   objective, audience, core message, flights (week by week), assets each
   flight needs, and budget math that sums to the ceiling. Read
   `references/campaign-planning.md`; the "when to stop" rules are written
   into the brief, not decided later.
8. **Set the metrics dashboard.** Choose the five to seven metrics that
   will steer the launch, each with a target, a cadence, and the decision
   it informs. Read `references/marketing-metrics.md`; adopt its
   attribution humility — dashboards do not explain causality, they show
   where to look.
9. **Pre-launch rehearsal.** Walk the calendar: every asset needed in week
   one exists or has an owner and a date; the waitlist or audience touch
   works; tracking is instrumented before the first visitor. Fix the
   calendar rather than the launch if the rehearsal fails.
10. **Execute the launch weeks.** Run flights in order, holding each week's
    objective above the temptation of the interesting tangent. Log
    deviations in the calendar file the day they happen — a calendar nobody
    updates is fiction within a week.
11. **Read the dashboard and decide.** At each cadence point, compare
    actuals to targets, apply the recovery rules pre-agreed in step 8, and
    record each decision with its evidence. Shifts in budget or channel
    priority get a dated line in the strategy document.
12. **Post-launch review and strategy update.** Within a week of the
    launch window closing, write the channel-by-channel review: expected
    versus actual, what to keep, kill, or double. Update the strategy
    document version and schedule the next review. Read
    `references/strategy-case-studies.md` to compare your funnel behavior
    against a full worked launch before finalizing the review.

Steps 1-5 are thinking work with one-page outputs. Steps 6-8 are planning
work with executable artifacts. Steps 9-12 are execution and evidence work.
Most failed launches skipped step 2 — the interviews — and spent week one
learning what a Tuesday of research would have taught them.

**Emitted artifacts by step:**

| Step | Emits |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Product overview and first-pass segmentation draft |
| 2 | Audience research summary: JTBD, quotes, viability rubric scores |
| 3 | Targeting record: chosen segment(s) and the reasoning |
| 4 | Positioning statement with rejected variants |
| 5 | Message hierarchy with proof points |
| 6 | Channel scorecard and selected channel plan |
| 7 | Campaign brief with flights, assets, budget math |
| 8 | Metrics dashboard definition with recovery rules |
| 9 | Rehearsal checklist results and calendar fixes |
| 10 | Updated calendar with dated execution notes |
| 11 | Dashboard readings and decisions log |
| 12 | Post-launch review and versioned strategy update |

**Common entry points.** Not every job starts at step 1:

- *Greenfield launch:* start at 1, run all twelve.
- *Single campaign inside an existing strategy:* enter at 7 with the
  strategy's positioning and segments; exit at 8.
- *Funnel diagnosis:* enter at 8 with current analytics; walk the metrics
  reference's recovery rules before changing anything.
- *Repositioning:* enter at 4 with fresh audience research; loop 4 → 5 → 6.
- *Metrics overhaul:* enter at 8; exit at 11 with a working dashboard.

**Working rhythm.** The full arc runs in four working sessions:

- Session one: steps 1-3, ending in a chosen segment and recorded reasoning.
- Session two: steps 4-6, ending in positioning, message hierarchy, and
  channel plan — the strategy document's spine.
- Session three: steps 7-9, ending in a rehearsed campaign brief.
- Session four: steps 10-12, the launch window itself and its review.

The rhythm assumes nights-and-weekends effort from one person or a two-person
team; a funded small team compresses sessions one and two into a week.
What does not compress is the ordering: research before targeting,
positioning before messaging, messaging before channels, channels before
budget, budget before execution. Reversing any of those orders is how
marketing becomes gambling.

## 05 — Rules and Quality Bar

1. **Segment before channel.** Every channel decision names the segment it
   reaches and the message it carries. A channel chosen because it is
   available is not a strategy; it is a habit with a budget.
2. **Positioning names a real alternative.** "Unlike [alternative]" must be
   a product or habit the buyer actually compares you to. Positioning
   against a strawman convinces no one and reads as insecurity.
3. **The differentiation must be provable.** A claim the buyer cannot
   verify in the demo, the trial, or the first session is a wish, and
   wish-based positioning collapses at the first review.
4. **Six interviews minimum before targeting is locked.** Fewer than six
   is anecdote; more than fifteen before a pivot is procrastination. The
   audience reference's viability rubric decides, not enthusiasm.
5. **One primary segment per campaign.** A message aimed at everyone is
   filed by everyone. Secondary segments get adapted messages, not the
   same one shouted louder.
6. **Rejected variants are recorded, not deleted.** Positioning variants,
   channel trade-offs, and budget alternatives stay in the document with
   reasons — unrecorded decisions get relitigated by the founder at
   midnight, usually badly.
7. **Every campaign has one objective, one message, one audience, one
   budget line.** Multiple objectives inside one campaign means none is
   measured; split them into separate briefs instead.
8. **Budget math sums to the ceiling.** Every line item is justified
   against the objective it serves; a line without a decision attached is
   cut before launch, not after.
9. **Expectations are numbers.** Every selected channel carries an
   expected conversion or response range with its source — a hope is not
   an expectation, and a number without a source is marked as an estimate.
10. **The dashboard holds five to seven metrics, each with a decision.**
    Metrics that inform no decision are cut at definition time. Vanity
    numbers (follower counts, raw hits) do not enter the dashboard.
11. **Attribution stays humble.** Channel reports state what they can and
    cannot know; "Reddit drove 40% of revenue" requires instrumentation,
    not vibes — and last-click stories about word-of-mouth are fiction.
12. **Recovery rules are pre-agreed.** IF launch week underperforms, THEN
    [specific action] is written before launch. Deciding under emotion at
    week one is how strategies die of anxiety instead of evidence.
13. **The calendar is the operational truth.** If reality diverges, the
    calendar is updated the same day with a dated note. A stale calendar
    is a strategy cosplaying as a plan.
14. **Strategies are versioned.** Content changes bump the version and the
    date; the post-launch review produces a new version, not an edit
    history nobody can reconstruct.
15. **Small-team constraints are honored, not resented.** No channel is
    chosen that the team cannot work by hand for six consecutive weeks;
    a channel abandoned at week two costs more than one never started.
16. **Hand off at the boundary, with the artifact.** Copywriters receive
    the message hierarchy, not a paragraph of vibes; social operators
    receive channel expectations with numbers; the accountant receives
    budget math that sums. Every handoff names the open questions the
    receiver must answer.

## 06 — Worked Example

Scenario: **Focus Forge**, a $15 one-time-purchase desktop productivity app
by a solo developer, macOS and Windows. Hook: start a session with one
window and only that application stays open — everything else is locked
behind a wall. The full run below is expanded artifact-by-artifact in
`examples/worked-marketing-strategy.md`; this section shows the intermediate
artifacts and the reasoning between them.

**Steps 1-3 artifacts (STP).** Product overview written: single-task
window-locking app, $15, no subscription. First-pass segmentation from the
fundamentals reference: freelance writers (pain: context-switching breaks
flow; ~2M US; reachable via Reddit and indie blogs; have tried two or more
focus tools already), remote knowledge workers (~8M; reachable via Hacker
News and LinkedIn; need a trial), students (~11M; price ceiling below
$10), digital-minimalism enthusiasts (~100k; very high purchase intent;
buy first, ask questions later). Six pre-STP interviews (three freelance
writers, one content marketer, two remote startup staff) validated the
functional job — "write 1,500 words without switching to social media" —
and purchase readiness. Selected: freelance writers as primary, digital
minimalists as secondary word-of-mouth engine; students cut by the
viability rubric (low purchase intent at $15).

**Step 4 artifact (positioning).** Final statement: "For independent
knowledge workers fighting to stay focused for an hour, Focus Forge is the
$15 single-task app that blocks distractions not via a whitelist but by
locking everything but your session window — unlike Freedom or Cold Turkey,
you open one window and nothing else." Two variants were rejected and
recorded: "the only focus app that doesn't require a whitelist" (negative
positioning that presumes the buyer already knows why whitelists fail) and
"zero willpower required" (untrue — starting the session takes willpower,
and overpromising burns trust).

**Step 5 artifact (message hierarchy).** Core message: *one window, nothing
else.* Supporting benefits in buyer priority: (1) start a session in one
keystroke; (2) no whitelist to configure or maintain; (3) one-time payment,
yours forever; (4) works offline. Proof points: a 30-second demo video for
benefit 1, the settings screen showing zero configuration for benefit 2,
the license page for benefit 3. Handed to the copywriter with the message
hierarchy and stage-mapped variants (stranger, evaluator, buyer).

**Step 6 artifact (channel scorecard).** Candidate channels scored on fit,
effort, cost, noise, lifespan: Reddit (r/productivity,
r/freelanceWriters) 13/15, Hacker News 13/15, a build-in-public blog
12/15, Product Hunt 12/15, Twitter audience-building 9/15, paid Reddit
held as a boost lever only. Selected for launch: Reddit, Hacker News,
Product Hunt (earned), plus one owned channel (the pre-launch email list)
with a $50 tooling budget. Expectations written as ranges with sources:
Product Hunt launch day 0.5-3% visit-to-purchase; email list 5% to
purchase.

**Steps 7-8 artifacts (campaign brief and dashboard).** Six-week launch
calendar: W1 waitlist building via a blog post and Twitter thread (target
50 subscribers); W2 positioning test on r/productivity (20 comments,
sentiment check); W3 waitlist growth via indie forums and a build video
(150 subscribers cumulative); launch day on Product Hunt with everything
active (target: top-5 finish, 30-50 units); W5 email conversion push to
the waitlist ($3 discount, 3-6% conversion); W6 channel review. Budget
sums to $320: email tooling $50, launch discount cost $150, paid Reddit
test $100, graphics $20. Dashboard: 200 pre-launch email signups (weekly),
80+ PH upvotes (launch day), 30-50 launch-day units (day + 24h), CAC
below $10 (weekly), LTV:CAC above 2:1 (at 100 customers), top-three
channels by revenue (after week 4). Recovery rules pre-agreed: if launch
day delivers fewer than 10 units, shift the remaining paid budget to
Reddit and retest positioning with the r/productivity thread's language.

**Steps 9-12 artifacts (rehearsal, execution, review).** Rehearsal caught
two gaps: the PH listing's first comment was unwritten (owner assigned,
due two days early) and tracking lacked an email-attribution tag (fixed
before launch). Execution weeks were logged in the calendar with dated
notes; W2's positioning thread produced the phrase "window jail," which
was adopted into the message hierarchy on the spot — evidence beat
intuition. The post-launch review read the dashboard against targets,
found email underperforming its 5% estimate (1.8% actual) and Reddit
overperforming, reallocated the remaining $100 accordingly, bumped the
strategy document to version 04, and scheduled the next review for month
two. Unit economics: $320 spend against ~150 units at $15 yields a $2.13
CAC — well under the $10 ceiling.

**What the example demonstrates.** Research preceded targeting; targeting
was recorded against a named alternative; the rejected positioning
variants are still in the document doing their job (defending the
decision); every channel expectation was a number with a source; the
budget summed to a ceiling with a justification per line; recovery rules
existed before the data arrived; and one piece of launch-week evidence
changed the message without a meeting.

## 07 — Failure Modes and Recovery

1. **Channel before strategy.** Signal: the plan is a list of platforms
   with no named segment or message; "we should be on TikTok" arrives
   before "who is this for." Correction: return to steps 1-3, produce the
   segment and positioning, then re-score channels — the list usually
   shrinks by half. Prevention: the scorecard requires segment names per
   channel row.
2. **The invented persona.** Signal: the segment has a stock-photo name,
   a quote nobody said, and no reachable channel; "everyone needs this"
   appears in the strategy. Correction: run the audience reference's
   interview protocol and viability rubric; a segment that cannot name
   its pain in the buyer's words is not yet a segment.
3. **Aspirational positioning.** Signal: the statement could describe any
   product in the category; alternatives named are vague ("the old way")
   or the differentiator is unprovable. Correction: rerun step 4 with the
   credibility checks; write the rejected variants down; test the winner
   on the segment (a positioning thread, not a poll).
4. **Metrics as mood.** Signal: the dashboard has a dozen charts, none
   with a decision attached; meetings re-litigate feelings. Correction:
   cut to the five to seven decision-linked metrics from step 8; kill
   vanity metrics; give each survivor a target and a cadence.
5. **The abandoned channel.** Signal: three channels started in week one,
   one alive by week three; effort was underestimated. Correction: rerun
   the scorecard with honest effort scoring (hours per week, by hand);
   kill two channels deliberately and work one to its evaluation date.
   Prevention: rule 15 — no channel the team cannot work for six weeks.
6. **Launch-week panic.** Signal: mid-launch pivots — new pricing, new
   tagline, a discount nobody priced — driven by one bad day. Correction:
   read the pre-agreed recovery rules before acting; make changes on the
   cadence, not the emotion; log the decision in the calendar with its
   evidence. Prevention: the rules are written at step 8, when nobody is
   panicking.
7. **The stale calendar.** Signal: weeks three and four of the calendar
   no longer resemble reality; nobody can say what "done" looked like.
   Correction: rebuild the remaining calendar from what actually
   happened, dated; re-plan only the remaining flights; note the drift
   in the strategy document's version history.
8. **The budget that grew.** Signal: tool subscriptions and "small tests"
   have doubled the ceiling without a decision on record. Correction:
   freeze spend, list every line with its objective, cut lines with no
   decision attached, and re-baseline the strategy document's budget
   table with reasons and dates.

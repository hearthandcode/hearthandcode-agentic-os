# Positioning and Differentiation

Positioning is the third act of STP and the most individually important — it transforms market analysis into a decision about what the product means to its buyer. Differentiation is the set of product or service attributes that make the position credible.

## The positioning statement

A positioning statement is a single paragraph (ideally 1–2 sentences, never more than three) that answers: who are we for? what category do we compete in? what is our unique benefit? why should they believe us?

The standard template, adapted from Ries and Trout:

> For [target customer], [product name] is the [category frame / competitive alternative] that [single most important benefit] — unlike [primary competitor], we [key differentiator].

Write one positioning statement for every campaign and at least one for the product. Bid as blunt as possible. If a competitive alternative category frame stinks like a lie, do not force it — use a primary-benefit frame.

### Example (Focus Forge)

> For independent knowledge workers who struggle to context-switch in a distracted day, Focus Forge is the single-task desktop app that you pay for once and that blocks distraction with a strict one-rule mode — unlike Freedom or Cold Turkey, Focus Forge uses a singular-tab sandbox for each session, not a whitelist, so no toggling back to social media becomes a decision you cannot make.

### Functions of the positioning statement: 

1. **Gives directions to a single and message writers.** Every headline, social post, and campaign starts from here.
2. **Declares where you are not competing.** By choosing one position cross you out of others — this is painful but necessary. If the statement reads "we are for everyone," the positioning is not complete.
3. **Makes filtering obvious.** A reviewer or partner reading the statement can tell at a glance whether the product is relevant to them.

## Category design basics

Category design is the shift from "I would like to take share of an existing category" to "I would like to create a new mental category and own it." This matters most for genuinely novel products — something with no exact comparison.

### Three questions for the positioning: 

1. **Does this exist for my audience?** If the answer exists, you are entering an existing category. Compete on a differentiated promise within it.
2. **If it doesn't exist, is there a adjacent category?** Most innovation parks near an known category rather than creating a new one from nothing. A "single-task desktop app" sits near "focus app" but adds the sharp constraint of a single window — a tweak, not an invention.
3. **Do I have the resources to category create?** Category creation requires at least 18 months of sustained effort, multiple voices (reviews, press, talk), and a dramatically different product. Solo founders should rarely attempt it; instead push into an established category with a slot that reveals a failure in the existing category.

### The "plus slot" approach

Instead of creating a new category, pick an existing one and occupy a previously unreachable slot within it — cheaper, simpler, focused, more private, more vertical. The slot is a problem with the existing category that your product solves. This is usually the right tactic for solo indie developers.

Common slots:

| Existing category | Unowned slot | Example for a productivity app |
|-----------------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Focus app | Too expensive/too many features | "Do one thing: focus. No thirds." |
| Habit trackers | Too complicated | "The one-minute morning setup, reward counted." |
| Workforce tools | Too corporate | "Built for a freelance VPN, not a 500-person team." |

## Rejected positioning variants (with why)

Document why you threw a variant away. The reasoning is the durable artifact; the discarded wording is not.

**Rejected for Focus Forge:** "A focus app that doesn't sell your data."
- Why rejected: Privacy is not a differentiator in the focus-app category anymore. It is a baseline expectation. Leading with it makes the product sound like it has nothing else to say. Privacy is a dimension of trust, not a product promise.

**Rejected:** "The ultimate productivity tool."
- Why rejected: Vague, hyperbolic, and indistinguishable from a thousand other claims. "Ultimate" cannot be disproved and therefore carries no information. A strong position is falsifiable — if you say "one focused at a time," you commit to something someone can disagree with.

## Differentiation sources: 

Not all differentiators are created equal.

| Source | Credibility | Examples | Notes |
|--------|-------------|----------|-------|
| Product feature | High | Single window, above timer, works offline | Best for small teams; features are visible and testable in a trial |
| Business model | Medium | One-time purchase, no subscription | Often powerful for a $15 app targeting subscription fatigue |
| Audience focus | Medium | Designed for freelance writers only | Narrow, but deep; the narrower the audience the more precise the message |
| Customer experience | High | Setup in 10 seconds, no account needed | Difficult to maintain without a team |
| Brand voice | Low to medium | Witty, darkly, earnest | Easy to imitate but the hardest to prove; substitutes product differences only if consistent |

## Before-and-after positioning

Write the positioning sentence before doing any marketing work and again after the campaign. The difference reveals whether the product or the market or your reading of it changed.

## Checklist for positioning

- [ ] Is this positioning plausible for the target segment? Can a non-paying customer verify or immediately?
- [ ] Is it distinct from the top three alternatives (including doing nothing)?
- [ ] Could a competitor capture the same positioning without changing their product?
- [ ] Does the positioning statement name a category the customer given recognizes?
- [ ] Is the positioning backed by one concrete proof point?
- [ ] Would the target smilingly openly read this in a review of your product?

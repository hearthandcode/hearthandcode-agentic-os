# Planning Case Studies

Two fictional businesses, planned with the same workflow, exposing two different failure surfaces: a service business that dies quietly from concentration and unpriced time, and a product business that survives its launch by killing a feature early. Both start from real planning inputs; neither is a real company. Read them as calibration: what good intermediate planning artifacts look like, and what each saved or caught.

## Case 1: Harbor & Lane Editorial Services — service business

### The situation

Dana, a trade-book copyeditor, leaves a publishing house to freelance. Assets: twelve years of editing craft, a network of forty authors and two acquisition editors who owe her favors, $22,000 in savings, no business training. Constraint: family budget requires $4,000/month net personal income by month six, hard requirement from the household conversation.

### The model, before financials

Using the five questions (business-model-design): Who pays? — commissioning editors at small-to-mid trade houses and self-publishing authors; two very different buyers. What do they get? — manuscripts publishable on schedule, with a named editor and a fixed turnaround. Why her? — genre fluency and speed; proof exists in her portfolio and references. How do they pay? — per-word for books, monthly for a "manuscript pipeline" retainer she invents for two houses. What is left over? — per-word prices minus almost no direct cost; contribution margin ≈ 95 percent, but the founder-wage problem is the real issue: at $0.02/word and 12 pages/hour pace on developmental work, her effective rate is $24/hour before taxes — below the floor she needs.

The model interrogation surfaced the real decision: not "how much editing," but whether to sell hours, pages, or outcome ("publish-ready manuscripts") — and whether the retainer invention is sellable. Both become headline assumptions to test.

### Intermediate artifacts

**Assumption list (from assumption mapping):**

- A1 (high impact, high uncertainty): houses will buy a monthly pipeline retainer at $2,500. Test: pitch to two editors before resigning from the job. Result: one says yes at $2,000, one says no. Action: price retainers at $2,000, keep the offer.
- A2 (high impact, high uncertainty): self-published authors will pay $0.03/word. Test: quote five authors from the network. Result: two accept, three counter at $0.025. Action: $0.028/word for self-pub, $0.032 for houses (their budgets differ); kill condition recorded if close rate under 20 percent.
- A3 (high impact, low uncertainty): she can sustain 10 productive editing hours/day. Test: two weeks at the planned pace before launch. Result: no — quality degrades after 6 hours. Action: plan built on 6 hours/day; revenue capacity drops; the plan absorbs this instead of discovering it in month four.

**First-pass financial sketch (annualized, condensed):** fixed costs $6,200 (software, accounting, insurance, professional memberships); founder income target $57,600 ($4,000 × 12 net); taxes provision 35 percent; billable capacity 6 h/day × 5 days × 46 weeks ≈ 1,380 hours. Cost floor: ($57,600 + taxes ~$20,200 + $6,200) / 1,380 ≈ $61/hour — meaning per-word prices must yield at least $61/hour of editing time, which the $0.028 self-pub rate fails on developmental work (≈$34/hour). Decision: developmental editing is priced per-page for houses only; self-pub work is copyediting (faster, ≈$63/hour effective) — a wedge correction the arithmetic forced.

**Break-even and runway:** founder-level break-even ≈ ($57,600 + $20,200 + $6,200) / 0.95 ≈ $88,400/year ≈ $7,400/month billable. Cash: $22,000 reserve; burn ≈ $4,500/month until income ramps; first retainer cash lands month two. Trough ≈ $9,000 at month four without any project work — thin, so she takes a 3-month contract bridge at reduced hours, planned deliberately rather than stumbled into.

**Quarterly milestones (draft):** Q1: resign; first retainer live; 3 self-pub clients. Q2: retainer #2; raise house per-word rate for new clients. Q3: subcontractor for overflow proof-of-concept. Q4: decide — subcontractor retained or capacity capped.

**Risk register (top rows):** client concentration — one house is 55 percent of planned revenue → cap at 40 percent, pitch second house before launch (kill-class, likely/severe). Income floor miss — early signal: two consecutive months under $5,000 billable → trigger household conversation with a written plan (kill-class). Burnout — the 6-hour discovery — buffer days after each manuscript (friction-class, possible/moderate).

### Outcome and what planning caught

Year one (fictional outcome): $86,000 revenue, $51,000 founder income, two retainers at 38 percent of revenue. The plan caught three things that instinct would have missed: the developmental-editing pricing failure (found by cost-floor arithmetic before it was found by poverty), the 6-hour capacity limit (found by a two-week test instead of a quarter of guilt), and the concentration cap that forced the second-house pitch before resigning. What planning did not catch: a month-eight payment dispute with a self-pub author who disputed 30 percent of an invoice — no signed agreement template existed; the fix cost a week and produced the contract template now used before any work begins.

## Case 2: Fieldnote Trail Supply — product business

### The situation

Priya and Sam, two product designers, build a durable trail-journal notebook with a weatherproof cover and a guided page system. Assets: $30,000 combined savings, a prototype, 3,000 combined newsletter subscribers, full-time jobs they plan to leave within a year. Constraint: neither quits until monthly profit pays 60 percent of a salary.

### The model and the market check

Buyers: long-distance hikers and outdoor-gift purchasers. The market check (bottom-up): 3,000 subscribers, 12 percent visit the product page, 8 percent of those pre-order at $34 → 29 pre-orders ≈ $986 — brutal, and clarifying: the newsletter cannot carry the launch alone. Wedge chosen: pre-orders through two outdoor-gear micro-influencer collaborations (their audiences overlap the newsletter) plus one retail account at a local gear shop. Expansion path: region → adjacent categories (maps, cases).

### Intermediate artifacts

**Assumption tests and results:**

- B1 (high impact, high uncertainty): enough pre-orders to justify a 500-unit first run. Falsification condition: fewer than 150 pre-orders in 45 days kills the in-house production plan, triggers a print-on-demand fallback. Result: 187 pre-orders in 45 days — survive, but the margin math changes (see below).
- B2: cost per unit under $12 at 500 units. Test: three manufacturer quotes. Result: $11.40 at 500 units, $8.90 at 2,000. Action: first run sized to 500; requote at 2,000 only after two successful quarters.
- B3: the guided page system is the reason people buy. Test: pre-order page A/B (two versions, same traffic). Result: the weatherproof cover converted at 9.1 percent, the guided system at 4.3 percent. Action: reposition the offer around the cover; demote the guided system to a free insert — a feature killed by one cheap test.

**Financial sketch (first year, condensed):** COGS $11.40/unit; price $34 → contribution $22.60/unit before fees; shipping and payment fees ≈ $4.10/unit → net unit contribution ≈ $18.50. Fixed: tools, website, insurance, trade-show fees ≈ $9,600/year. Break-even ≈ 520 units/year. Cash timing dominates: pre-order cash funds the production order; the risk is timing mismatch between deposit and manufacturer's payment schedule (50 percent down required). Runway rule adopted: never commit more than 60 percent of collected cash to a production order.

**Quarterly milestones:** Q1: pre-order campaign, order production at ≥150 units. Q2: fulfillment + retail shop account; measure return/refund rate. Q3: second product (trail map folio) smoke test only. Q4: wholesale margin decision — at wholesale price $20 (retail $34), unit contribution halves; requires either volume or a price bump to $38.

**Risk register (top rows):** cash-timing mismatch on production deposits (kill, possible/severe) → 60-percent rule + manufacturer deposit schedule negotiated to 30/40/30. Single-batch quality failure (kill, unlikely/severe) → sample inspection gate before full run; supplier's second reference checked. Demand spike they cannot fill (friction, possible/moderate) → waitlist, not sold-out silence. The founders' dual employment (kill, likely/severe in year one) → no resignation until 60-percent rule met for three consecutive months; the calendar carries this check.

### Outcome and what planning caught

Year one: 780 units sold, revenue ≈ $26,500, modest profit, both still employed — which the plan treats as success, not failure, because the resignation rule was explicit. The A/B test killed the wrong-hero feature before it was manufactured into 500 notebooks; the 60-percent cash rule caught what would have been a fatal deposit mismatch in month five; the wholesale math was surfaced in Q4 planning instead of discovered at the first retail pitch, in time to plan the $38 retail test.

## What both cases share

- The decisions that mattered were made on intermediate artifacts — assumption logs, cost-floor arithmetic, A/B results — not on the final documents.
- Every plan carried a written resignation/commitment rule, a concentration cap, and a cash rule; those three lines saved both businesses more than any market research.
- Both revised the offer before launch on test evidence, and neither regretted it.
- Both plans were wrong in ways only monthly scorecards could catch — and the scorecards existed.

## Lessons extracted

1. Derive prices from your cost floor before polishing them against the market; the floor is where plans fail first.
2. Test capacity limits on your own body and calendar before selling against them.
3. Concentration caps and cash-timing rules do more for survival than growth projections.
4. Cheap commitment tests (pre-orders, quoted prices, deposits) beat opinion collection at every decision point.
5. Write commitment rules (when to quit a job, when to order inventory) into the plan with triggers — future-you is a different decision-maker than present-you, and not a better one.

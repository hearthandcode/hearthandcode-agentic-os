# Goal Setting and Metrics

A business plan without a measurement layer is a wish with a budget. This document covers choosing a North Star metric, complementing it with input metrics, setting a quarterly cadence that actually runs, and interpreting metric movement without fooling yourself. It is written for businesses where one person can see all the numbers — where the risk is not too little data but measuring the wrong things and reading them wrongly.

## What makes a good North Star

A North Star metric is the single number that best represents the value your business delivers, stated at a cadence you can influence monthly or quarterly. Tests for a good one:

1. **Value-reflecting.** It moves when customers genuinely benefit, not when you merely work more. Billable hours move when you work; *retainer revenue* moves when clients keep finding the work worth paying for.
2. **Leading enough.** It predicts near-term health. Cash this quarter is a lagging echo of agreements signed earlier; signed-agreement value is leading.
3. **Moveable.** Your actions can move it within a quarter. If nothing you could do this month changes the number, it is a scoreboard, not a steering metric.
4. **Simple.** One number, defined in one sentence, computable from records you already keep. If it needs a methodology document, it will not survive a busy month.

Common North Star shapes by business type: signed monthly agreement value (services), retained-client revenue (studios), active licensed seats (products), monthly recurring revenue (subscriptions), published-and-used artifacts (content businesses). Note these are all *commitment* or *usage* quantities, not activity quantities — that is the pattern.

## The trap of vanity metrics

Vanity metrics are numbers that only go up: followers, total page views, cumulative "impressions," hours worked, total proposals sent. They fail the tests above — they do not reflect delivered value and they cannot be steered — and their real cost is subtler than embarrassment:

- They crowd out the two or three numbers that would change decisions.
- They create the feeling of progress during weeks of drift.
- They invite optimizing for the audience that reads the number, not the customer who pays.

The corrective habit is not to banish every flattering number but to keep each vanity metric tied to a paired conversion metric: followers → inbound conversations; page views → proposals requested; impressions → replies.

## North Star plus input metrics

One number steers; input metrics explain and enable. The working structure:

- **North Star (one):** the value delivered, e.g., monthly recurring revenue.
- **Input metrics (three to five):** the levers that drive it, e.g., proposals sent, proposal win rate, average project size, retainer agreements live.
- **Health metrics (two to three):** things that should stay in safe ranges: utilization, average collection days, minimum cash balance, founder hours.

The relationships form a tree: North Star at the root, inputs as branches, each input assignable to specific weekly actions. When the North Star misses, you diagnose downward: which input missed, and which action should have moved that input. When it beats, you diagnose the same way to learn what worked.

### The metric tree for a small design studio (illustrative)

- North Star: retainer + signed-project revenue per month.
- Inputs: proposals sent per month; win rate; average project value; retainer agreements live.
- Health: utilization (target 60–70 percent); days to payment (target under 35); minimum cash (never below one month of burn); revision rounds per project (target ≤ 2).

Write the tree at the top of the one-page plan. Every quarterly review starts from it.

## The quarterly cadence

A cadence that runs beats a sophisticated one that does not. For a small business, three rhythms suffice:

- **Monthly, 60–90 minutes:** update the scorecard; compare actuals to plan; pick at most two corrective actions; log them with owners (you) and dates.
- **Quarterly, half a day:** re-derive the North Star tree if the model changed; set the next quarter's input targets from the arithmetic of the North Star target; retire metrics nobody looked at; do a written pre-mortem on the biggest upcoming bet.
- **Annually, one day:** full plan refresh — market analysis, model, financials, risks. The annual plan is a revision, not a rewrite: keep the history so you can see your own forecasting error.

Two cadence rules: never add a metric without retiring one, and never hold a metrics review without the actuals visible — from records, not memory. For a solo founder, "the meeting with yourself" is real: put it in the calendar, prepare the scorecard the day before, write the decisions down.

## Target setting without theater

Targets should be derived, then tempered. Derivation first: if the annual North Star target is $110,000 of revenue and the average project is $7,000 with a 40 percent win rate, you need roughly 39 projects proposed → about 16 won. That is the arithmetic that sets the input targets: proposals per quarter, win rate to maintain, pipeline needed. Tempering second: apply a haircut for ramp (the first quarter always underperforms) and a stretch only on inputs you control, never on outcomes you do not.

Rules of thumb:

- One measurable target per input metric; more dilutes attention.
- Targets you do not fully control get ranges, not points.
- Every target carries its "why this number" note in one line; unexplained targets get gamed or ignored.

## Reading movement without fooling yourself

- **Small numbers swing.** With five projects a quarter, one win moves the win rate 20 points. Judge trends across two quarters, and track absolute counts alongside rates.
- **Seasonality is real.** Many service businesses have slow Augusts and Decembers. Compare to the same quarter last cycle, or plan the dip explicitly instead of calling it failure.
- **Correlation is not causation.** Inquiries rose after you raised prices — or after the referral campaign. If you changed two things at once, you have learned nothing; change one at a time when the stakes justify it.
- **Watch the denominator.** Win rate without proposal count is noise; revenue per client without client count is worse. Report pairs, not fractions alone.
- **Lag awareness.** A sales action today shows up in revenue next quarter. Measure actions weekly, results quarterly; do not fire the tactic after three weeks.

## The one-page scorecard

Keep a single page with, per month: North Star actual vs target; each input metric actual vs target; each health metric actual vs safe range; and two lines of notes — what was tried, what was learned. Historical pages accumulate; the trend across pages is the business's autobiography. When the scorecard shows three consecutive misses on the same input, that is not a motivation problem — it is a model problem, and the correct response is to revisit the assumption behind the target, not to push harder on the same lever.

## Failure modes

- **Metric soup.** Twelve tracked numbers, no decisions — prevented by the retire-one rule.
- **North Star drift.** Changing the definition mid-year to make trends look better — prevented by writing the definition once and versioning changes.
- **Activity worship.** Counting effort (posts sent, hours billed) instead of movement — prevented by the vanity/conversion pairing.
- **Target amnesia.** Hitting a target nobody can explain — prevented by the "why this number" note.
- **Review theater.** Meetings that report numbers and decide nothing — prevented by the two-corrective-actions-maximum monthly rule.

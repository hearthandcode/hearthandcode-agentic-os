# Market Analysis

Market analysis for a small business is not a research report; it is a set of decisions — who to serve, where to enter, what to charge — each backed by the cheapest evidence you can get. This document covers honest market sizing, competitor mapping, and wedge selection at the scale where a handful of customers is the whole market.

## Why small-scale market analysis is different

A large company estimates markets in billions; a solo studio needs to find thirty customers. At small scale, the method inverts:

- Bottom-up arithmetic beats top-down percentages, always.
- A few real conversations outweigh any purchased report.
- The goal is not precision; it is direction plus a falsifiable number you can be wrong about.

Keep the analysis proportional: a week of focused work is enough for a one-person business plan. Anything longer is usually procrastination dressed as diligence.

## Sizing honestly: TAM, SAM, SOM without the theater

The standard funnel — total market, serviceable market, obtainable market — is useful only if each layer is derived, not asserted. At small scale, spend your effort on the bottom layer:

- **TAM** (category spend) earns one sentence, cited, with its source named. It proves the pond exists, nothing more.
- **SAM** (the part you can serve) is a filter: geography, segment, budget. One or two sentences of criteria.
- **SOM** (what you can realistically win in year one) is the only number that drives decisions. Derive it bottom-up.

### Bottom-up arithmetic (the only kind that matters here)

Formula: reachable buyers × realistic close rate × average first-year value.

- Reachable buyers: people you can actually contact or reach through a channel you operate, in a year. Not "everyone on the internet."
- Realistic close rate: 10–30 percent of qualified conversations for a warm-referral service business; lower for cold outbound; higher for inbound with intent. Justify your pick from evidence, even thin evidence.
- Average first-year value: price × expected units in year one, per customer.

Worked fictional example: a freelance brand designer with 150 reachable contacts (former colleagues and their networks), a 20 percent conversation-to-proposal rate, a 40 percent proposal-to-close rate, and $7,000 average first-year value: 150 × 0.20 × 0.40 = 12 proposals → 4.8 → ~5 clients × $7,000 = $35,000 from the warm network alone. The arithmetic exposes the real constraint: the network, not the market. That is a planning insight, not a statistic.

### TAM honesty red flags

- "One percent of a $10B market" — the percentage is doing all the work; delete it.
- Confusing category spend with addressable spend (all marketing spend is not your market).
- Citing a report's growth rate as if it accrued to you.
- A year-one target larger than your delivery capacity — capacity caps SOM before the market does.

## Competitor mapping

You have three kinds of competition, and the third is usually the largest:

- **Direct:** others selling a similar outcome to the same buyer. List the five a buyer would actually compare, not the famous ones.
- **Indirect:** different shapes solving the same problem — an agency's junior team, a template product, a freelancer marketplace.
- **Status quo ("do nothing"):** the buyer keeps tolerating the problem. This competitor wins by default and never shows up in a competitor list.

Build a simple table: alternative | what it costs the buyer | where it is weak | what you must prove to win. The last column becomes your positioning to-do list. For the status quo row, the honest entry for "where it is weak" is usually "pains are real but tolerable" — which tells you your offer must reduce pain or add gain visibly enough to justify switching.

## Demand signals worth collecting

- Search and marketplace volume for the problem's words, not your solution's name.
- Repeating questions in communities where buyers already gather.
- Competitor price increases and hiring in the segment — both signal willingness to pay.
- Requests for proposals, job posts, and tool budgets in the segment.
- Commitment signals: deposits, paid pilots, signed letters of intent, pre-orders.

Distinguish interest from commitment. "I'd love this" is interest; money paid or time scheduled is commitment. Weight signals accordingly, and log each signal with its date and source so the plan can cite it.

## Wedge selection

A wedge is the deliberately narrow entry point: the smallest credible market where you can win first and expand from. Test a candidate wedge against four criteria:

1. **Underserved.** Incumbents treat it as too small or too odd to serve well.
2. **Reachable.** You have a repeatable way to the first ten buyers — a channel you can operate, not a hope.
3. **Winnable.** You can be credibly better at one thing that matters to this segment, and prove it.
4. **Expandable.** Adjacent segments are reachable with the same or a lightly adapted offer.

Wedge discipline includes writing down the wedge you are not taking, and the conditions under which you would revisit it. Otherwise every attractive distraction reopens the decision monthly.

## Primary research on a small budget

- **Problem interviews (10–20).** Ask about past behavior and money already spent, never about intent. "What did you do about this last quarter?" beats "would you buy this?"
- **Pricing tests.** Quote concrete numbers in real proposal contexts and record reactions; surveys about price produce fiction.
- **Smoke tests.** A page with a real offer and a deposit or a dated waitlist. The commitment is the data.
- **Concierge pilots.** Deliver the service manually to three buyers before productizing anything.

Two rules govern all of it: never ask "would you buy," and never let a single enthusiastic friend count as validation. Three independent strangers who paid beat thirty who nodded.

## Deliverables of a good small-scale market analysis

1. A bottom-up market size with the arithmetic shown, not just the result.
2. A competitor map with the status quo row filled in.
3. A named wedge, its expansion path, and the wedge you declined.
4. A demand-signal log with dates and sources.
5. A list of assumptions this analysis could not verify — this list feeds assumption testing, which is the next stage of planning.

## Common failure modes

- **Report theater.** Pages of cited statistics and no bottom-up number.
- **Market myopia.** Sizing the whole category while ignoring that your capacity caps year one.
- **Competitor envy.** Studying famous players instead of the five the buyer actually compares.
- **Wedge drift.** Refusing to narrow, therefore being nothing specific to anyone.
- **Survey validation.** Converting polite hypotheticals into "the market wants this."

# Business Model Design

A business model is the set of choices that determines who pays you, for what, why they pay you instead of an alternative, and whether anything survives costs. It comes before financial planning: a revenue projection built on an unexamined business model is arithmetic standing on hope. This document covers the working canvas, the difference between creating and capturing value, and the unit logic that keeps projections honest.

## The five questions every model must answer

Before filling in any template, force written answers to these. Vague answers here predict vague businesses.

1. **Who pays?** Name a specific buyer — a role, an organization type, a budget owner. "Small businesses" is not an answer; "operations leads at 10–40 person logistics companies" is.
2. **What do they get?** The outcome they buy, in their words. Clients buy a working site and relief from the decisions, not "design services."
3. **Why you, and not the alternatives?** The alternatives include competitors, in-house effort, and doing nothing. Your answer must survive the question "why not the cheapest acceptable option?"
4. **How do they pay?** The shape of payment: one-time, recurring, per unit, milestone-based. Shape drives cash timing as much as amount.
5. **What is left over?** Price minus direct cost, per unit. If this number is small or unknown, growth makes things worse, not better.

## The nine building blocks

Work the canvas in this order — customer first, costs last. The order matters because earlier blocks constrain later ones.

1. **Customer segments.** Who has the problem, the budget, and the urgency. For a solo or small firm, pick one primary segment; two is a stretch, three is a mistake.
2. **Value proposition.** The specific outcome, the timeframe, and the reason to believe. "Rebrand in six weeks, fixed fee, with a portfolio of comparable work" beats "quality design."
3. **Channels.** How customers find you and how you find them: referrals, outbound, marketplaces, content, partnerships. Match effort to the channel that actually converts, not the one that feels productive.
4. **Customer relationships.** Hand-held project work, self-serve purchase, or an ongoing retainer relationship. This choice sets your workload shape as much as your revenue shape.
5. **Revenue streams.** What each segment pays for, at what price, on what terms. Separate one-time from recurring lines in every table you build from here on.
6. **Key resources.** What you must have: skills, tools, a portfolio, certifications, a cash buffer. Name the ones that are scarce — those are your real moat, if any.
7. **Key activities.** What you must do repeatedly: selling, delivering, quality control, bookkeeping. Estimate hours per unit of sale; this feeds capacity math later.
8. **Key partnerships.** Suppliers, subcontractors, referral sources, platforms. Ask what each partner gets from the deal; one-sided partnerships do not persist.
9. **Cost structure.** Fixed versus variable, one-time versus recurring. Cost structure determines break-even, so treat it as a design choice, not an afterthought.

## Value creation versus value capture

Creating value is making someone materially better off. Capturing value is being paid a fair share of the improvement. They are independent variables: you can create enormous value and capture none of it (free work), or capture well while creating little (which ends at the first renewal conversation).

Common capture failures in small businesses:

- Free pilots framed as marketing that quietly become the product.
- Hourly billing that caps income at available hours and punishes efficiency.
- Underpricing the second client because the first was a friend or a favor.
- Bundling unlimited revisions into a fixed fee with no change process.
- Retainers priced below the real cost of the responsiveness they promise.

The corrective habit: after every engagement, write down the value the client received — revenue enabled, cost saved, risk removed, time recovered — and compare it to what they paid. If the ratio is more than roughly five to one, your price is a negotiation opening, not a ceiling.

## Unit logic

Every business has a unit — the thing you multiply to get revenue. Services sell hours, projects, seats, or retainers. Products sell items, subscriptions, or licenses. You must be able to state, for one unit:

- **Price:** what one unit sells for.
- **Direct cost:** what one unit costs to deliver — contractors, materials, transaction fees, support time.
- **Contribution:** price minus direct cost, the amount left to cover fixed costs and profit.

If you cannot state these three numbers for your main unit, you do not yet have a business model; you have an intention. Write the numbers down even as estimates, and mark how confident you are in each.

Unit-logic traps:

- Blending units. Retainer revenue and project revenue follow different math and different risk. Keep separate lines everywhere.
- Excluding your own hours. Price your own time at a market rate inside the cost side, or your "profit" is unpaid wages in disguise.
- Averaging across a mix. A blended margin hides which line carries the business and which one quietly subsidizes it.

## Recurring versus one-time revenue

Recurring revenue buys stability and predictability; one-time revenue buys larger tickets and variety. Durable small businesses usually blend them deliberately:

- One-time work funds today, but it stops the moment selling stops.
- Recurring work compounds: each new agreement raises the floor under next month.
- Scope retainers to a defined, renewable set of deliverables — never "whatever comes up."

A useful planning ratio: decide what fraction of revenue you want recurring within a year — for many service studios, 20–40 percent — then engineer an offer that gets you there.

## Patterns worth borrowing

- **Productized service.** Fixed scope, fixed price, fixed duration. Trades customization for repeatability, faster sales, and easier delegation.
- **Care plans and retainers.** A monthly fee for a defined maintenance relationship. Smooths cash and creates a listening post for follow-on work.
- **Value-based project pricing.** Price against the client's economics rather than your hours. Requires discovery discipline and confidence.
- **Fractional roles.** Renting senior expertise a few days a month. High effective rates; calendar fragmentation is the cost.
- **Templates and licensing.** Selling the same artifact many times. Scales without hours but usually needs an audience first.

Each pattern is a trade, not an upgrade. Name what you are giving up when you adopt one.

## Interrogation checklist

Review any draft model against these questions. A "no" or "don't know" is a to-do, not a dealbreaker — but it must be written down.

1. Can the buyer pay without a committee or a budget cycle you do not understand?
2. Is there a repeatable way to reach the next ten customers, or only the first one?
3. Does the unit contribution survive a 20 percent cost overrun or a 10 percent price cut?
4. Would the model still work if your largest current relationship disappeared tomorrow?
5. What would make a customer switch away from you within six months?
6. Is any revenue line dependent on work you personally cannot scale?
7. Do the payment terms protect cash — deposits, short payment windows — or extend free credit to clients?

## Failure modes this methodology prevents

- **Hope-based projections.** Revenue lines with no named buyer and no path — prevented by the five questions.
- **Marginless growth.** More volume, no more money — prevented by unit logic.
- **Single-point pricing.** One client at 60 percent of revenue with no plan B — surfaced by the interrogation checklist.
- **The service trap.** A business that only earns when the founder works — surfaced by the recurring-versus-one-time ratio.

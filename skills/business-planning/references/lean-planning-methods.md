# Lean Planning Methods

A full plan is a commitment device; a lean plan is a learning device. This document covers the one-page plan, assumption mapping and testing, the build-measure-learn loop applied to business planning, and pivot decisions made on evidence instead of mood. Use it to keep planning cheap while the model is still uncertain — and to know when the model has earned a fuller plan.

## Plans and experiments are different documents

A plan commits resources against assumptions; an experiment tests the assumptions cheaply before resources commit. The failure pattern in early-stage planning is writing the full plan first and defending it thereafter, so evidence that contradicts it feels like defeat instead of progress. The lean sequence inverts this:

1. Draft the model as assumptions, not conclusions.
2. Identify the two or three assumptions whose failure would kill the plan.
3. Test those first, cheaply, in the real world.
4. Only after the riskiest assumptions survive, write the fuller plan.

The sequence does not replace planning — it changes what kind of plan you are allowed to write at each stage. Testing first earns a confident plan; skipping testing produces a plan that must be defended emotionally, which is how businesses die politely.

## The one-page plan

A one-page plan is a working document, not a summary. Its sections, roughly in fill order:

1. **Identity line:** what the business is, one sentence, plain words.
2. **Customer:** the primary segment and the wedge, with the expansion path.
3. **Offer:** what you sell — named products or service lines with prices.
4. **Why you win:** the one thing you can credibly be best at, and the proof you have.
5. **Model math:** unit economics in one line — price, direct cost, contribution.
6. **Revenue goal:** the year-one number and its bottom-up derivation.
7. **Milestones:** the next 90 days, as dated outcomes (not activities).
8. **Key assumptions:** the 3–5 that would change everything if wrong, each with its test and test date.
9. **Risks:** the top three, each with a trigger and a response.
10. **Metrics:** the North Star and the three input metrics on the scorecard.

The discipline of the page is compression. If a section will not fit, the underlying thinking is unfinished — the page is a diagnostic before it is a document. Rewrite the page monthly in the first six months; version it (v1, v2, v3 in a corner) so the evolution is visible.

What belongs on the page is decisions; what belongs behind the page is evidence: interview notes, quotes, market arithmetic. The page cites its evidence; it does not contain it.

## Assumption mapping

List every belief your plan depends on, then score each on two axes:

- **Impact of being wrong:** would the plan still work if this were false?
- **Uncertainty:** how confident are you, honestly, today?

The two dimensions give four cells:

- **High impact, high uncertainty — test first.** These are the plan's actual foundations.
- **High impact, low uncertainty — protect.** Watch them; do not spend test money on them.
- **Low impact, high uncertainty — note and defer.** Cheap to be wrong about.
- **Low impact, low uncertainty — ignore.** Spend nothing here.

Most planning failure is misallocation across this grid: elaborate tests of low-impact unknowns while the make-or-break beliefs are asserted, not tested.

### Testing assumptions cheaply

Match the test type to the assumption, cheapest first:

- **Interview:** a belief about what customers do or value. Ten conversations, past behavior only.
- **Smoke test:** a belief about whether anyone will pay. A real offer, a real price, a real commitment (deposit or dated waitlist). No commitment, no data.
- **Pilot:** a belief about delivery — that you can deliver the promised quality at the promised cost. Sell one unit at full price before building anything scalable.
- **Data pull:** a belief about market size or competitor behavior. An hour with records and public information, not a month.
- **Time-boxed operational test:** a belief about yourself — that you can sustain the selling cadence or the delivery hours. Two weeks at the planned rate, logged honestly.

Set a falsification condition before running any test: "this assumption is wrong if fewer than X of the ten interviews mention the problem unprompted." Writing the kill condition first is what keeps tests honest; deciding afterward what would have counted always finds a reason to continue.

### Recording results

Keep an assumption log: assumption | why it matters | test | falsification condition | result | action. The log is the memory of the planning process and the antidote to the most expensive habit in early business: quietly re-deciding the same question every quarter because no one recorded the last answer.

## The build-measure-learn loop, applied to planning

- **Build:** make the smallest real version of the offer — a productized service description with a price, a landing page, a pilot engagement.
- **Measure:** the commitment metrics — proposals requested, deposits paid, delivery cost versus estimate. Interest metrics (clicks, compliments) are context, not results.
- **Learn:** compare to the falsification conditions; update the assumption log; revise the one-page plan.

Cycle length matters: two to four weeks per loop in a service business. If a loop takes a quarter, the tests are too big; if a week, they may be too flimsy to trust. The loop is the unit of progress; the plan is the record of what the loops have taught so far.

## Pivots: kinds, signals, and mechanics

A pivot is a structured change to one model component while keeping the rest, not a random restart. Common kinds, with their signals:

- **Customer segment pivot.** Same offer, different buyer. Signal: proposals land well in one segment and die in another, repeatedly.
- **Offer pivot.** Same buyer, different deliverable. Signal: you keep getting hired for the add-on, not the headline service.
- **Channel pivot.** Same offer and buyer, different way of reaching them. Signal: referrals convert at triple the rate of outbound; follow the arithmetic.
- **Revenue model pivot.** Projects to retainers, custom to productized. Signal: cash timing makes the current shape unrunnable even when demand exists.
- **Zoom-in pivot.** One piece of a broader offer becomes the whole business. Signal: the piece everyone praises is the one nobody pays for yet.

Mechanics: define the pivot hypothesis ("segment X buys offer Y for reason Z"), the evidence that triggered it, what stays constant, the falsification condition for the new configuration, and a decision date — usually 60–90 days out. A pivot without a decision date is just churn with a vocabulary.

Never pivot in the trough of a bad month; pivot on a scheduled review with the scorecard in front of you. Mood is not data.

## When to graduate from lean to full planning

Move to a fuller plan when the model has survived its riskiest assumptions and the constraint shifts from "is this viable" to "how do I run it": repeatable sales, stable unit economics, a pipeline beyond the founder's personal network. The graduation test: three consecutive months where the scorecard's misses were operational (capacity, delivery, collections) rather than existential (demand, model). Existential misses mean more testing; operational misses mean more planning. Until then, the one-page plan, the assumption log, and the scorecard are the entire planning stack — and that is enough.

# Stakeholder Communication

A plan that lives in a drawer is a diary; a plan that runs through structured updates becomes an operating agreement between the business and everyone it depends on. This document covers the stakeholder map, update formats and rhythms, making asks cleanly, expectation management, and the communication aspects of bad news and crises. It is written for the small-business case where the "stakeholders" are few and personal: clients, collaborators, a partner or family sharing financial risk, a lender, a mentor.

## Map the stakeholders before writing anything

For each stakeholder, write four things:

1. **What they need from the business.** Outcomes, returns, certainty, involvement — in their terms, not yours.
2. **What they fear.** Every stakeholder has a loss scenario; the update should quietly address it.
3. **Their decision rights.** What they can veto, what they merely want visibility into.
4. **The right cadence.** Weekly, monthly, quarterly — and the medium: a call, a one-pager, a dashboard.

Typical small-business map: clients (fear scope chaos and missed dates; need status and reliability), subcontractors (fear payment risk and vague briefs; need clear scopes and prompt payment), a spouse or partner sharing finances (fear unbounded downside; need honest runway numbers and a fixed review rhythm), a bank or lender (fear default; needs covenant compliance and early warning), mentors or advisors (fear wasted advice; need to see whether their input was used).

Two kinds of stakeholders get special treatment: those who can end the business unilaterally (the 45-percent client, the lender) and those whose information advantage over you is real (a good accountant). Over-communicate with the first class, systematize contact with the second.

## The update discipline

Structure beats eloquence. A monthly stakeholder update, one page or five minutes, in four blocks:

1. **Headline:** one sentence — on track / off track / changed, against the number they care about.
2. **Progress:** two or three outcomes shipped since the last update, stated as finished things ("the site went live"), not activities ("worked on the site").
3. **Numbers:** the two or three metrics the stakeholder actually tracks — revenue versus plan for a partner; milestones for a client; runway for a lender. Same metrics every time, in the same order, so drift is visible across updates.
4. **Asks and flags:** what you need from them and what could go wrong next month. Asks get owners and dates; flags get a sentence of trigger ("if the second project slips, delivery moves to...").

The pattern that separates professional from amateur updates: consistency of shape. When the format is stable, a missing number is as loud as a wrong number. Varying the format to bury a miss is the most common amateur tell, and stakeholders who read three consecutive updates will notice.

## Making asks cleanly

An ask is a request for a bounded action with a deadline, not a hint or a status disguised as a question. Rules:

- **One ask per message.** Stacked asks get partially done; a lone ask gets done.
- **Name the date and the shape.** "Can you introduce me to X by Friday?" not "let me know if you hear anything."
- **Say what happens without it.** A plan B makes the ask easy to grant or decline honestly; missing plan Bs pressure people.
- **Make declining cheap.** "A one-line no is a fine answer" gets you more truth than silence would.

Asks to stakeholders are also a leading indicator: a founder who cannot make clean asks of clients, collaborators, or family will run a business that starves quietly between failures.

## Expectation management

Most stakeholder conflict is a difference between the promised and the delivered, discovered late. The machinery of expectation management is boring and effective:

- **Under-promise on dates, over-deliver on communication.** Quote the deadline you can hit on a bad week, then hit it.
- **State scope in writing, including what is excluded.** The excluded list prevents more conflict than the included list.
- **Announce changes before they are felt.** A price change announced a quarter ahead reads as professionalism; sprung at invoice, it reads as breach.
- **Give ranges with reasons.** "Delivery in 4–6 weeks; the range covers client feedback turnaround, which is the part I don't control."
- **Never let silence imply good news.** If you have not updated in a while, the stakeholder has filled the gap with their fear, which is always worse than reality.

## Sharing numbers with non-business stakeholders

For the partner or family member sharing risk, run a quarterly 30-minute numbers conversation with four fixed items: cash position and runway; the quarter's revenue versus plan; the single biggest upcoming risk; the next quarter's one main goal. Fixed format, fixed calendar, honest numbers including the uncomfortable ones. The alternative — sharing only good quarters — converts a partner into an accidental adversary at the worst possible moment.

Rules for the conversation: lead with the headline; never present a number without its trend (this quarter versus last); end with decisions, not just data; write the agreed decisions down and open the next meeting by reading them back.

## Delivering bad news

Bad news ages badly. The professional sequence:

1. **Tell them early, with the plan attached.** "Project slips two weeks; here's why; here's the new date; here's what changes." An early miss with a plan is management; a late surprise with an excuse is damage.
2. **Separate the news from the negotiation.** State the situation, then pause for their reaction before proposing remedies.
3. **Own it without over-apologizing.** One clear acknowledgment, then the fix. Endless apology reads as chaos, not contrition.
4. **Re-anchor with the next commitment.** What happens next, by when, and how they will know.

What to never do: bury the miss in an update full of positives, announce structural problems on a Friday afternoon, or let the client learn it from an invoice.

## A short crisis-communication playbook

When something has already gone visibly wrong — a breach, a public failure, a payment dispute:

- One spokesperson (in a solo business, that is you, prepared, not improvising).
- Facts known, facts unknown, stated separately.
- The remedy and its date, stated once and then honored.
- No speculation, no blaming the client or a vendor, no over-promising the fix.
- A written record of what was said to whom, because disputes remember differently than you do.

## Failure modes

- **The silence spiral.** Delaying an update until there is good news; the update never comes — prevented by the fixed cadence and format.
- **The surprise invoice.** Stakeholders discovering changes through documents — prevented by announcing changes before they are felt.
- **The humble-mumble ask.** Requests so hedged they are undetectable as requests — prevented by the one-ask, one-date rule.
- **Optimism whiplash.** Alternating over-promising and backpedaling, which destroys credibility faster than any single miss — prevented by ranges with reasons and a written record.
- **Financial secrecy at home.** Hiding runway trouble from a partner who shares the downside — prevented by the quarterly numbers conversation, which makes honesty cheap and routine.

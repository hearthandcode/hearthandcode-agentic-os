# Financial Planning Basics

Financial planning converts a business model into numbers you can act on and be corrected by. It is not accounting and it is not a pitch deck: it is a working sketch good enough to make hiring, pricing, and spending decisions, and honest enough to fail visibly when reality disagrees. This document covers cost structure, pricing, break-even, runway math, and how much precision a small business actually needs.

## The right level of precision

A one-person plan needs quarterly granularity and order-of-magnitude confidence, not decimal places. The test of a good financial sketch is not "is it right" — it will be wrong — but "does being wrong show up early and say something." Build so that each month's actuals can be dropped next to the plan, and let the variance tell you which assumption to fix.

## Cost structure

Separate every cost along two axes: fixed versus variable, and must-have versus discretionary.

- **Fixed:** rent, software subscriptions, insurance, loan payments. Paid whether or not revenue arrives.
- **Variable:** contractors, materials, transaction fees, travel per engagement. Roughly proportional to sales.
- **Discretionary:** upgrades, courses, conference travel, equipment above the baseline. Deferrable without breaking the business.

For each line, record: amount, frequency, and whether you can cancel it within thirty days. A lean solo business typically carries 10–20 fixed lines; if yours carries forty, that itself is a finding.

Sample fixed-cost lines for a solo studio (illustrative figures, annual, in dollars): accounting and tax prep 1,500; business insurance 900; core software subscriptions (design, files, finance) 2,400; professional development 1,200; hardware reserve 1,000. Total ≈ 7,000. Keep reserves like the hardware line — they are real costs, not optimism offsets.

## Pricing from cost, market, and value

Three anchors, used together rather than picked among:

- **Cost floor:** your true hourly cost. Compute: target annual income, plus taxes at an effective rate (30–40 percent for many freelancers), plus fixed costs, divided by billable hours. At 1,400 billable hours — not 2,000; realistic utilization for a service business is 55–70 percent — with $70,000 income, $25,000 taxes and costs, and $7,000 fixed: $102,000 / 1,400 ≈ $73/hour.
- **Market range:** what comparable work sells for. Collect real quotes and rate cards; anonymous surveys are weak evidence. If your cost floor exceeds the market range, the business model has a problem, not just the price.
- **Value anchor:** the client's economics — what the work is worth to them in revenue, savings, or risk. Justifies the top of the range; requires discovery to use.

Common structures: hourly (simple, caps income, punishes efficiency), per-project (client-friendly, demands scope discipline), per-day (good for defined engagements), retainer (recurring, must be scoped to be sustainable), value-based (highest ceiling, highest sales skill). Choose per service line; the plan should name which anchor set each line's price came from.

## Break-even analysis

Break-even answers: how much revenue covers all costs, with nothing left over?

- **Contribution margin:** revenue minus variable costs, per unit or as a percentage. For a studio with light variable costs, it is typically 85–95 percent.
- **Break-even revenue:** fixed costs ÷ contribution margin. With $7,000 annual fixed costs at a 90 percent margin: about $7,800 per year — trivially low. This is the small-business truth: break-even is nearly always about *founder income*, not overhead.

Therefore compute the founder-level break-even: required income + taxes + fixed costs, all divided by contribution margin. The $102,000 example at a 90 percent margin means roughly $113,000 of billable revenue, or about $9,400 of revenue per month. Every projection should carry this number, because any month below it is borrowing against the future — and the plan should say so.

## Cash runway

Runway is not profit math; it is calendar math. Two distinctions matter more than any others:

- **Revenue is not cash.** A signed engagement paid 50 percent on signature and 50 percent on delivery (net-30) converts to cash at start and roughly two months after completion. Map each revenue line to its cash-timing rules.
- **Runway formula:** cash on hand ÷ average monthly cash burn (all outflows including founder draws, minus cash actually received). Recompute monthly; runway above roughly six months lets you survive a bad quarter; below three, cut discretionary spend immediately and shorten payment terms.

Small-business cash traps: deposits too small or absent; net-60 payment terms accepted without protest; the tax bill treated as a surprise; annual software renewals landing in a thin month; personal draws rising with revenue before profit exists. Each has a boring preventive habit: deposits of 30–50 percent, payment terms stated in the agreement, a separate tax account funded at each invoice, a reserve calendar, and a fixed draw with a review date.

## Runway arithmetic, worked

Scenario: $18,000 personal cash reserve, plan of $9,400 monthly revenue starting to accrue at month three, personal burn $4,000/month, business fixed costs $583/month (the $7,000 annual above), first client cash arriving month two ($3,500 deposit).

- Months 1–2 burn: 2 × $4,583 = $9,166 minus the month-two deposit $3,500 = $5,666. Cash after month two: $12,334.
- From month three, target cash inflow ≈ $7,500/month (early months underplan revenue): net +$2,900/month. Cash climbs from the month-two trough.
- Trough runway check: $12,334 ÷ $4,583 ≈ 2.7 months at zero new revenue — thin. This says the plan needs a faster path to a first deposit, a lower burn in months one and two, or a part-time bridge income, *before* launch rather than after the first panic.

That last sentence is the entire point of runway math: it converts anxiety into one of three concrete decisions, each of which changes the plan.

## Building the 12-month sketch

1. List revenue by service line, with price × expected volume per quarter. Separate project lines from recurring lines.
2. List costs: fixed by month; variable as a percentage or per-unit rule tied to revenue lines.
3. Compute contribution margin per line, then blended.
4. Lay out the 12 months: revenue by line → total revenue → variable costs → fixed costs → founder draw → taxes provision → net cash flow → running cash balance.
5. Mark the break-even line on the same table and the trough month of cash.
6. Add one downside case: revenue at 60 percent of plan. If the trough breaks your minimum-cash rule, adjust the plan, not the optimism.

## Reading the numbers: three diagnostic questions

- **Is the business solvent at this trajectory?** Runway and trough month answer this.
- **Is it worth the founder's time?** Founder income per hour, at the planned utilization, answers this. A plan that yields $30/hour is a hobby with overhead.
- **Does growth help?** If contribution margin is thin or delivery is founder-time-bound, more sales can worsen cash (earlier costs, later cash). Growth helps only when contribution survives its own costs.

## Common failure modes

- **Revenue extrapolation.** A good month annualized — prevented by quarterly pricing × volume lines.
- **The missing founder salary.** "Profit" that is unpaid wages — prevented by the cost-floor calculation.
- **Cash blindness.** Profitable on paper, broke in month four — prevented by the cash-timing map and trough analysis.
- **Precision theater.** A 24-month model with invented decimals — prevented by the quarterly sketch and monthly variance review.
- **Tax surprise.** Prevented by the separate tax account funded per invoice; this is a planning failure, not an accounting one.

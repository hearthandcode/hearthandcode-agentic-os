# Narrative Through Environment

*Methodology.*

## Overview

Environmental storytelling tells the player a story through a space, objects, and composition — without dialogue, text, or cutscenes. The player pieces together what happened, what is happening, and what is about to happen by reading the environment. This reference covers the core techniques.

## Principles of Environmental Storytelling

1. **Show, don't tell — mechanically.** The state of the world reflects its history. A broken barricade says something broke through it. A pile of ash says someone was burned. A sequence of identical skeletons suggests an assembly-line death.
2. **Let the player discover.** The story is found, not broadcast. Marking items with loud text is signposting, not storytelling. A trail of blood diminishing towards and ending in a large claw appears — the player follows, then sees the claw. That is storytelling and action.
3. **Contradiction is good.** The environment should indicate safety when it is not, and offer clues to why the one corpse did not follow the pattern of the other dead guards. Contradiction makes the player lean in to resolve it.
4. **Every corpse tells a story.** The position of a dead body, its relation to objects around it, and how it came to be there conveys more was in a paragraph. Always ask: did this person run toward something? away from it? Are they holding something? Did they die with a key or a weapon or an empty clip?

## The bundled approach

| A player enters a room and sees | The player suspicion |
|---|---|
| A door that is locked from the inside | They locked themselves in. |
| A door surrounded by furniture | They were sealing something out. |
| A door with cracks running and smell | Something big hit the door. |
| A door fit open, and the nearby light is clipped | Something passed through. |

## Set up the state of the space

- **Disposition:** The room is warm (gut feeling: safe) vs cold (unsafe), vs darkness.
- **Disorder:** Chaos (the fight happened) vs order (the person lives, or the room is a workspace).
- **Treasure:** The objects of the dead: maybe the player picks up something.
- **Conditions of the bodies:** Blunt trauma, piercing, fire, poison — each tells a different story.

## Use the environment to foreshadow

The player should see the outcome of prior events before they recognize what they imply.

**Foreshadowing:**

- In the first level, player sees a guard shooting a civilian who they did not realise they were a civilian — later you see that the guard was a robot.
- The colour of the light on the wall suggests the boss's energy type before the boss room.
- The bodies of enemies that appear earlier in one area appear again in a later chapter, partially eaten. The sign that something bigger is coming.

## Environmental pacing

- **Entrance:** The first 3-5 seconds of any region should offer an establishing shot (a vista, a focus on a landmark, lighting that draws the eye).
- **Set Pieces:** Every major combat zone or key encounter should have a distinct set piece — a unique, interactive, visually obvious arrangement that stands out from repeated level elements.
- **Exposition:** The environmental story rarely progresses at the same rate as gameplay. If the gameplay is intense, the environment might be a dim corridor. If the gameplay is slow (puzzle), the environment should be full of visual treasure.

## Classic techniques

| **Technique** | **Description** | **How to use** |
|---|---|---|
| **Diorama** | A freeze-frame of the story in the middle of an action | A soldier aiming endlessly, a patient in a chair when something took the patient. |
| **Shadow** | The player sees an enemy's shadow before the enemy body — a story about the enemy's location | The entry of a building projects a large guard pattern the player hasn't seen. |
| **Atmosphere + Colour** | The colour temperature tells about safety history | A blue-room cold ("danger, heat, life"), a warm orange room ("safe, but broken"), a red denouement ("everything at risk"). |
| **Spatial context** | The greater environment locations punch above their weight in one small because of the lesson they hold | The bridge was a civil engineering triumph, now dynamic. A confident in the sun, now it rains inside. |

## Integration with level mechanics

Environmental storytelling is not an overlay — it must align with level flow, pacing, and the player's objectives. If a diorama takes 10 seconds to parse and is followed by an immediate boss fight, the diorama is a texture, not a story beat.

**Recommended integration lane:**

- Use environmental story to indicate the next **phase of combat**. If the last room had broken barricades, the next combat is defensive for the player.
- Use environmental story as a **reward**. The view after the boss, the past as new discovery has coloured lighting.
- Use environmental story as a **warning**. A defensive position that was lost marks the boundary of a safe zone.

## Checklist for environmental storytelling

- [ ] Does the room, as the player first enters, convey one story beat?
- [ ] Is there a single piece of environmental detail that the player would notice off the beaten path?
- [ ] Does the space contain any contradiction that forces the player to make sense of it?
- [ ] Would the game lose something if the environment were filled with random assets? (If yes, start over.)
- [ ] Are there 2-4 items in the space that combine to tell a short story of 5 words or less?
- [ ] Does any story detail have a secret expectation set before it is revealed?
- [ ] Does the environment match the level's runtime frequency? (fast fight: fewer details. Slow exploration: many.)
- [ ] Are there signs of what happened before the player arrived?
- [ ] Are any signs of what awaits — but only if you decide to look?

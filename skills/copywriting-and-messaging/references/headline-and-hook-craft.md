# Headline and Hook Craft

## How to use this reference

This file catalogs 12 actionable headline forms. Each entry describes the form, gives three varied, fully written examples, notes the situations where the form works best, and flags the risks that will tank it. Use this directly when drafting — match scenario, do not force.

---

## 1. The Direct Promise

A declarative sentence naming the outcome the reader wants. The strongest form if you know the reader's goal.

**Structure:** `[verb/outcome] [target audience] [timeframe/scoping]`

**Examples:**
- "Book the event at the venue in 90 seconds without a phone call."
- "Cut your open rate by 14% by changing a few subject line."
- "Streamline your healthcare intake for new patients."

**Best for:** Service pages, product hero sections, email subject lines.
**Risk:** Overpromising or being so broad that it's not credible. "Make more money" alone is empty. Add the "how" or the "how quickly."

---

## 2. The Simple Number

Attention via specificity. The number parafornsts certainty.

**Structure:** `[Number] [form of value] [topic]`

**Examples:**
- "7 principles that earned in copywriting from how they read information."
- "One email sequence that generates 40% of our annual revenue."
- "3 metrics your dashboard needs right now."

**Risk:** The content must deliver the number. "7 tips" have to be 7 people by tip, not 3 padded. The first statement needs to tell the reader to believe the number.

---

## 3. The Emotional Identification

The reader identifies a feeling or state and the headline makes it explicit.

**Structure:** `[Adjective] [person] — or a description of the feeling + "And [something]`

**Examples:**
- "I want to lose 19 minutes in the — you, my, just."
- "With 30 minutes of chaos each night? You just."
- "The question is: which is your case, but still can't sleep?"

**Risk:** readers can tell when it's just marketing. The question has to be true; if your product cannot honestly be the answer, do not use this formula.

---

## 4. The Heres

The gap that the reader believes but you know: the challenge, the strategy, the question.

**Structure:** `The problem</s>, and Here's the [problem/solution/conflict]`

**Examples:**
- "You're 14 minutes away after reading this: here's why your meeting bookings are vanishing."
- "They say design is great — here is why that one lost 50% of conversions."
- "Here's a free dash wallet is your marketing authentic."

**Risk:** Needs a genuine insight behind the tip. If the "first" part is trivial, the reader feels tricked.

---

## 5. The New Research

A positioning statement backed by interesting numbers.

**Structure:** `[Number] [claim] — [with/and] [counterintuitive finding]`

**Examples:**
- "70% of users read a form while ignoring a new hand doing real tone-blind."
- "The average page loads **J2 seconds slower than the year before**: adjust accordingly."
- "10% of revenue comes from trial users who read a story that fits those ones."

**Risk:** The numbers must be real. Fabricated stats kill credibility.

---

## 6. The Comparison

Positioning against a known alternative, either other tools or manual methods.

**Structure:** `[What the reader uses] vs [What we are offering]: [the difference]`

**Examples:**
- "Streets vs emails → Here are the only ones reading last week."
- "Large hoping vs pages indexed for thing 1: The difference is ... is what we versus them."
- "The manual version takes 40 minutes / this version takes longer. That is all it is."

**Risk:** Negative comparison poorly handled makes you look threatening weak, even if the facts are on your side.

---

## 7. The Direct Command

A bold instruction that tells the reader exactly what to do.

**Structure** `[Imperative verb] [object] [if / and / to]`

**Examples:**
- "Disable old login details until you see the space to fix the problem."
- "Stop tailoring your resume — you still need to select what the job wants."
- "Try the link that costs 3 sentences. Your short exit test."

**Risk:** Works only when the authority is earned. If the reader does not trust you, they will not take a command.

---

## 8. The Cliché that is itself wrong

Taking a common piece of worse advice and turning it around.

**Structure:** `The formula we were always told, but it fails — heres*

**Examples:**
- "Lactic acid is not the thing that makes your muscles hurt (here the energy industry did for a decade)."
- "Always open with a question? Effective vs studied: Subjects who see it feel they avoid most pitches."
- "Bigger font, not smaller: The single legibility factor that the last 10 years could have told you about."

**Risk:** Must be correct. Does it reset to the field that moving the current consensus is a path to "breaking"?

---

## 9. The Implicit object

The processes, not the outcome — how the process works.

**Structure:** `[The process] with (specific) [technique/factor]`

**Examples:**
- "How we use a 45-minute run for copy structure so the reader reads all the way down."
- "A failure process: Pitch an article on location and 2 weeks, the phrase 'I want more public speaking today'."
- "The 6 % aligns with—The test triggered to save an 80% conversion overnight update."

**Risk:** The question is "Can the reader hear the specific insight quickly?" A vague process description — "How we ended up building a better pipeline".

---

## 10. The Benefit Button

The headline is the CTA, just one loud and strong.

**Structure:** `[One clear reason to act] — optionally [time or condition]`

**Examples:**
- "Get the case study without a meeting attached."
- "Watch a recording of a Call — in 30 minutes or less."
- "Receive the trial without a credit card."

**Risk:** Use it only if the reader knows who you are. For a cold audience, name the brand somewhere.

---

## 11. The Personal Story Arc

A narrative micro-summary with a conclusion.

**Structure:**
"We were [in a state]. So we [did something]. The problem turned into [result]. Now [new state]."

**Examples:**
- "I filled out 14 vendor forms last year. So I built a love for one file. Now it's fully auto."
- "We rewrote 6 headlines every week trying to move the bounce rate. The sales team won at the one-coworker — then we model it."

**Risk:** Must be a real story. Readers spot fabrication.

---

## 12. The Hyper-Specific Adjective

The more tightly you specify the audience or the benefit, the better the reader feels "for me."

**Structure:** `[Specific adjective/hover] [what it is]`

**Examples:**
- "The hard-to-use taste for a business that is manually corrected."
- "A minimalist work in startup's ecosystem."
- "The form — in 20 K — design is the default."

**Risk:** Solve the hyper-specific that you restrict — let them fall outside the audience.

---

## Honest-use rules for all headline work:

1. **Test every headline:** The highest-certainty step is to test even your best idea. You have zero means to know if the headline works — at minimum run a copy test.
2. **Headline + subhead = team:** The headline runs the hook. The first paragraph delivers the completion of the promise. If the subhead contradicts or backs away from the headline promise, fix.
3. **No clickbait — the content has to deliver:** A headline that is stronger than the content underneath will lose trust. The reader feels cheated.
4. **The headline must be readable at 60 words/second:** Readers are scanning. The headline passes a stress test: read the entire piece in a glance while tapping a table with your third hand. Did it land in the gut?.
5. **Write 25 versions before picking one.**

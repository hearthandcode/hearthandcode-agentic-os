# Copywriting Principles

## Clarity over Cleverness

The reader must understand the message in one pass. Every extra cognitive load — a pun that needs decoding, a metaphor that stretches too far, a sentence that must be re-read — costs attention. Attention is a finite budget.

**How to prioritise clarity:**
- Write at the 8th-grade reading level for general audiences. Use Hemingway or the Flesch-Kincaid score as a diagnostic, not a target.
- After writing, ask: "Would my reader explain this back to me correctly?" If not, the message is not clear enough.
- A clever line that confuses 20% of readers is worse than a clear line that persuades 80%.
- Reserve wordplay for headlines the reader will see more than once (taglines, logos, repeatable slogans).

**When to break this rule:** For an expert audience that expects industry jargon, or for brand voice pieces where wordplay is the brand. But even then, make sure the meaning survives the joke.

## Benefits over Features

A feature is what the product does. A benefit is what the feature does for the reader. Readers do not buy features; they buy outcomes.

| Feature | Benefit |
|---------|---------|
| 256-bit encryption | "Your data stays yours — no one else reads it." |
| 24-hour customer support | "When your order breaks, fix it before your audience wakes up." |
| Machine-learning scheduling | "The calendar fills itself. You just show up." |
| 10GB cloud storage | "Every photo from every device, in one place." |

**The "So what?" test:** After every feature statement, ask "So what?" until the answer is a human outcome.

    "Our app uses end-to-end encryption." → So what? → "That means nobody except you can read your messages. Not even us."

**When features beat benefits:** In a technical evaluation, the buyer needs both. Specialist buyers need features first; everything else needs benefits first.

## One Reader

Write to one reader. A known, specific human with a name, a job title, a frustration, and a goal.

**How to find your one reader:**
- Demographics: age range, job, income bracket
- Psychographics: what does this person fear? What does this person want more than sleep?
- Moment: where are they when they read? (Mobile phone on a bus, or desk at work with a coffee at 2 p.m.?)
- Previous attempt: Why have they not solved this problem already? What failed?

**Execute the one-reader rule:**
- Use "you" and "your" as the dominant pronoun. Write as if one person is reading over your shoulder.
- Share references: "If you've ever felt X, you're not alone."
- Do not write for "everyone." If it reads like it could apply to anyone, it applies to no one.

## Show, Don't List

Lists of features do not convince. Vivid outcome descriptions do. The reader needs to see themselves using the product to trust that it will be worth the price.

**Bad:** "The app tracks sleep, exercise, food, and water."
**Good:** "Swipe open in the morning. Green when you sleep, blue when it's real."

**Technique: the benefit tableau**
Write a paragraph about a specific person using the product and getting a specific outcome. Use sensory details, consequences, change.

---

## Specificity

Vague claims erode trust. Specific claims give the reader something to believe. The difference between "our software is fast" and "minutes in to find the what you need" is the difference between hot hair and a claim that a reader can test.

**Replace:**
- "Fast" → "57ms response time"
- "Affordable" → "From $19/mo, no hidden fees"
- "Loved by users" → "8,200+ active teams as of Q2"

---

## One Sentence per Idea

A sentence should carry one idea. Complex ideas get broken into separate sentences. This is not about length — it is about density.

**Dense example:** "The end-to-end platform with automated billing, A/B testing, and real-time analytics lets you convert more visitors with less coding."
**Broken:** "The platform does end-to-end management. Billing, A/B testing, and real-time analytics run themselves. You write less code. Your visitors convert more."

---

## The Inverted Pyramid

Start with the conclusion. Then the supporting details. Then the background. This is the newsroom structure — it gives the reader the most important thing first, even if they read only the first paragraph.

**Structure:**
1. The headline says what it is.
2. The first paragraph says what it means or why it matters.
3. The second paragraph adds context and evidence.
4. The rest provides background, caveats, and details.

---

## Consistency of Terms

Use the same word for the same thing every time. Do not alternate "app," "platform," "tool," and "solution" for variety. Each change is a signal to the reader that they are reading about something different.

**Rule:** Pick one term per concept and stay with it. If you need to introduce a new term, define it explicitly and stay consistent.

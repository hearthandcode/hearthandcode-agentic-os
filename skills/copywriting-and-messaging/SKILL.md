# copywriting-and-messaging

## 01 — Purpose

Copywriting is the craft of writing to move someone to think, feel, or act
on a specific next step. It differs from content writing (which primarily
informs or educates) and technical writing (which documents with completeness
as the goal). Every piece of copy has one job: the reader should feel more
interested, more trusting, or readier to act after reading than before.

This skill covers the complete professional process from brief intake
through final delivery and measurement planning: understanding what the
reader needs at each decision stage, structuring the message so they absorb
it without effort, choosing powerful words, editing until only the strongest
remain, and preparing measurement so the writer can prove the copy works.

**Three foundational crafts.** Empathy: you must know how the reader feels
before they read. Clarity: you must put the right words in the right order so
they process without effort. Persuasion: you must connect the reader's problem
to the next step. Without empathy, copy is on-brand but disconnected. Without
clarity, the reader stops reading. Without persuasion, the reader finishes and
does nothing.

**What the skill provides exactly:**
- A 10-step workflow from brief alignment to measurement planning (Section
04), each step naming a reference file and ending with a checkpoint question.
- Eight reference files covering principles, voice and tone, headline patterns,
long-form vs short-form, conversion tactics, editing methodology, A/B testing
discipline, and case studies.
- One messaging brief template and one complete worked example.
- Checkpoint questions at each step. They are not optional — each catches a
common gap: missing audience detail, mismatched tone, unfocused CTA,
insufficient editing.

**What the skill does NOT cover:**
- Marketing strategy or brand messaging architecture. Those are upstream
decisions. Use marketing-strategy for positioning and channel selection.
- Long-form content marketing (blog posts, SEO articles) built to educate
over time. Those follow different structural rules.
- UI microcopy: button labels, error messages, confirmation dialogs require
a UX-specific approach not covered here.
- Language adaptation or translation. The skill writes in the language of
the brief.
- Creative fiction, narrative arcs, or world-building. Those belong to
story-and-narrative-design elsewhere in the repository.

**The one-reader discipline:**
Every piece is written for one specific person — named, modeled, and placed in
their actual daily situation. The reader is not a demographic. They are a
single human with one problem and one decision to make. The copy must speak to
the problem before the product speaks. If the writer does not know who that
person is, the audience is "everyone," and the copy reads to no one.

This skill's SKILL.md contains the full workflow and rules. Each section is named in the reference table below.

## 02 — When to Use / When Not to Use

### Use this skill when:

1. Writing a landing page or product page whose only job is conversion.
   The page has one audience and one goal. The headline has two seconds to
   capture the reader. The page then guides from benefit to proof to CTA.
   - references/copywriting-principles.md for the one-reader rule and the
     inverted pyramid structure.
   - references/conversion-copywriting.md for CTA placement and objection
     handling patterns.

2. Crafting email copy that must first earn an open and then earn a click.
   The subject line and preview text are the only gate to the reader's
   attention. The body must not waste that opening.
   - references/headline-and-hook-craft.md for 12 subject line patterns.
   - references/voice-and-tone.md for warmth and formality modulation.

3. Reviving copy that is clearly underperforming its goals.
   Concrete signals: bounce over 65%, conversion below 2%, user comments
   saying "too wordy" or "what is this even about?"
   - references/editing-and-tightening.md for the five-pass diagnosis.
   - references/copy-case-studies.md for a before-and-after with real
   metrics.

4. Preparing copy for an A/B test.
   The test requires a clean control and a single-variable variant. The
   hypothesis must be specific enough to be clearly confirmed or discarded.
   - references/a-b-testing-copy.md covers hypothesis construction, sample
   size estimation, and duration.

5. Drafting a messaging brief for a new campaign or feature launch.
   - templates/messaging-brief-template.md captures audience, core message,
   primary objection, tone, and measurement on one page.

6. Writing a help article, step-by-step guide, or feature walkthrough.
   The same clarity and specificity rules apply, even when the goal is to
   inform rather than persuade.
   - references/copywriting-principles.md for the inverted pyramid structure.

7. Writing a headline or subject line for any medium or channel.
   Even a short social post needs a hook that pulls the reader into the body.
   - references/headline-and-hook-craft.md supplies forms for every channel:
   print, email, blog, social, landing page, and ad.

### Do NOT use this skill when:

1. You need marketing strategy before copy exists. Use marketing-strategy
   for positioning, audience modeling, and distribution.

2. You need a content calendar or social cadence plan. Use
   content-calendar-planning. This skill handles one piece of copy at a time.

3. You need fiction, narrative arcs, or creative world-building. Use
   story-and-narrative-design. The techniques here are for commercial copy.

4. You need a full brand voice guide or positioning document. This skill
   defines 3--5 voice attributes per piece, enough for one project. Use
   marketing-strategy for a full manual.

5. The copy is a small UI element. A button label ("Save", "Delete") or
   a single error message does not need the full workflow. Apply UX
   micro-copy guidelines separately.

## 03 — Inputs and Outputs

### Inputs

The skill expects one or more of the following from the practitioner:

- **A writing brief or project description.** The preferred format is the
  template at templates/messaging-brief-template.md. If no template exists,
  the skill reconstructs a complete brief from a sentence that names the
  product, the audience, and the tone. Every gap in the reconstruction
  produces a risk in the written output. Missing the audience name means
  the piece reads generically.

- **A draft copy that needs revision.** A landing page, email, or headline
  that is clearly underperforming its goal. The skill analyzes this copy
  section by section against the principles in references/copywriting-principles.md
  and produces a structured diagnosis: which principle was violated in which
  section, what the weaker text is, and what replacement approach to use.

- **An A/B test hypothesis or idea.** The user has a change candidate and
  a desired outcome. The skill validates the test design against
  references/a-b-testing-copy.md, checking whether the change is large enough
  to be detectable, whether the sample size is feasible given traffic, and
  whether technical bias could produce a false result.

- **A tone and audience description alone.** From an audience description and
  a tone indicator (warm vs corporate, short vs detailed, formal vs casual),
  the skill generates a complete piece step by step through the workflow.
  The output includes a voice guide as a side product.

- **A voice or brand style document.** The skill merges the provided document
  with the framework in references/voice-and-tone.md and produces a custom
  voice and tone guide for this project, with do and do not examples.

### Outputs

The skill delivers one or more of the following, depending on the input:

1. Edited copy in the format requested (draft or final).
2. A section-by-section diagnosis for any revision intake: original text,
   violated principle by name, referenced file path, suggested rewrite.
   Example: "Section 2: feature list. Violates benefits-over-features from
   references/copywriting-principles.md. Rewrite each line as 'X, which means Y.'"
3. A completed messaging brief delivered in the same shape as the template
   templates/messaging-brief-template.md.
4. An A/B test plan: control header, variant header, hypothesis line, sample
   size estimate, duration estimate, significance threshold.
5. A custom voice and tone guide for this project with defined attributes
   and do/don't examples per channel (web, email, social).

## 04 — Workflow

### Step 1 — Brief and alignment

Reference: templates/messaging-brief-template.md

Open the template and fill every field. Start with the audience: name, role,
trigger. Then the situation or problem. Then the offering, its primary benefit,
and the one objection causing hesitation.

If the user supplies a prose brief, map each element to template fields.
Every gap is a risk: missing audience means writing for everyone and attracting
no one. Missing benefit means the copy has no anchor. Missing objection means
the copy avoids the doubt the reader is forming.

Write the one-sentence core: "For [audience] who [problem], our [offering]
helps you [benefit]. Unlike [objection], it [risk reversal]." Every following
decision refers to this sentence.

Checkpoint: "Can I name the audience, the problem, the main benefit, and the
objection we address in one sentence?"

### Step 2 — Define voice and tone

Reference: references/voice-and-tone.md

Pick 3--5 voice attributes. For each, produce a line that shows the attribute
applied and a line that shows its opposite. Keep these on the same page as
you write.

Set three sliders relative to baseline:
- Warmth from -3 (cold, automatic) to +3 (warm, neighborly)
- Formality from -3 (casual) to +3 (formal)
- Energy from -3 (low energy) to +3 (high energy)

A B2B proposal for a VP: -1 warmth, +2 formality, +1 energy. An email to free
trial users: +2 warmth, -2 formality, +3 energy.

Checkpoint: "Does the tone set by these values match the audience and channel?"

### Step 3 — Write the headline or subject line

Reference: references/headline-and-hook-craft.md

Select one or two forms from the 12-form catalog. Base the selection on the
reader's decision stage: comparing (Direct Promise), problem unknown (Problem),
ready to buy (Command).

Generate 12--25 candidates across the selected forms without filtering. Read
each aloud. Cut any that cause pause. Choose the top three.

Test the three headlines with five target readers. Ask: "Based on this
headline, what do you expect the rest of the page to be about?" If the
answers do not match the page content, the headline fails.

Checkpoint: "If the reader sees only the headline, will they know the page
is relevant and worth their time?"

### Step 4 — Write the hero (first 50 words)

Reference: references/copywriting-principles.md (inverted pyramid, specificity)

The hero delivers the headline promise with a concrete example. "Your week's
meals in 30 seconds" becomes "Share preferences, allergies, and meal count.
We deliver the menu and list sorted by aisle in one click."

Use the inverted pyramid: conclusion first, detail second. Eliminate
company-centered phrases: "Welcome to", "We are excited".

Checkpoint: "The reader who absorbs only the first 50 words can describe
exactly what this page offers."

### Step 5 — Write the body: benefits, objections, structure

References:
- references/copywriting-principles.md for benefits.
- references/long-form-vs-short-form.md for format selection.
- references/conversion-copywriting.md for objection handling.

Write the body answering the five questions the reader brings without naming
them:
1. "What do I get?" -- the direct benefit.
2. "Is this for someone at my stage?" -- audience reinforcement.
3. "What makes that claim credible?" -- specific evidence.
4. "What about my specific concern?" -- objection handling.
5. "What do I do now?" -- the action direction.

For each feature, apply the "so what" test. Add "which means..." and complete
the benefit. An AI selects meals. Which means you never decide what's
cook. That is the benefit.

Paragraphs: maximum 3--4 lines each. Over 6 lines and the scanning reader
loses the thread.

Write the objection from Step 1 directly in the reader's words. Then head
it off with a specific risk reversal. "If you are concerned about setting
time up: three fields, 60 seconds."

Checkpoint: "The reader at the end of the body can name the benefit, the next
step, and the primary reason to trust."

### Step 6 — Write social proof

References:
- references/conversion-copywriting.md (testimonial structure)
- references/copy-case-studies.md (examples)

Social proof is a before/after story line, not a quote. Format:

- The situation: "Monday at 6, refrigerator empty, nothing planned."
- The product intervention: "Set up the profile in 4 minutes."
- The outcome: "Tuesday shopping was 15 minutes less than normal."

Avoid use of "trusted by thousands" without specific story.

If no real testimonials exist, use references/copy-case-studies.md for a
parallel with numbers.

Checkpoint: "The proof reads as one specific person with a real situation,
a real interaction, and a real outcome."

### Step 7 — Write CTA and risk reversal

Reference: references/conversion-copywriting.md

The CTA starts with a verb and names the benefit. "Plan your week",
"Build your list". Not "Learn More" which invites the reader to step back.

Place risk reversal next to the CTA:
- Free trial: "No card required. Cancel before the trial ends."
- Guarantee: "If it does not save time, you do not pay."
- No commitment: "Skip at any time. The data stays with you."

One CTA per viewport. On a longer page: repeating the CTA after hero,
after the main benefit block, and at the bottom.

Checkpoint: "If all text disappeared from the page except the CTA and its
adjacent risk reversal, would the reader know the next step and if it
is safe?"

### Step 8 — Tighten (20% cut)

Reference: references/editing-and-tightening.md

Five passes through the draft:

1. Structure: remove any section not driving toward the CTA.
2. Sentence: compress filler. "In order to" becomes "to".
   "Utilizing" becomes "using".
3. Weasel word inventory: search for "believe", "think", "likely",
   "probably", "seems", "could", "may". Replace each with a specific verb.
4. Rhythm: read the entire block aloud. Vary sentence length.
5. Count: compare word counts. Must trim 20%. If not, content padding.

Checkpoint: "The piece reads faster but says the same thing, 20% shorter."

### Step 9 — Quality control

Reference: references/voice-and-tone.md

Read aloud from start to finish. Pause where the tongue stops. Correct.
Check tone against the values in Step 2. "Friendly and direct" must read
friendly and direct.

Checkpoint: "If I removed the brand, could I identify the piece by voice?"

### Step 10 — Prepare measurements

Reference: references/a-b-testing-copy.md

Before launch, write:
- Baseline: current metric and value.
- Prediction: what the variant will achieve and why.
- Sample size: from the formula in the reference.
- Confidence threshold.

If the piece cannot produce a testable prediction, it is not
testable.

Checkpoint: "If the copy returns baseline, will I know which part of the
page did not cause the improvement?"

## 05 — Rules and Quality Bar

1. Write for one named reader. Not a persona, not a segment. The copy uses
   "you" and refers to one specific role and situation. Rule from
   references/copywriting-principles.md.

2. Length measures effort, not value. A 200-word page that is edited to
   the rules will always outperform a 1,000-word draft. The 20% cut
   applies at every scale. Rule from references/editing-and-tightening.md.

3. Every CTA starts with a verb and names the benefit. "Start your trial,"
   "Download the checklist," "Build your plan." Not "Submit" or "Learn More."
   Rule from references/conversion-copywriting.md.

4. Each feature gets a "so what" translation. Without a benefit attached,
   the feature is just product specification. Apply the pattern "Feature X,
   which means the reader can Y."

5. Headline and sub headline agree on one claim. One message per page.
   If the headline addresses time and the sub addresses price, the reader
   gets two conflicting messages. Rule from references/headline-and-hook-craft.md.

6. Every claim requires a specific evidence source. "87% faster" must carry
   the survey size, population, and baseline reference. No claim without
   evidence. Rule from references/copywriting-principles.md.

7. No weasel words in the copy. "May help", "try", "probably", "we believe",
   "seems" each lowers reader trust. Replace with active voice.
   Rule from references/editing-and-tightening.md.

8. One consistent tone per piece. The reader builds the speaker's voice from
   the first sentence. A tone shift in the middle disrupts trust.
   Rule from references/voice-and-tone.md.

9. Paragraphs of 3 lines or 4 lines. Over 6 lines and the scanning reader
   stops reading instead of scanning.

10. Test the final headline with five audience members. If any of the five
    misidentify the page topic from the headline, the headline fails.
    Rule from references/headline-and-hook-craft.md.

11. Write in the brand's voice, not the writer's own. The writer's personal
    tone should not override the voice defined in Step 2.

12. Direct always beats clever. The reader's first job is meaning extraction,
    not reader appreciation of word play. Rule from
    references/copywriting-principles.md.

13. Editing passes have distinct focuses. One pass for structure, one for
    words, one for rhythm. A single pass that tries to do everything
    misses everything.

14. A/B test the headline before the body. The headline has the biggest
    potential for lift (20%+ on a good day). The body needs much larger
    traffic to show significance. Rule from references/a-b-testing-copy.md.

15. Risk reversal sits next to the CTA. Not at the bottom of the page or in
    an entire paragraph. The reader must see the safety and the step.

16. When uncertain, cut. A shorter copy requires less customer processing
    and fewer chances to lose the reader.

17. No AI self-labeling. No phrases like "as an AI language model" or similar.
    The output is human-facing copy. Any meta-reference to the medium
    makes the message less credible.

18. Every factual claim in final copy must have a source ready at the moment
    of publishing. Even if not cited on the actual page, the source must
    be available.

19. Write for scannability. In the first paragraph, put the conclusion.
    Subheads, bold, and short paragraphs support scanning readers who jump
    through the page.

20. The page has one CTA per viewport area. More than one the same screen
    hesitant. Repeat the same single CTA at the hero slot, the benefit
    block, and the footer.

## 06 — Worked Example: Plate Planner Landing Page

Scenario: A meal planning app called Plate Planner. Generates weekly
menus and shopping lists from user preferences and dietary restrictions.
Original page converts at 2.2% with 68% bounce.

### Section 1: Hero text

**Original:**
> Welcome to Plate Planner. The intelligent way to combine your meals. A powerful platform for planning your week.

**Diagnosis:** Greets rather than pitches (violation of clarity-first, Section 05 Rule 12). "Intelligent way to connect" is meaningless — does not say what the product does. "Powerful platform" is a product-centered claim.

**Rewrite:**
> Your meals for the week, planned in 30 seconds.
> Tell us your preferences, dietary restrictions, and meal count. We deliver a menu and shopping list, sorted by aisle.

**Why:** Writes the outcome and time. Specific numbers. Removes company words. Starts with "you" instead of "we."

### Section 2: Problem statement

**Original:**
> Tired of meal planning? Our solution works for you.

**Diagnosis:** "Tired" is vague. "Works for you" is weasel language (Rule 7, Section 5). Produces around generic problem description that any competitor could claim.

**Rewrite:**
> It's 5 PM. The fridge is open, and nothing matches. You need from cdd after you bought the ingredients last week have no recipes — or the recipe calls for ingredients you do not have.

**Benefit:** Names a specific time, situation, and emotion. The reader who has been there already imagines the scene.

### Section 3: How it works

**Original:**
> Easy to use. Simple. Beautiful.

**Diagnosis:** weasel words ("easy", "simple") — Rule 7. No methodology.

**Rewrite:**
> 1. Set dietary preferences.
> 2. The system matches from 15,000 recipes.
> 3. The list is sorted per produce, then then protein.

**Why:** How it works is three steps with a number, clear.

### Section 4: Features list

**Original:**
> - AI-powered planning
> - Sync across all devices
> - Auto-generated shopping list
> - Nutritional breakdown

**Diagnosis:** Features without benefits (Rule 4). The reader can see what each feature is but not why it benefits them.

**Rewrite:**
> - Meals are chosen for you based on your preferences.
> - Swap one dish and the week's schedule recalculates automatically.
> - The shopping list is sorted by area — produce first, then proteins.

**Why:** Each point now states a situation-oriented benefit instead of a spec.

### Section 5: Social proof

**Original:**
> Trusted by thousands of happy users.

** repeats.** Unspecified backing with no evidence (Rule 6).

**Rewrite:**
> "Monday evening panic, fridge open, nothing decided. Wednesday pick up: app sorted the list by aisle, in 15 minutes." — Lina.

**Testimonial structure:** specific situation, before/after, numbers.

### Section 6: CTA

**Original:**
> Start your subscription. Only $9.99/month. 30-day free trial.

**Diagnosis:** The price and trial are both included, but neither is the benefit of the reader. The CTA is passive ("Start").

**Rewrite:**
> Plan your first week now. No credit card trial.

**Why:** The CTA is action-oriented. Risk reversal next to the CTA.

### Summary table

| Section | Original | Rewrite | Problem |
|---------|----------|---------|---------|
| Hero | "Welcome to Plate Planner..." | Outcome + time | Starts with product, no outcome |
| Problem | "Tired of meal planning?" | specific time scene | Abstract |
| How it works | "Easy to use" | Three numbers, steps | Claims without language |
| Features | list hard | benefits per item | Features |
| Social proof | "Thousands trust us" | Named, before/after | No point |
| CTA | "Start trial" | "Plan your first week" | Generic CTA |

### Prioritized A/B test plan

Given the 2.2% baseline conversion and 68% bounce, the test should run
at least 1,000 visitors per variant (sample-size of 385 per group at
80% power and delta of 30% relative improvement).

**Version A (control):** existing page (2.2% conversion baseline).

**Variant B (new, full rewrite above).**

**Hypothesis:** Moving form a product-centered launch sequence and weasel
words to a scenario-based, benefit, reader-oriented rewrite will increase
at least 30% lift in landing page conversion from a baseline at 2.2%.

**Primary metric:** Click-through to signup.

**Duration:** until each variant has 1,000 visitors.

**Risk:** The HEro rewrite may increase the bounce rate before readers
reach the body. Mitigation measures: also measure scroll depth > 30%.

The full rewrite and test plan expand completely in
examples/worked-copy-pass.md.

## 07 — Failure Modes and Recovery

### 1. Bounce rate stays at 68% after rewrite

**Early signal:** Bounce rate does not move. Sessions sit at 2--3 seconds.
**Cause:** Hero did not catch the reader. Either the headline expectation does
not match, or the first 50 words are company-talk.
**Corrective:** Rewrite the first 50 words entirely. Test with five readers.
**Prevention:** Before writing the body, confirm the hero with five readers.

### 2. Copy reads corporate or generic

**Signal:** Feedback is warm glowing -- "Looks professional!" "Good work."
But no conversion.

**Cause:** Too many "we" statements, not enough "you" perspective.

**Corrective:** Replace every "we" with "you" and see what is left. Any
sentence that has no "you" references the brand.

**Prevention:** Start every paragraph with a "you" phrase (without over it)
to make the reader central.

### 3. No CTA clicks

**Signal:** The page content looks clean, readers scroll, but no clicks.

**Cause:** The CTA is wrong for the decision stage. Option Comparison CTA
("Try") fails if the reader is still at problem awareness.

**Corrective.: Change the CTA verb to match the stage. "Explore how it
works" for awareness, "Build your list" for comparison,

**Prevention:** In Step 1, check the reader's decision stage
against the CTA type.

### 4. Copy is too long for the medium

**Cause:** The character limit of the medium was not set
as a constraint in the brief.

**Corrective:** 40% cut directly instead of the standard 20%.

**Prevention:** Write the limit as the
first line of Step 1: on brief template, medium constraint.

### 5. Testimonials feel right but generic

**Signal.** The testimonial could be for any product in the category.

**Corrective:** Replace the generic statement with a before/after line.

**Prevention:** In the testimonial template, require the source to
supply number from the before state, the product, and the outcome.

### 6. Sentence nesting

**Signal:** The reader re-reads a sentence

**Cause:** more than 22 words, more than one dependent clause.

**Corrective:** Break the sentence into two or three.
Fragments work in copy, especially after the CTA.

**Check.** Before publishing, identify sentences longer than
22 words.

### 7. The A/B test returns flat (no lift)

**Signal:** variant performs at baseline +-1%.

**Cause:** The variant was too similar to the control, the
hypothesis was too vague, or the sample was over a social time.

**Corrective:** Check the power calculation. If below 80%,
the test was not designed to find a difference.

**Prevention:** Write the hypothesis in Step 10 so a flat result
means the copy did not change, not that the test was not sized.
